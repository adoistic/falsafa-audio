WORK: De Anima  (slug: tertullian-de-anima-d01878)

CHAPTER: 01-de-anima



[0] 1. In my engagement with Hermogenes I dealt only with the question of the soul's origin, in so far as he presumed that it was constituted out of the underlying stuff of matter rather than out of the breath of God; now, turning to the remaining questions, I shall plainly be found contending at great length with the philosophers. The state of the soul was already skirmished over in the prison of Socrates. I do not now know whether his masters chose a fitting moment for it, though the place makes no difference. For what clear knowledge could the soul of Socrates have at that time, when the sacred ship had already returned, the hemlock of his condemnation had already been drained, and death was already present — a soul certainly thrown into some agitation, if according to nature, or driven beside itself, if not according to nature? For however calm and tranquil it was, which neither the weeping of his soon-to-be widow nor the sight of his soon-to-be orphaned children had been able to bend by the law of natural affection, yet in this very thing it was moved — that it should not be moved; and its very steadfastness was shaken in resisting the shock of unsteadiness. And what else would any man unjustly condemned be aware of, except the solace of his wrong? — still less a philosopher, that creature of glory, for whom a wrong is not to be consoled but rather to be defied. So it was that, after the sentence, when his wife met him and cried out in womanly fashion, ‘You are unjustly condemned, Socrates!’, he had already answered, even as a kind of congratulation, ‘But would you have had it just?’ It is no wonder, then, if even in prison, eager to break the snared hands of Anytus and Meletus, he asserts the immortality of the soul in the very face of death by a presumption made necessary to frustrate the wrong done to him. So entirely had all that wisdom of Socrates come at that moment out of a deliberately contrived equanimity, not out of any confidence in a truth discovered. For to whom is truth discovered without God? To whom is God known without Christ? To whom is Christ explored without the Holy Spirit? To whom is the Holy Spirit imparted without the sacrament of faith? Indeed, Socrates was the more easily driven by a different spirit, since they say a familiar spirit clung to him from boyhood — a thoroughly evil tutor in truth, even though among the poets and philosophers demons are ranked after the gods and along with the gods. For the proofs of Christian power had not yet come forth, which alone exposes that most pernicious force, never at any time good, but rather the artificer of all error and the seducer from all truth. But if the wisest Socrates was so accounted even by the vote of the Pythian demon — who was of course doing a service for his own confederate — how much worthier and more steadfast is the assertion of Christian wisdom, before whose breath the whole power of demons gives way! This wisdom from the school of heaven is freer to deny the gods of the age — it would not play the hypocrite by ordering a cock to be paid to Aesculapius; it brings in no new demons, but drives out the old; it corrupts no youth, but forms it in every good of modesty; and therefore it endures the unjust sentence not of one city but of the whole world, for the name of truth — a sentence the more hateful as it is the more full, so that it drinks down death not from a cup with an air of pleasantness, but drains it from the gibbet and the stake through every device of cruelty — meanwhile, in this darker prison of the age, among its own Cebeses and its own Phaedos, if anything is to be examined concerning the soul, it will refer it to the rules of God. Surely you can point to no better demonstrator of the soul than its Author. Learn from God what you hold from God, or learn it from no one else, if not from God. For who shall reveal what God has concealed? It must be inquired into from that source from which it is also safest not to know. It is better, through God, not to know, because He has not revealed it, than, through man, to know, because he has presumed it on his own.

[4130] 2. We shall, plainly, not deny that the philosophers have sometimes felt as we do. The very outcome is a testimony to the truth. Sometimes, even in a storm, when the tracks of sky and sea are confounded, some harbour is struck upon by a lucky error; sometimes, even in the dark, some entrance and exit are found by a blind good fortune. But many things are also suggested by nature, as if from the common stock of perception, with which God has deigned to endow the soul. Philosophy, having got hold of this, has puffed it up to the glory of its own art, out of that zeal for eloquence — no wonder if I put it so — which is trained to build up and pull down anything whatever, and which persuades more by speaking than by teaching. It imposes shapes upon things; now it levels them off, now it strips them away; it pre-judges what is uncertain from what is certain, appeals to examples as though all things were comparable, prescribes everything, even where things alike differ in their properties, reserves nothing to the divine liberty, and makes its own opinions into the laws of nature. I would bear with this, if philosophy itself were natural, so that it might be proved to be a partner of nature by sharing in her constitution. It has, indeed, seemed to itself to have drawn even from the sacred writings, as they call them, because antiquity reckoned most of its authors to be even gods, let alone divine men — such as the Egyptian Mercury, to whom Plato especially attached himself; such as the Phrygian Silenus, whose huge ears Midas received from him when shepherds brought him in; such as Hermotimus, to whom the people of Clazomenae dedicated a temple when he was dead; such as Orpheus, such as Musaeus, such as Pherecydes the teacher of Pythagoras. But what if the philosophers have also stumbled into those writings which among us are condemned by their confession of being apocryphal, since we are certain that nothing is to be received which does not agree with the genuine prophetic equipment that was already brought forth in that same age — seeing that we may remember both the false prophets and, much earlier, the apostate spirits, who by the cunning of such talents have furnished the whole face of the age? Finally, even if it is credible that every searcher after wisdom went to the prophets themselves out of the business of curiosity, yet you will find more disagreement among the philosophers than agreement, since even in their agreement their disagreement is detected. For whatever true things harmonize with the prophets they either commend from some other source or pervert to some other end, with the greatest injury to the truth, which they cause to be either aided by falsehoods or made the patron of them. This, then, is what has brought us and the philosophers into conflict, in this matter especially: that sometimes they clothe common conclusions in their own argumentations, contrary in places to our rule, and sometimes they fortify their own conclusions with common argumentations, agreeable in places to their rule — so that truth is almost shut out of philosophy by its own sorceries against her. And therefore, since the adversary of truth presses us under both titles of that fellowship, we are bound both to free the common conclusions from the argumentations of the philosophers and to separate the common argumentations from their conclusions, recalling the questions to the writings of God — except, plainly, those which it will be lawful to take up for simple testimony without the snare of any prejudice, since even from rivals testimony is sometimes necessary, provided it does not profit the rivals. Nor am I ignorant how great a forest this subject is among the philosophers, in proportion to the very number of its commentators — how many varieties of opinion, how many wrestling-schools of theories, how many offshoots of questions, how many entanglements of solutions. But I have looked also into medicine, the sister of philosophy, as they call it, which lays claim to this business for itself as well, on the ground that the account of the soul belongs the more to that art which deals with the care of the body. Hence too it most opposes its sister, in that it claims to know the soul better by treating it, as it were, face to face, in its own dwelling-place. But let the ambition of each for pre-eminence look to it. Philosophy has had the liberty of genius, and medicine the necessity of craft, for extending the discussions about the soul; uncertain things are inquired into at length, and presumed things are disputed at greater length still. As great as is the difficulty of proving, so great is the labour of persuading; so that with good reason that obscure Heraclitus, observing the vaster fogs among the examiners of the soul, declared out of weariness with the questions that he had nowise found the boundaries of the soul, though he had entered upon every road. But for the Christian, few things are needful to the knowledge of this matter. For certainty always lies in few things, and he is not permitted to inquire further than what it is permitted to find — for the apostle forbids endless questions. Moreover, it is not permitted to find more than what is learned from God; and what is learned from God is the whole.

[9425] 3. And would that it had been needful for no heresies to exist, so that the approved might be revealed; then we should have no dealings at all with the philosophers concerning the soul either — those, so to speak, who are the patriarchs of the heretics. For even by the apostle, already then, philosophy was foreseen to be the shaker of truth; since at Athens, having experienced that talkative city when he had tasted all those hucksters of wisdom and eloquence, he conceived that forewarning edict. For so it is that the account of the soul too has been adulterated by the philosophized doctrines of men, who mix water with the wine. Some deny the soul to be immortal; others affirm it to be more than immortal; some dispute about its substance, some about its form, some about each of its arrangements; these draw its origin from one quarter, those draw its departure off to another — according as either the prestige of Plato, or the vigour of Zeno, or the steadiness of Aristotle, or the stupor of Epicurus, or the gloom of Heraclitus, or the frenzy of Empedocles has persuaded them. The divine doctrine has erred, I suppose, in rising out of Judaea rather than out of Greece. Christ too went astray in sending out fishermen rather than a sophist to preach. If, then, anything in this fashion darkens the bright and pure air of truth with the fumes of philosophy, these things must be cleared away by Christians, who both strike at the original argumentations — that is, the philosophical ones — and oppose to them the heavenly definitions — that is, the Lord's — so that both the things by which the heathen are captured through philosophy may be destroyed, and the things by which the faithful are shaken through heresy may be beaten back. One contest having already been decided against Hermogenes, as we said before — since we claim that the soul is from the breath of God, not from matter, being fortified there too by the inerasable rule of the divine determination, ‘And God breathed the breath of life into the face of man, and man became a living soul,’ assuredly from the breath of God — about this nothing more need be turned over. It has its own title and its own heretic. From this point I shall bring in the beginning of the rest.

[11676] 4. After the definition of its origin, the question of its condition presents itself. For it follows that, having professed the soul to be from the breath of God, we should assign it a beginning. This Plato excludes, wishing the soul to be unborn and uncreated. But we teach that it is both born and made, by the constitution of its beginning. Nor have we at once erred in saying both, since being born is one thing and being made is another — the former being proper to living creatures. But distinctions, while keeping their own places and times, have at times also their mutual exchanges. So it admits of being laid down that a thing made has come to be; since everything that in any way receives existence is generated. For the maker himself may also be called the parent of the thing made; and so Plato uses the word. Therefore, as concerns our faith in the soul as made and born, the philosopher's opinion has been driven off by the authority of prophecy as well.

[12647] 5. Let him summon up some Eubulus and Critolaus and Xenocrates, and in this place Aristotle the friend of Plato. Perhaps they will rather be built up to take away the corporeality of the soul — unless they have looked at others on the contrary side, and indeed more of them, who claim a body for the soul. And I do not mean only those who fashion it out of manifest bodily things — as Hipparchus and Heraclitus out of fire, as Hippo and Thales out of water, as Empedocles and Critias out of blood, as Epicurus out of atoms (if atoms too compose corporealities by their conjunction), as Critolaus and his Peripatetics out of some fifth substance I know not what (if that too is a body, since it includes bodies) — but I cite even the Stoics, who, by proclaiming the soul to be breath, hold almost with us, since breath and spirit are near akin to each other; and yet they will easily persuade that the soul is a body. In short, Zeno, defining the soul as a breath sown together with the body, instructs us in this manner: ‘That,’ he says, ‘on whose withdrawal the living creature dies, is a body; but on the withdrawal of the sown breath the living creature dies; therefore the sown breath is a body; but the sown breath is the soul; therefore the soul is a body.’ Cleanthes too holds that the resemblance of parents in their children answers not only in the lineaments of the body but also in the marks of the soul — that is, in the mirror, namely, of character and of disposition and of the passions — and that the soul takes on likeness and unlikeness along with the body. So the body is liable to likeness or unlikeness. Likewise, that the affections of corporeal and incorporeal things have nothing in common with each other. Moreover, that the soul suffers together with the body, grieving when it is hurt by blows, wounds, and ulcers; and the body together with the soul, cleaving to it when it is afflicted with anxiety, with anguish, with love, through the loss of its partner's vigour — whose shame and fear it attests by blushing and pallor. Therefore the soul is a body, by its sharing in corporeal passions. But Chrysippus too lends him a hand, laying it down that corporeal things can in no way be deserted by incorporeal ones, since they are not even touched by them — whence Lucretius too says, ‘For nothing but body can touch or be touched’ — and that the body, being deserted by the soul, is affected by death. Therefore the soul is a body, since, were it not corporeal, it would not desert the body.

[15166] 6. The Platonists confound these things by subtlety rather than by truth. ‘Every body,’ they say, ‘must be either animate or inanimate. And if it is inanimate, it will be moved from without; but if animate, from within. Now the soul will not be moved from without, as being not inanimate, nor from within, as being itself rather that which moves the body. Therefore it does not appear to be a body, since it is not moved like the form of corporeal things from some quarter.’ To this we shall first wonder at the incongruity of the definition, which appeals to things that do not apply to the soul. For the soul cannot be called an animate or an inanimate body, since it is itself that which makes the body either animate, if it is present, or inanimate, if it is absent from it. And so that which makes cannot be the very thing that is made, so as to be called animate or inanimate. For the soul is named by the name of its own substance. But if it cannot be called an animate or inanimate body, since it is the soul, how shall it be appealed to under the form of animate and inanimate bodies? Next, if it is the property of a body to be moved from without by another, and we have shown above that the soul too is moved by another — when it prophesies, when it raves, certainly from without, since by another — then with good reason I shall recognize as a body, according to the terms of their own example, that which will be moved from without by another. Indeed, if to be moved by another belongs to a body, how much more to move another! Now the soul moves the body, and its efforts appear outwardly. For it is from the soul that the feet are driven to walking, the hands to touch, the eyes to seeing, and the tongue to speech — a single inner agency stirring the surface as with the motion of a seal. Whence comes this power to an incorporeal soul? Whence the power of an empty thing to drive solid masses? But how is it that bodily and intellectual senses appear divided in man? The qualities of corporeal things, they say — as of earth, as of fire — are reported by the corporeal senses, as by touch and by sight; but those of incorporeal things are apprehended by the intellectual senses, as kindness, as malice. And so it is established that the soul is incorporeal, whose qualities are grasped not by corporeal but by intellectual senses. Plainly so — unless I exclude this step of the definition. For behold, I show incorporeal things subjected to corporeal senses: sound to hearing, colour to sight, odour to smell; by whose example the soul too belongs to the body. Do not say that incorporeal things are reported through the corporeal senses because they belong to corporeal things; for if it is established that incorporeal things too are apprehended by corporeal things, why should not the soul too, which is corporeal, be reported by incorporeal things? So let the definition be excluded. Among their more notable argumentations will be this also, that every body, they judge, is nourished by corporeal things, but the soul, as incorporeal, by incorporeal things — namely, the pursuits of wisdom. But this step too will not stand; for even Soranus, a most accomplished authority on the methodical school of medicine, answers that the soul is nourished by corporeal things as well; in fact, that when it grows faint it is for the most part propped up with food. Why not? For when food is withdrawn, the soul slips away wholly from the body. So Soranus himself too, who treated of the soul most fully in four volumes, and tested it against all the opinions of the philosophers, claims a corporeal substance for the soul, even though he cheated it of immortality — for it is not for all to believe what belongs to Christians. As, then, Soranus himself shows by facts that the soul is nourished by corporeal things, so let the philosopher in turn exhibit it as fed on incorporeal ones. But no one ever poured honeyed water out of an eloquence of Plato into a soul in doubt at its departure, nor stuffed it with the crumbs of Aristotle's hair-splitting talk. And what will so many and so great souls of mountain-folk and barbarians do, who lack the nourishments of wisdom, and yet are strong in untaught prudence, and — without academies and Attic porticoes, and indeed the prison of Socrates — nonetheless live while fasting from philosophy? For the nourishments of studies do not profit the substance itself, but its training; since they make the soul not richer, but more adorned. But it is well that the Stoics affirm even the arts to be corporeal. So in this way too the soul is corporeal, if it is believed to be nourished even by the arts. But the immense intentness of philosophy is often wont not even to look out before its own feet. So Thales fell into a well. It is wont also to suspect that the corruption of its health is the cause of its not understanding its own conclusions. So Chrysippus turned to hellebore. Something of this sort, I suppose, befell him when he denied that two bodies could be in one, alienated from any view or consideration of pregnant women, who carry not single bodies daily, but two and three within the compass of one womb. There is found even in the civil law a certain Greek woman who brought forth five sons, mother of all at one birth, parent manifold of a single delivery, a numerous childbearer of a single womb, who, packed about with so many bodies — I had almost said a people — was herself the sixth body. The whole order of things will testify that bodies destined to proceed from bodies are already there whence they proceed. That which is from another must be second. But nothing is from another except while it is being begotten; and then they are two.

[20887] 7. As far as the philosophers are concerned, this is enough — since, as far as our own people are concerned, it is more than enough; for them the corporeality of the soul will shine out in the Gospel itself. The soul of a certain man is in pain among the dead, and is punished in flame, and is tormented in its tongue, and from the finger of a happier soul implores the solace of a drop of dew. Do you think that departure of the rejoicing poor man and the grieving rich man a mere image? And what is the name of Lazarus doing there, if the matter is not real? But even if it is to be believed an image, it will be a testimony to the truth. For if the soul had not a body, the image of the soul would not admit the image of a body, nor would Scripture lie about corporeal members if they were not there. And what is it that is carried off to the lower regions after the divorce from the body, that is detained there, that is reserved unto the day of judgment, to which Christ too descended by dying? To the souls of the patriarchs, I think. But to what end, if the soul is nothing beneath the earth? For it is nothing, if it is not a body. Incorporeality is free from every kind of confinement, exempt both from penalty and from comfort. For that by which one is punished or cherished — this will be a body. Of this I shall render account more fully and more opportunely. Therefore, if the soul plucks beforehand any torment or any solace in the prison or lodging of the lower regions, in the fire or in the bosom of Abraham, the corporeality of the soul will be proved. For incorporeality suffers nothing, having nothing through which it can suffer; or if it has, this will be a body. For in so far as everything corporeal is capable of suffering, in so far is that which is capable of suffering corporeal.

[22696] 8. It is, moreover, abrupt and absurd to take anything out of the reckoning of corporeal things merely because it is not equal to the other examples of corporeal things. And where then are the private distinctions of properties, by which the magnificence of the Author is signalized out of the very diversity of His works, so that they are as distinct as they are alike, as friendly as they are even rivals? Since the philosophers themselves agree that the universe is composed of contraries, according to the friendship and enmity of Empedocles. So then, even if corporeal things stand over against incorporeal ones, they too differ thus among themselves, in such a way that the difference enlarges their species without changing their genus — so that they remain corporeal: so many things to the glory of God, while they are various; so various, while diverse; so diverse, while to these belongs one set of qualities of perception, to those another; while to these belong some nourishments, to those others; while these are invisible, those visible; while these are heavy, those light. For they say that the soul too is to be reported incorporeal on this further ground, that when it has departed, the bodies of the dead become heavier, whereas they ought to be lighter, the weight of one body being taken away, if the soul were a body. ‘For what,’ says Soranus, ‘if they should deny the sea to be a body, because outside the sea a ship is made motionless and heavy?’ How much more powerful, then, is the body of the soul, which afterwards carries about with the lightest mobility a body of so great a weight! Moreover, even if the soul is invisible, it is so according to the condition of its own body, and according to the property of its substance, and according also to the nature of those to whom it has been allotted to be invisible. Owls do not know the sun with their eyes; eagles so endure it that they judge the nobility of their offspring by the boldness of their pupils — otherwise they will not rear, as degenerate, the one which the ray of the sun has turned aside. There is, then, that which is invisible to one and not to another, which is not therefore incorporeal because the power does not avail equally. For the sun is a body, being fire; but that the eagle should confess it and the owl deny it does not prejudice the eagle. Just so the body of the soul is invisible to the flesh, but visible, it may be, to the spirit. So John, become in the Spirit of God, beheld the souls of the martyrs.

[25211] 9. When we assert that the soul is a body of its own quality and its own kind, this condition of property will already pre-judge concerning the other accidents of corporeality — that these too belong to it, which we have shown to be a body, but themselves of their own kind according to the property of the body; or even if they are not present, this is a matter of property — that there are not present to the body of the soul those things which are present to the rest of the bodies. And yet we shall not inconsistently profess that all those things which are in every way due to corporeality are present to the soul also: as habit, as limit, and that threefold dimension — I mean length, and breadth, and height — by which the philosophers measure bodies. What now, that we even give the soul a shape? Plato is unwilling, as if the immortality of the soul were thereby endangered. For he affirms that everything shaped is composite and built up, and that everything composite and built up is dissoluble; but that the soul is immortal, therefore indissoluble, in so far as it is immortal, and unshaped, in so far as indissoluble — otherwise composite and built up, if shaped. Yet he shapes it after another manner, with intellectual forms, beautiful through justice and the disciplines of philosophy, but misshapen through the contrary practices. But we inscribe corporeal lines upon it too, not only from confidence in its corporeality by reasoning, but also from the constancy of grace by revelation. For since we acknowledge spiritual gifts, after John too we have deserved to attain to prophecy. There is among us at this present day a sister who has been allotted the gifts of revelations, which she experiences in the church amid the Lord's solemnities through ecstasy in the Spirit; she converses with angels, sometimes even with the Lord, and sees and hears mysteries, and reads the hearts of certain persons, and supplies medicines to those who desire them. And so, as the Scriptures are read, or the psalms sung, or addresses delivered, or petitions offered, the materials for her visions are thus supplied. We had happened to be discussing something or other about the soul, when that sister was in the Spirit. After the solemnities were over and the people dismissed — it being her custom to report to us what she has seen (for these things are most diligently sorted, that they may even be proved) — ‘Among other things,’ she said, ‘there was shown to me a soul corporeally, and a spirit appeared, but not of an empty and void quality, rather one that even promised to be held in the hand, tender and lucid and of an airy colour, and in form altogether human.’ This was the vision, and God is witness, and the apostle a sufficient sponsor of the gifts that were to be in the church. Will you, then, not believe, even if the very thing has persuaded you in its particulars? For if the soul is a body, it is without doubt among those things which we have above professed. Accordingly the property of colour too cleaves to every body. What other colour, then, will you reckon for the soul than the airy and lucid? — not that air is its very substance (although this seemed so to Aenesidemus and Anaximenes, and, I think, according to some, to Heraclitus too), nor that it is light (although this pleased Heraclides of Pontus). For neither is the substance of thunderstones fiery merely because they flash with a reddish glow, nor of beryls watery merely because they shimmer with a filtered brightness. For how many things does colour associate which nature disjoins? But since everything thin and translucent is the rival of air, this the soul will be, in so far as it is a breath and a transmitted spirit — since, by the very subtlety of its fineness, it is endangered in respect of the faith of its corporeality. So conceive of its shape now from your own sense, that no other is to be assigned to the human soul than the human, and indeed that of the very body which each one carried about. Let the contemplation of its first origin meanwhile bring us to understand this. For consider: when God had breathed into the face of man the breath of life, and man had become a living soul, that breath was certainly at once transmitted through the face into the interior parts and diffused through all the spaces of the body, and at the same time, condensed by the divine inbreathing, was impressed upon every inner line which, condensed, it had filled, and, as it were, froze into the mould. Hence, then, both the corporeity of the soul was solidified out of this condensation, and its shape was formed by the impression. This will be the inner man, another the outer, the one being twofold; he too has eyes and ears of his own, by which Paul ought to have heard and seen the Lord; he has also the other limbs, which he uses both in thoughts and exercises in dreams. So the rich man among the dead has a tongue, and the poor man a finger, and there is the bosom of Abraham. By these lines too the souls of the martyrs under the altar are understood. For from the first origin the soul, conjoined and configured to the body in Adam, made itself the seed both of the whole substance and of this condition.

[30426] 10. It pertains to the standing of the faith to determine the soul to be simple, according to Plato, that is, uniform — at least in respect of the name of its substance. Let the arts and disciplines look to themselves, and the shapes too. For some wish there to be in it another natural substance, namely spirit, as though to live were one thing, which comes from the soul, and to breathe another, which is done by the spirit. For not all living creatures, they say, have both; for many only live, and do not also breathe, in that they have not the organs of breath, lungs and arteries. But what kind of thing is it, in the examination of the human soul, to regard the arguments of the gnat and the ant, when God the artificer has tempered the vital functions, according to the disposition of each kind, peculiar to all living creatures, so that no conjecture is to be caught from thence? For neither will man, if he is built with lungs and arteries, therefore breathe from one source and live from another; nor will the ant, if it lacks members of this sort, therefore be denied to breathe, as if merely living. But to whom has it lain so open in the works of God that he should presume these things to be lacking to anything? That Herophilus, physician or butcher, who cut up six hundred persons in order to scrutinize nature, who hated man in order to know him — I do not know whether he clearly explored all his inward parts, since death itself altered what had lived, and a death not simple, but one that strayed in the very arts of dissection. The philosophers have for certain reported that gnats and ants and moths lack lungs and arteries. Tell me, most curious inspector, do they have eyes for seeing? And yet they go where they will, and shun and seek the things they know by seeing; point out their eyes, mark out their pupils. But the moths also eat through things: show their jaws, bring forth their grinders. The gnats also sound their note, not even deaf as to the ears in the dark: show me alike the trumpet and the lance of that mouth. Whatever animal there be, even of a single point, must of necessity be nourished by something: exhibit the members for transmitting, cooking, and voiding the food. What then shall we say? If it is by these that life is sustained, these will assuredly be in all things that live, even if they are not seen, even if they are not apprehended because of their smallness. This you will believe the more, if you bethink yourself that God is as great an artificer in the minute as in the greatest. But if you think the genius of God incapable of fashioning such tiny little bodies, in this way too acknowledge His magnificence, that He has equipped little animals to live nonetheless without the necessary members — sight preserved even without eyes, and eating without teeth, and digestion without bowels; just as some things even move without feet by a flowing impulse, as snakes do, and by a rising effort, as worms do, and by a foaming crawl, as slugs do. So why do you not think that breathing too is done without the bellows of lungs and without the pipes of arteries — so that you should embrace it as a great argument that the spirit is added to the human soul because there are things which lack spirit, and that they lack spirit because they are not built with the organs of breathing? Do you reckon that anything lives without spirit, and do you not think that anything breathes without lungs? What is it, I ask you, to breathe? To drive breath out of oneself, I suppose. What is it not to live? Not to drive breath out of oneself, I suppose. For this I shall be bound to answer, if to breathe is not the same as to live. But it will belong to the dead not to drive breath; therefore it belongs to the living to drive breath. But it belongs also to the breathing to drive breath; therefore to breathe too belongs to the living. If both could have run their course without the soul, then breathing would not have belonged to the soul, but only living. But in fact to live is to breathe, and to breathe is to live. Therefore the whole of this — both to breathe and to live — belongs to that to which living also belongs, that is, to the soul. Finally, if you separate spirit and soul, separate their works too. Let both do something in distinct fashion, the soul apart, the spirit apart; let the soul live without the spirit, the spirit breathe without the soul; let the one leave the body, the other remain; let death and life come together. For if soul and spirit are two, they can be divided, so that by their division — the one departing, the other remaining — a concurrence of death and life may come about. But it will in no way come about; therefore they will not be two things, which cannot be divided, but could be divided if they had been two. Yet, you say, two things may be conjoined. But they will not be conjoined, if to live is one thing and to breathe another. Their works distinguish substances. And how much the firmer is it now that you should believe them one, since you do not give a distance such as would make the soul one thing and the spirit another, seeing that breathing belongs to the same thing as living does? For what if you wished day to be reckoned one thing, and light, which befalls the day, another, when day itself is light? Plainly there will be other kinds of light too, as from the service of fires. For there will be other species of spirit also, as from God, as from the devil. So when soul and spirit are in question, the soul itself will be spirit, just as the day itself is light. For the very thing through which something exists is that something.

[36077] 11. But that I should call the soul a spirit, the reasoning of the present question compels me, because breathing is ascribed to another substance. While we claim this for the soul, which we acknowledge to be uniform and simple, we must call it spirit under a certain condition — not by the name of its state, but of its act; not under the title of substance, but of operation, because it breathes, not because it is properly spirit. For to blow too is to breathe. So the soul also, which we defend as breath by reason of its property, we now pronounce spirit by reason of necessity. But against Hermogenes, who contends that it is from matter, not from the breath of God, we uphold properly the term ‘breath.’ For he, against the faith of Scripture itself, turns breath into spirit, so that — since it is incredible that the Spirit of God should come into transgression and then into judgment — the soul should rather be believed to be from matter than from the Spirit of God. Therefore both there we defend it as breath, not spirit, according to Scripture and according to the distinction of the spirit, and here we pronounce it spirit, however reluctantly, according to the community of breathing and blowing. There the question is of substance; for breathing is an act of substance. Nor need we dwell longer on this, except on account of the heretics who stuff into the soul some spiritual seed I know not what, conferred out of the hidden bounty of their mother Sophia, without the Maker's knowledge — whereas Scripture, more conscious of its Maker, who is its own God, has promulgated nothing further than that God breathed into the face of man the breath of life, and that man became a living soul, through which thereafter he both lives and breathes; the difference between spirit and soul being sufficiently declared in the following instruments, God Himself pronouncing, ‘The spirit went forth from me, and I made every breath.’ For the soul too is breath made out of spirit. And again, ‘Who gave breath to the people upon the earth, and spirit to those who tread it.’ For first the soul — that is, breath — to the people walking on the earth, that is, acting carnally in the flesh; afterwards the spirit to those who tread the earth, that is, who subdue the works of the flesh; since the apostle too says, not first that which is spiritual, but that which is animal, and afterwards the spiritual. For even though Adam at once prophesied that great mystery concerning Christ and the Church, ‘This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh; for this cause shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave to his wife, and they two shall be one flesh,’ he experienced an access of the spirit. For an ecstasy fell upon him, the operative power of the Holy Spirit, which is prophecy. For the evil spirit too is an accidental thing. So afterwards the Spirit of God turned Saul into another man, that is, into a prophet, when it was said, ‘What is this to the son of Kish? Is Saul too among the prophets?’ — just as afterwards the evil spirit turned him into another man, namely an apostate. Judas too, for some while reckoned among the elect, even to the office of the purse, though already a thief, was nonetheless not yet a traitor, until afterwards the devil entered into him. Therefore, if neither the spirit of God nor the spirit of the devil is sown into the soul from its birth, it stands that the soul is alone before the access of either spirit; and if alone, then simple and uniform in the name of its substance, and thus breathing from no other source than from the lot of its own substance.

[39704] 12. Accordingly the mind also — or the intellect, which the Greeks call ‘nous’ — we understand to be nothing other than a faculty of the soul, inborn and implanted and proper to it from its birth, by which it acts, by which it knows, which, having it with itself, out of itself it moves itself within itself, and so seems to be moved by it as if by another substance; as those would have it who decree that the mind is even the mover of the universe — that god of Socrates, that Only-begotten of Valentinus from the Father ‘Nous’ and the mother ‘Truth.’ How confused was the opinion of Anaxagoras! For, having reasoned that the mind is the beginning of all things, and having hung the swinging-pole of the universe upon its axis, and affirmed it to be pure and simple and unmixable, he by this very title above all segregates it from any commingling with the soul, and yet elsewhere assigns it to the soul. This Aristotle too noted, more ready, I know not, to fill out his own views than to empty others'. In short, he too, while he deferred the definition of the mind, meanwhile pronounced one kind of mind, the divine; and showing this in turn to be impassible, he too removed it from any partnership with the soul. For since it is agreed that the soul is capable of suffering the things it has been allotted to suffer, it will suffer either through the mind and with the mind: if it is conjoined with the mind, the mind cannot be brought in as impassible; or if not through the mind, the soul will not suffer with the mind either, and it will not be conjoined with that with which, and which, suffers nothing. Moreover, if the soul will suffer nothing through it and with it, then it will neither perceive nor know nor be moved through it, as they would have it. For Aristotle too makes the senses passions. Why not? For to perceive too is to suffer, since to suffer is to perceive. Accordingly to know too is to perceive, and to be moved is to perceive; so the whole is to suffer. But we see that the soul experiences none of these things without its being attributed also to the mind, since it is transacted through it and with it. Now therefore the mind is both mixable, against Anaxagoras, and passible, against Aristotle. But if a distinction is admitted, so that mind and soul are two things in substance, then to the one will belong every passion and perception and knowledge and act and motion, but to the other leisure and rest and torpor and now no employment at all; and either the mind or the soul will be idle. But if it is agreed that all these things are to be reckoned to both, then both will be one, and Democritus will prevail, removing the difference; and it will be asked how both are one — whether from a fusion of two substances, or from the arrangement of one. But we say that the mind is so conjoined with the soul, not as another in substance, but as the function of a substance.

[42613] 13. To this it remains to consider where the principality lies, that is, which presides over which; so that whatever is found to have the principality may be the mass of the substance, while that over which the mass of the substance presides may be reckoned among the natural functions of the substance. But who will not grant the whole sum to the soul, in whose name the mention of the whole man is entitled? ‘How many souls do I feed,’ says the rich man — not minds; and the helmsman wishes the souls safe, not the minds; and the countryman at his work, and the soldier in battle, affirms that he stakes his soul, not his mind. Whose perils or whose vows are the more named, the mind's or the soul's? And what are the dying said to be losing — the mind or the soul? Lastly, the philosophers themselves and the physicians themselves, although they too are about to dispute concerning the mind, yet each has inscribed the face of his work and the front of his subject as ‘Of the Soul.’ And that you may learn it from God too: God always addresses the soul, calls upon and summons the soul, that it may turn the mind to Him. It is the soul that Christ came to save; the soul that He threatens to destroy in hell; the soul that He forbids to be set at a higher price; the soul that He Himself, the good shepherd, lays down for His own sheep. You have the principality of the soul; you have in it the union of substance; whose instrument, you may understand, the mind is, not its patron.

[44099] 14. Otherwise the soul is single and simple and wholly of its own, no more buildable up from elsewhere than divisible out of itself, since it is also not dissoluble. For if it were buildable and dissoluble, it would no longer be immortal. And so, since it is no longer mortal, it is neither dissoluble nor divisible. For to be divided is to be dissolved, and to be dissolved is to die. But it is divided into parts — now into two by Plato, now into three by Zeno, now into five and into six by Panaetius, into seven by Soranus, even into eight in the school of Chrysippus, even into nine in that of Apollophanes; but also into twelve among certain of the Stoics, and into two more than that in the school of Posidonius, who, having begun from two titles, the principal — which they call the ‘hegemonic’ — and the rational — which they call the ‘logical’ — went on from there to seventeen. So various species, derived one from another, divide the soul. But things of this sort will be reckoned not so much parts of the soul as powers and efficacies and operations; as Aristotle too judged concerning some of them. For they are not members of the animate substance, but faculties — as the locomotive, the active, the cogitative, and whatever they distinguish in this manner, even those five most famous senses, sight, hearing, taste, touch, smell. To all of which, even though they have assigned certain dwelling-places severally in the body, this distribution will not therefore pertain to any sections of the soul; since not even the body itself is so divided into members as these would have the soul divided. On the contrary, out of the multitude of members one body is made, so that the very division is rather a conjoining. Behold the most marvellous munificence of Archimedes — I mean the water-organ: so many members, so many parts, so many fastenings, so many passages for voices, so many resources of sounds, so many combinations of measures, so many rows of pipes, and they will all be one mass. So also the breath, which there pants from the engine of water, will not therefore be separated into parts because it is administered through parts — solid indeed in substance, but in operation divided. This example is not far from Strato and Aenesidemus and Heraclitus; for they too maintain the unity of the soul, which, diffused through the whole body and everywhere itself, like breath in a pipe through its holes, so flashes forth through the organs of sense in various ways — not so much cut up as dispensed. By what titles all these things are named, and by what divisions out of themselves they are held, and by what allotted stations in the body they are sequestered, the physicians will rather consider together with the philosophers; for us a few things will suffice.

[46884] 15. First of all, whether there is any supreme grade of vital and sapiential power in the soul, which they call the ‘hegemonic,’ that is, the principal; for if it is denied, the whole standing of the soul is endangered. In short, those who deny the principal have first reckoned the soul itself to be nothing. A certain Dicaearchus of Messene, and, among the physicians, Andreas and Asclepiades, thus did away with the principal, since they would have the senses themselves be in the mind, on whose behalf the principal is claimed. Asclepiades is moreover carried along by this argumentation: that very many animals, when those parts of the body are taken away in which the principal is thought chiefly to consist, both live somewhat further and nonetheless retain their wits — as flies and wasps and locusts, if you cut off their heads, as goats and tortoises and eels, if you take out their hearts; and so the principal does not exist, for if it had, the vigour of the soul would not persist when lost along with its seats. But more, both philosophers — against Dicaearchus, Plato, Strato, Epicurus, Democritus, Empedocles, Socrates, Aristotle — and physicians against Andreas and Asclepiades, Herophilus, Erasistratus, Diocles, Hippocrates, and Soranus himself; and now, more than all, we Christians, who are instructed by God on both points: that there is a principal in the soul, and that it is consecrated in a certain recess of the body. For if we read that God is the searcher and beholder of the heart; if His prophet too is proved to be such by betraying the secrets of the heart; if God Himself anticipated the considering of the heart in the people, ‘Why do you think evil in your hearts?’; if David too says, ‘Create in me a clean heart, O God,’ and Paul says that one believes with the heart unto righteousness, and John says that each one is reproved by his own heart; if, lastly, he who has looked on a woman to lust after her has already committed adultery in his heart — then both things shine forth at once: that there is a principal in the soul, which the divine intention addresses, that is, a sapiential and vital power (for what is wise is living), and that it is held in that treasure-house of the body to which God looks. So you are not to think that this principal is agitated from without, according to Heraclitus, nor fanned through the whole body, according to Moschion, nor shut up in the head, according to Plato, nor that it presides rather in the crown of the head, according to Xenocrates, nor that it lies in the brain, according to Hippocrates; nor yet about the base of the brain, as Herophilus holds, nor in the little membranes, as Strato and Erasistratus, nor in the middle space of the eyebrows, as Strato the Natural Philosopher, nor in the whole breastplate of the chest, as Epicurus — but where both the Egyptians reported it, and those who were held to be commentators on divine things, as in that verse of Orpheus or Empedocles: ‘For in man the blood about the heart is perception.’ Protagoras too, and Apollodorus and Chrysippus, hold these views; so that, beaten back even by these, Asclepiades may go seek his goats bleating without a heart and drive off his flies flitting without a head, and let all men now know that they themselves rather live without heart and brain, who have pre-judged the disposition of the human soul from the condition of beasts.

[50291] 16. There is this also, pertaining to the faith, that Plato divides the soul in two ways, by the rational and the irrational. To this definition we too give our applause, but not so that both should be reckoned to nature. For the rational is to be believed natural, as being inborn in the soul from the first origin, by a rational Author, namely. For what is not rational which God has put forth even by His command, let alone that which He has properly emitted by His own breath? But the irrational is to be understood as something later, as having befallen by the instigation of the serpent — that very admission of transgression — and as having thence grown in and grown up together in the soul to the likeness now of a natural thing, since it befell at once in the first origin of nature. But since the same Plato says that the rational alone is, as it were, of the soul of God Himself, if we too should ascribe the irrational to nature, which our soul has been allotted from God, the irrational would equally be of God, as being natural, since God is the Author of nature. But in fact the entrance of fault is from the devil, and every fault is irrational; therefore the irrational is from the devil, from whom the fault too is, being foreign to God, from whom the irrational is alien. Accordingly the diversity of these things is from the distance between their authors. Accordingly, when Plato, segregating the rational to God alone, subdivides two kinds out of the rational — the indignant, which they call ‘thymikon,’ and the concupiscent, which they call ‘epithymetikon,’ so that the former is common to us and to lions, the latter to flies, but the rational belongs with God — I see that I too must retract upon this, on account of the things which are discovered in Christ. For behold, this whole trinity is in the Lord too: the rational, by which He teaches, by which He discourses, by which He paves the ways of salvation; the indignant, by which He inveighs against the scribes and pharisees; and the concupiscent, by which He desires to eat the passover with His disciples. Therefore among us the indignant and the concupiscent are not always to be reckoned from the irrational, since we are certain that in the Lord they ran their course rationally. God will be indignant rationally, namely with those with whom He ought; and God will desire rationally things worthy of Himself. For He will both be indignant with evil and desire salvation for the good. The apostle too gives us concupiscence: ‘If anyone desires the office of bishop, he desires a good work.’ But in saying ‘a good work,’ he shows a rational concupiscence. He concedes indignation also. Why not, since he himself took it up? ‘I would,’ he says, ‘that they were even cut off who subvert you.’ Rational is the indignation that is from a feeling of discipline. But when he says, ‘We were once by nature children of wrath,’ he brands the irrational indignation, as not being of that nature which is from God, but of that which the devil introduced, he himself being called the lord of his own order: ‘You cannot serve two masters’; and himself surnamed father: ‘You are of the devil your father.’ Do not fear to ascribe to him too the property of another nature, a later and an adulterous one — him whom you read of as the over-sower of wild oats and the nocturnal corrupter of the wheaten crop.

[53668] 17. There touches us also that question of the five senses, which we learn in our first lessons, since from this too something is procured for the heretics. There is sight, and hearing, and smell, and taste, and touch. The faith of these the Academics too harshly condemn — according to some, Heraclitus too and Diocles and Empedocles; and Plato certainly in the Timaeus, pronouncing sense-perception irrational and entangled with mere opinion. So a lie is charged against sight, in that it asserts oars in water to be bent or broken, against the consciousness of their soundness; in that it persuades us that a four-cornered tower seen from afar is round; in that it defames a most level portico as narrower at its far end; in that it joins to the sea a sky suspended at so great a height. Likewise hearing is held guilty of fallacy, as when we think a heavenly rumble, and it is a wagon; or, while a thunderclap is preparing, we believe for certain that the sound is from a wagon. So too smell and taste are arraigned, since the same unguents and the same wines are cheapened by later use. So touch too is reproached, since the same pavements are believed rougher to the hands, smoother to the feet, and in the baths the same pool of hot water is reported at first most boiling, then most temperate. ‘So in this way too,’ they say, ‘we are deceived by the senses, when we change our verdicts.’ More moderately the Stoics do not charge every sense nor always with falsehood. The Epicureans more consistently defend a truth equal to all and perpetual, but by another road. For it is not the sense that lies, but the opinion. For the sense suffers, it does not opine; the soul opines. And they cut off both opinion from sense and sense from the soul. But whence opinion, if not from sense? In short, unless sight had perceived the tower round, there would be no opinion of roundness. And whence sense, if not from the soul? In short, a body lacking soul will lack sense too. So sense is from the soul, and opinion from sense, and the soul is the whole. For the rest, it will best be laid down that there is certainly something which causes a thing to be reported by the senses otherwise than it is in fact. Moreover, if that can be reported which is not in the thing, why should it not equally be possible for that to be reported through which is not in the senses, but in those causes that intervene under their own name? And so it will be permitted to recognize these causes. For that an oar should appear bent or broken in water, the water is the cause; in short, out of the water the oar is whole to the sight. But the tenderness of that substance, by which a mirror is produced from light, just as it is struck or moved, so, quivering the image, overturns the line of the straight. Likewise, that the look of the tower should deceive, the condition of distance compels in the open; for the equality of the surrounding air, clothing the corners with equal light, obliterates the lines. So too the uniformity of the portico is sharpened at the end, while the line of sight, packed close in the enclosed space, is there thinned out at the very point where it is also extended. So too the sky is united with the sea, where vision is consumed, which, as long as it is vigorous, so long divides them. And what else will deceive hearing than the likeness of sounds? And if afterwards the unguent breathes less, and the wine tastes less, and the pool boils less, in all of them the first force is almost entire. For the rest, as to rough and smooth, the hands and feet — the one tender, the other callous — rightly disagree. So in this way no frustration of the senses will be without a cause. But if the causes deceive the senses, and through the senses the opinions, then the fallacy is to be placed neither in the senses, which follow the causes, nor in the opinions, which are directed by the senses that follow the causes. Those who are mad see some men in others, as Orestes sees his mother in his sister, and Ajax sees Ulysses in the herd, as Athamas and Agave see beasts in their children. Will you charge this lie to the eyes, or to the furies? Those who are jaundiced by an overflow of bile find all things bitter. Will you then charge taste with prevarication, or their state of health? And so all the senses are overturned or circumvented for a time, so that they are free from the property of fallacy. Nay, the verdict of fallacy is now not even to be ascribed to the causes themselves. For if these things happen by reason, reason does not deserve to be called fallacy. What ought to happen so is no lie. And so, if even the causes themselves are freed from infamy, how much more the senses, before which the causes now freely go — since precisely from this very thing truth and faith and integrity must be claimed for the senses, in that they report nothing otherwise than that reason ordained which causes a thing to be reported by the senses otherwise than it is in fact. What are you doing, most insolent Academy? You overturn the whole standing of life, you disturb the whole order of nature, you blind the very providence of God, who has set over all His works — to be understood, inhabited, dispensed, and enjoyed — fallacious and lying masters, the senses! Is not the whole creation administered to us by these? Is it not through these that a second equipping of the world came to it: so many arts, so many talents, so many studies, businesses, duties, dealings, remedies, counsels, consolations, ways of life, of culture, of adornment? They have seasoned the whole savour of life, while through these senses man alone of all creatures is recognized as a rational animal, capable of intelligence and knowledge, and of the Academy itself. But indeed Plato, that he might set no mark of testimony upon the senses, therefore in the Phaedrus too, in the person of Socrates, denies that he can know himself, as the Delphic inscription admonishes, and in the Theaetetus takes from himself knowing and perceiving, and in the Phaedo defers his verdict until after death, a posthumous verdict on truth, namely — and yet, not yet dead, he was philosophizing. It is not lawful, it is not lawful for us to call these senses into doubt, lest in Christ too their faith be deliberated, lest perhaps it be said that He falsely saw Satan cast down from heaven, or falsely heard the Father's voice testifying concerning Him, or was deceived when He touched Peter's mother-in-law, or afterwards perceived another smell of the unguent which He accepted for His burial, another taste of the wine which He consecrated in memory of His blood. For so Marcion too chose to believe Him a phantom, scorning the truth of His whole body in Him. But not even in His apostles was nature mocked. Faithful was both the sight and the hearing on the mount; faithful too the taste of that wine, though it was water before, at the marriage of Galilee; faithful too the touch thereafter of the believing Thomas. Recite the attestation of John: ‘What we have seen,’ he says, ‘what we have heard, we have seen with our eyes, and our hands have handled, of the word of life.’ A false attestation, assuredly, if the sense of eyes and ears and hands lies by nature.

[60910] 18. I turn to the part of the intellectual faculties, and how Plato commended them to the heretics as separate from corporeal things, having attained their cognition before death. For he says in the Phaedo: ‘What then concerning the very possession of wisdom? Will the body be a hindrance or not, if anyone takes it as a partner in the inquiry? Some such thing I mean — has sight and hearing any truth for men, or not? Do not even the poets always mutter this to us, that we hear nothing certain nor see anything certain?’ He remembered, of course, Epicharmus the comic poet too: ‘The mind sees, the mind hears; the rest is deaf and blind.’ And so again he says that he therefore over-knows who most knows with the mind, neither setting before himself vision nor drawing in any sense of that kind to the mind, but using the pure mind itself in reflection, to grasp each pure thing of reality — having withdrawn especially from eyes and ears and, if it must be said, from the whole body, as a thing that disturbs and does not permit the soul to possess truth and wisdom whenever it has dealings with it. We see, then, that against the corporeal senses another equipment is set forth, as far more suitable — namely the powers of the soul, working out the intellect of that truth whose objects are not present nor lie subject to the corporeal senses, but are far absent from common consciousness, in the secret and in the higher places and with God Himself. For Plato will have there be certain substances, invisible, incorporeal, supramundane, divine and eternal, which he calls Ideas, that is, forms, exemplars and causes of these natural things that are manifest and subject to corporeal senses; and these to be the truths, but the others to be their images. Do not the heretical seeds of the Gnostics and the Valentinians already shine out? For from this they snatch the difference between the corporeal senses and the intellectual powers, which they accommodate even to the parable of the ten virgins — so that the five foolish ones figure the corporeal senses, foolish, namely, because easy to be deceived, while the wise express the mark of the intellectual powers, wise, namely, because they touch that truth which is hidden and supernal and constituted in the Pleroma — the mysteries of the heretical Ideas. For this is what their Aeons and their genealogies are. And so they divide sense too, attributing the intellectual to their spiritual seed, but the sensual to the animal, since the animal in no way grasps the spiritual; and they say that to the one belong invisible things, but to the other things visible and lowly and temporal, which are met with by sense, constituted in images. For these reasons, then, we have established beforehand that the mind is nothing else than a faculty of the soul, fashioned with it; and that the spirit is nothing foreign except what the soul itself is through breath; and that what is to be reckoned an accession is whatever God afterwards, or the devil, has breathed in. And now, as to the difference of the sensual and the intellectual, we admit nothing other than the diversities of things — corporeal and spiritual, visible and invisible, published and hidden — in that the former are attributed to sense, the latter to intellect; the soul, however, being assigned to the service both of these and of those, so that it equally perceives corporeal things through the body as it understands incorporeal things through the mind, with this proviso, that it even perceives while it understands. For is not to perceive too to understand, and to understand to perceive? Or what will sense be, except the understanding of the thing that is perceived? What will intellect be, except the perception of the thing that is understood? Whence these torments of torturing simplicity and hanging up the truth? Who will exhibit to me a sense not understanding what it perceives, or an intellect not perceiving what it understands, so as to prove that the one can exist without the other? If corporeal things indeed are perceived, but incorporeal things understood, the kinds of things are diverse, not the dwelling-places of sense and intellect, that is, not soul and mind. In short, by what are corporeal things perceived? If by the soul, then the mind too is already sensual, not only intellectual, for while it understands, it perceives, because if it does not perceive, neither does it understand. But if corporeal things are perceived by the soul, then the intellectual power too is already of the soul, not only the sensual; for while it perceives, it understands, because if it does not understand, neither does it perceive. Accordingly, by what are incorporeal things understood? If by the mind, where will the soul be? If by the soul, where will the mind be? For things that are distant must be mutually absent when they work with their own functions. You will think indeed that the mind is sometimes absent from the soul. For thus it comes about that we do not know that we have seen or heard something, because the mind was elsewhere. Nay, I shall rather contend that the soul itself neither saw nor heard, because it was elsewhere with its own power, that is, the mind. For when a man is out of his mind, the soul is out of its mind, the mind not being abroad, but then suffering with it. For the rest, the lapse is principally the soul's. Whence is this confirmed? From the fact that when the soul has departed, the mind too is not found in the man; rather, it follows the soul everywhere, from which it does not lag behind even at the end. But since the mind follows and is assigned to the soul, just so the intellect is assigned to the soul as far as the mind follows it, to which the intellect is assigned. Let the intellect now be even superior to sense, and a better knower of mysteries — provided that it too is a proper power of the soul, as sense also is. It makes no difference to me, except when the intellect is preferred to sense for this purpose, that from this too it should be held the more separate in proportion as it is affirmed the superior. Then, after the difference, the preference too must be beaten back by me, who am to go forward even to the persuasion of a superior god. But concerning God we shall make trial with the heretics on its own field too. Now the title is of the soul, and there is no place for treacherously preferring the intellect. For even though the things attained by the intellect, as spiritual, are superior to those attained by sense, as corporeal, it will be a preference of the things — the more sublime, namely, over the lowlier — not of intellect over sense. For how can the intellect be preferred to sense, by which it is informed for the cognition of truths? For if truths are apprehended through images — that is, invisible things are known through visible — since the apostle too writes to us, ‘For the invisible things of Him, from the creation of the world, are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made,’ and Plato says to the heretics, ‘The face of hidden things is the things which appear,’ and it is altogether necessary that this world be a certain image of some other — there, indeed, the intellect is seen to use sense as guide and author and principal foundation, nor can it touch truths without it. How then will it be superior to that through which it exists, of which it has need, to which it owes all that it attains? Thus both are concluded: neither is the intellect to be preferred to sense — for that through which a thing subsists is inferior to itself — nor is it to be separated from sense; for that through which a thing exists is with it.

[68591] 19. But not even those are to be passed over who widow the soul of intellect, if only for a little while. For thus they pave the way for afterwards bringing it in, as also the mind, from which, namely, intellect would proceed. They will have infancy contained by the soul alone, by which alone it lives, so as not equally to know — since not all things that live also know. In short, trees live, and yet do not know, according to Aristotle, and to anyone else who imparts the animal substance to the universe, which among us in man is a private thing, not merely as the work of God, which the rest are too, but as the breath of God, which this alone, of which we speak, is born with all its equipment. And if we are challenged to the trees, we will embrace the example; since in them too, while still not even little trees, but as yet stocks and shoots, the moment they arise out of the trenches, there is in them the proper power of the soul. But, according to the reckoning of time, it tarries, coalescing and growing up together with its own strength, until age fills out the habit by which nature performs its work. Or whence presently are little branches grafted into them, and leaves formed, and buds swelled, and tiny flowers adorned, and juices seasoned, if not because in them rests all the equipment of the kind, and, brought forth by parts, grows great? Thence, then, they have knowledge whence they have life, by a property as much of living as of knowing, and indeed from infancy, themselves their own. For I see the vine, still tender and unripe, yet already understanding its own work and wishing to cleave to something, on which, leaning and entwined, it may thrive. In short, without waiting for the rustic's discipline, without reed, without stake, if it has touched anything, it will of its own accord love it, and indeed will embrace it more lustily out of its own genius than out of your decision. It hastens to be secure. I see ivies too, as much as you will, straightway attempting the heights from the very first, and hanging up with none going before, since they would rather climb up the walls in their woven thicket than be worn on the ground by a voluntary injury. On the other hand, those that fare ill on a building — how, as they grow, they recede, how they flee! You may perceive the branches aimed elsewhere, and understand the animation of the tree from its divorce from the wall; it is content with its own littleness, which it learned from the first origin of the most provident plant, fearing even ruin. These wisdoms and sciences of trees — why should I not maintain them? Let them live, as the philosophers will; let them know, as the philosophers will not; and let the infancy of wood understand — how much more that of man? Whose soul, like a kind of shoot drawn off into a slip from the stock of Adam and entrusted to the genital furrows of the female, has sprouted with all its equipment, as much in intellect as in sense. I lie, if not at once the infant, as soon as it has greeted life with a wail, testifies by this very thing that it has perceived and understood that it is born, dedicating all its senses there at once: sight by light, hearing by sound, taste by moisture, smell by air, touch by the earth. So that first cry is forced out of the first voices of the senses and out of the first pulses of the intellects. There is more: that some interpret that mournful voice as the augur, in the prospect of a tearful life, of its discomforts — so that it is to be reckoned even foreseeing from the entrance of birth, let alone understanding. Thereafter it proves its mother by spirit, and examines its nurse by spirit, and recognizes its carrier by spirit, fleeing strange breasts and refusing unknown cradles, seeking no one but from habit. Whence has it judgment of novelty and of custom, if it does not know? Whence has it to be offended and to be soothed, if it does not understand? It is wonderful enough that infancy should be naturally spirited without having a mind, and naturally affectionate without having intellect. But indeed Christ, by appealing to the praise out of the mouth of sucklings and little ones, pronounced neither childhood nor infancy dull; of which the one, meeting Him with its suffrage, was able to offer Him testimony, the other, slaughtered for His sake, assuredly felt the force.

[72954] 20. And here too, accordingly, we conclude that all the natural things of the soul, as belonging to its substance, are in it and proceed and thrive along with it, from the very point at which it is reckoned. As our own Seneca too often says: ‘There are implanted in us the seeds of all arts and ages, and God the Master brings out our talents from the hidden,’ namely from seeds implanted and hidden through infancy, which are also the seeds of the intellect. For from these the talents are produced. Moreover, of the seeds of crops too there is one form for each kind, yet various developments: some come out in entire condition, some answer even better, some degenerate, according to the condition of sky and soil, according to the manner of work and care, according to the chance of seasons, according to the licence of accidents. So too it will be possible for the soul to be uniform in its seed, manifold in its produce. For here too the places make a difference. At Thebes it is reported that dull and brutish men are born, at Athens men most acute in knowing and speaking; where, in the district of Colyttus, boys speak a month sooner with a precocious tongue. Indeed Plato too in the Timaeus affirms that Minerva, when she was constructing that city, looked to nothing else than the nature of the region, which promised such talents; whence too in the Laws he himself prescribes to Megillus and Clinias to provide a place for the city to be founded. But Empedocles places the cause of a keen disposition, or of a dull one, in the quality of the blood; the perfected and advanced he deduces from teaching and discipline. Yet the matter of national properties is now common knowledge. The comic poets mock the Phrygians as timid; Sallust strikes the Moors as vain and the Dalmatians as fierce; the apostle too brands the Cretans as liars. Perhaps something accrues also from the body and from health. Fatness hinders wisdom, leanness expedites it; paralysis squanders the mind, consumption preserves it. How much more will those things be reckoned among the accidents which, apart from corporeality and bodily strength, either sharpen or blunt. Doctrines, disciplines, arts and experiences, businesses, studies sharpen; ignorances, idlenesses, slothfulnesses, lusts, inexperiences, leisures, vices blunt; and besides these, if there are also other powers that preside. And indeed they do preside: according to us, God the Lord and the devil the rival; but according to the common opinion, the fate of providence too, and necessity, and the free arbitration of fortune. For the philosophers too distinguish these, and we have vowed them to be discussed according to the faith under their own title now. It appears how many are the things which have variously disposed the one nature of the soul, so that they are commonly reckoned to nature, when they are not species, but lots of nature and of one substance — that, namely, which God conferred upon Adam and made the womb of all. And so they will be lots, not species, of one substance; likewise this variety of character, which is now as great as it is, was not so great in the very head of the race, Adam. For all these things ought to have been in him, as in the fount of nature, and from there to have flowed forth with all their variety, if the variety had been of nature.

[76289] 21. But if the nature of the soul was uniform from the beginning in Adam, before so many talents, then it is not manifold through so many talents, nor in any way triform — that the Valentinian trinity may be cut down even now, which is not itself recognized in Adam. For what is spiritual in him? If it is that he prophesied that great mystery concerning Christ and the Church — ‘This is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh; she shall be called woman; for this cause a man shall leave his father and his mother, and shall be glued to his wife, and they shall be two in one flesh’ — this befell him afterwards, when God sent upon him the trance, the spiritual power in which prophecy consists. If the evil too appeared in him, the admitted transgression, this too is not to be reckoned natural, since he wrought it by the instigation of the serpent — as little natural as material, for we have already excluded the faith of matter too. But if neither the spiritual nor what is called the material was proper in him — and if it were from matter, there would have been a seed of evil — it remains that there was in him alone and only the natural, which is reckoned animal, which we have defended as simple and uniform in condition. Of this it plainly remains to ask whether it ought to be believed changeable, which is called natural. For the same persons deny nature to be convertible, that they may fix their trinity in its several properties; since a good tree bears not evil fruits, nor an evil tree good ones, and no one gathers figs from thorns or grapes from briars. Therefore, if it is so, neither will God be able to raise up sons of Abraham from the stones, nor make the offspring of vipers bring forth fruits of repentance. And the apostle erred in writing, ‘You too were once darkness,’ and, ‘We too were once by nature children of wrath,’ and, ‘In these you too once were, but you have been washed.’ But the holy sentences will never disagree. For the evil tree will not give good fruits, if it is not grafted; and the good will give evil ones, if it is not cultivated. And the stones will become sons of Abraham, if they are formed into the faith of Abraham. And the offspring of vipers will produce the fruit of repentance, if they spit out the venoms of malice. This will be the power of divine grace, more powerful assuredly than nature, having in us the free power of the will subject to itself, which is called ‘autexousion’; which, since it too is natural and changeable, is converted with nature whichever way it turns. But that the ‘autexousion’ is naturally in us we have already shown both to Marcion and to Hermogenes. What now, if the condition of nature must be so defined as to be determined twofold — of the born and the unborn, of the made and the uncreated? And so whatever is found to be born and made, its nature will admit of change; for it can both be born again and remade. But the unborn and uncreated will stand immovable. And since this belongs to God alone, as to the only unborn and uncreated, and therefore immortal and unconvertible, it is settled that the nature of all the rest, born and made, is convertible and changeable — so that, even if a trinity were to be ascribed to the soul, it would be reckoned from a change of accident, not from the establishment of nature.

[79608] 22. The other natural endowments of the soul Hermogenes has already heard from us, with the defence and proof of them, by which it is recognized as akin to God rather than to matter. Here they shall only be named, lest they seem to have been passed over. For we gave it both freedom of will, as we wrote above, and dominion over things, and divination at times — setting aside that which comes by the grace of God from prophecy. And so I shall now withdraw from this retracing of its disposition, in order to complete the account of it. We define the soul as born of the breath of God, immortal, corporeal, shaped, simple in substance, knowing of itself, proceeding variously, free in will, subject to accidents, changeable through its faculties, rational, sovereign, divining, redundant out of one. It follows now that we consider how it is redundant out of one, that is, whence and when and by what reason it is taken up.

[80533] 23. Some believe that they have come down from the heavens, with as much persuasion as they undoubtedly promise that they will return thither — as Saturninus, the disciple of Menander the Simonian, brought in, affirming that man was made by angels, and at first a feeble and weak and unstable work, throbbing on the earth like a worm, since the powers to stand were wanting; then, out of the mercy of the supreme power, to whose likeness, though not yet fully perceived, he had been rashly constructed, he obtained a little spark of life, which roused and erected him and animated him more steadily, and which after the departure of life is to be carried back to the mother-source. But Carpocrates too claims as much for himself out of the higher places, so that his disciples both equal their own souls now to Christ, let alone to the apostles, and, when they will, prefer them — souls which they have accordingly conceived out of a sublime virtue, despising the world's powerful principalities. Apelles relates that the souls were solicited by earthly baits, out of their supercelestial seats, by a fiery angel, the God of Israel and ours, who thereafter molded round them a sinful flesh. The school of Valentinus stuffs into the soul the seed of Sophia, through which they recognize the histories and Milesian tales of their Aeons out of the images of visible things. I grieve, in good faith, that Plato has been made the caterer of all the heretics. For it is his, in the Phaedo, that souls go thither from here and come hither from there. Likewise in the Timaeus, that the offspring of God, having had the generation of mortals delegated to them, when they had received the beginning of an immortal soul, froze about it a mortal body; and again that this world is the image of some other. All which, that he might commend it to belief — both that the soul, in former time in the higher places, lived with God in the fellowship of the Ideas, and that it crosses over hither from there, and that it here reviews the things it formerly knew of the exemplars — he elaborated a new argument, that ‘mathēseis’ are ‘anamnēseis,’ that is, that learnings are recollections. For souls, coming hither from there, forget the things in which they were before, and thereafter, taught by these visible things, remember. Since, then, by an argument of this kind those things are insinuated by Plato which the heretics borrow, I shall sufficiently strike the heretics back if I shatter the argument of Plato.

[83031] 24. First, indeed, I shall not concede that the soul is capable of forgetting, because he has granted it so great a divinity that it is made equal to God. He makes it unborn, which alone I might have armed myself with for the testimony of full divinity; he adds immortal, incorruptible, incorporeal — for who has believed even God to be this? — invisible, unshapeable, uniform, principal, rational, intellectual. What more could he proscribe for the soul, if he called it God? But we, who attach nothing to God, by this very thing weigh the soul far below God, in that we acknowledge it to be born, and through this of a more diluted divinity and a slenderer felicity, as breath, not as spirit; and even if immortal, so that this is a matter of divinity, yet passible, so that this is a matter of birth, and therefore, from the very first origin, capable of straying, and thence too akin to forgetfulness. Enough of this with Hermogenes. But that which, so as to be deservedly held God out of the equalizing of all its properties, would lie subject to no passion, would also lie subject to no forgetfulness — since forgetfulness is as great an injury as is the glory of that of which it is the injury, namely memory, which Plato himself proclaimed the safety of the senses and the intellects, and Cicero the treasure-house of all studies. Nor is this now to be brought into doubt, whether so divine a soul could have lost its memory, but whether it can again recover what it has lost. For a thing which ought not to have forgotten, if it has forgotten, I know not whether it can remember. So both suit my soul, not Plato's. In the second place I shall object: Do you make the soul possessed of those Ideas by nature, or not? ‘By nature, surely,’ you say. No one, then, will grant that natural knowledge of natural arts can fall away. The knowledge of studies will fall away, of doctrines, of disciplines; perhaps too of talents and affections, which seem natural and yet are not — because, as we premised, they are constituted out of accidents, according to places and institutions and corporealities and states of health and presiding powers and freedoms of will. But the knowledge of natural things does not fail even in beasts. The lion will plainly forget his ferocity, when forestalled by the training of tameness, and with all his array of mane will become the darling of some queen Berenice, cleaning her cheeks with his tongue. Manners will leave the beast; the knowledge of natural things will remain. The same will not forget the natural foods, the natural remedies, the natural terrors; and even if the queen offer him fish, even if cakes, he will desire flesh; and if she compose theriac for him when ailing, the lion will require an ape; and if she set no hunting-spear against him, yet he will dread a cock. Just so for man too — perhaps the most forgetful of all — the knowledge of natural things alone, as alone natural, will persevere unobliterated: ever mindful of eating in hunger and drinking in thirst, and of what is to be seen with the eyes and heard with the ears and smelled with the nostrils and tasted with the mouth and handled with the hand. These are surely the senses, which philosophy depreciates by the preference of the intellectual. Therefore, if the natural knowledge of sensible things remains, how is it that the knowledge of intellectual things, which is held the superior, has perished? Whence now is the very force of forgetfulness that precedes recollection? ‘From the multitude of time,’ he says. Improvidently enough. For the quantity of time will not pertain to a thing said to be unborn, and through this above all believed eternal. For that which is eternal, in that it is unborn, admitting neither beginning nor end of time, suffers no measure of time. For that which has no measure of time is neither subject to time by any change, nor is there any force in it out of a multitude of time. If time is the cause of forgetfulness, why is it that, from the moment the soul is brought into the body, memory slips away, as though the soul thenceforth endures time — which without doubt was not prior to the body without time? But entering the body, does it at once forget, or some while after? If at once, what multitude of time, not yet to be reckoned, will there be? Infancy, namely. If some while after, then in that space, before the times of forgetfulness, will the soul still act mindful? And what kind of thing is it that afterwards it should forget, and again afterwards remember? But at whatever time forgetfulness rushes in upon it, how great a measure of time too will be reckoned here? The whole course of life, I suppose, will not be enough to overturn the memory of so great an age before the body. But again Plato changes the cause to the body, as though this too were worthy of belief, that a born substance should extinguish the power of an unborn one. But there are great and many differences of bodies, according to nationality, magnitude, habit, age, health. Will then differences of forgetfulnesses too be reckoned? But forgetfulness is uniform; therefore the multiform corporeality will not be the cause of a uniform result. Many proofs likewise, Plato himself being witness, have proved the divination of the soul, which we have already set forth to Hermogenes. But there is no one of men who does not himself at some time feel his soul to be foreseeing, the augur of an omen or a peril or a joy. If the body does not obstruct divination, neither, I suppose, will it stand in the way of memory. In the same body, certainly, souls both forget and remember. If some condition of the body strikes in forgetfulness, how will it admit recollection, which is its contrary? — since recollection itself too, after forgetfulness, is a memory revived. What is adverse to the first memory, why should it not also be repugnant to the second? Lastly, who would remember more than little children, as the more recent souls, as not yet plunged into domestic and public cares, as given over to their studies alone — whose learnings become recollections? Nay, why do we not all alike remember, since we all alike forget? But only the philosophers, you say. Not even all of these. Plato alone, namely, in so great a forest of nations, in so great a meadow of the wise, both forgot and remembered the Ideas. Therefore, if this chief argumentation in no way stands, the whole of that to which it was accommodated is equally overturned — that souls were to be believed unborn, and conversant in the heavens, and conscious there of divine things, and carried down from there, and here remembering — plainly to furnish occasions to the heretics.

[89721] 25. Now then I shall return to the cause of this digression, to render how souls are redundant out of one — when, and where, and by what reason they are taken up. About which kind it matters nothing whether the question comes from a philosopher, or from a heretic, or from the common crowd. It makes no difference to the professors of truth about her adversaries, especially such bold ones as are, in the first place, those who presume that the soul is not conceived in the womb, nor compacted and produced together with the modeling of the flesh, but, when the birth has already been brought forth and the infant is not yet alive, is impressed upon it from without. Moreover, that the seed, sequestered from intercourse in the female parts and quickened by natural motion, thickens into the substance of flesh only; and that this, brought forth, and smoking from the furnace of the womb and loosened by heat, like iron made fiery and there plunged into cold water, so, struck by the rigour of the air, both seizes the animal power and gives back the sound of a voice. This the Stoics hold with Aenesidemus, and Plato himself at times, when he says that the soul, otherwise strange and an exile from the womb, is led in by the first inbreathing of the infant as it is born, just as it is led out by the last out-breathing. We shall see whether he has invented this opinion. Nor among the physicians too was Hicesius lacking, a prevaricator both of nature and of his own art. They were ashamed, I suppose, to lay down this which women would recognize. But how much more blushworthy an outcome, to be refuted by women than approved by them! For in this matter there is no master, arbiter, witness so suitable as the sex itself. Answer, you mothers — you who are pregnant, and you who have given birth — let the barren and the males be silent; the truth of your nature is inquired into, the faith of your experience is convened. Do you perceive in the fetus any vital movement other than your own, by which the loins throb, the sides quiver, the whole compass of the belly is pulsed, the whole region of the burden shifts? Are these motions your joys and your sure security, that you so confide the infant both to live and to play? Or, if its restlessness ceases, do you fear for it first? Does it even now hear within you, when it is startled at a new sound? Do you even crave food-fancies for it, and even feel loathings for it? Do you even share your states of health with each other — it indeed even out of your bruises, by which it too within is marked through the same members, snatching to itself its mother's injuries? If livid bruise and redness is a passion of the blood, there will be no blood without a soul; if illness is an access of the soul, there will be no illness without a soul; if nourishment, fasting, increases, decreases, fear, motion are a handling of the soul, then he who exercises these will live. In short, he ceases to live who ceases to exercise them. In short, the dead too are brought forth — how, unless they too were once alive? And who are even the dead, unless those who were first alive? But indeed even in the very womb the infant is slaughtered by a necessary cruelty, when in the delivery, turned aslant, it denies the birth — a matricide, unless it is to die. And so among the instruments of physicians there is even an organ by which the secret parts are first compelled to open, with a screw-fitted apparatus; with a ring-knife, by which the members within are cut up by an anxious decision; with a blunt hook, by which the whole deed is drawn out by a violent delivery. There is also a brazen spike, by which the very throat-cutting is directed in a blind brigandage: they call it the ‘embryo-slayer,’ from the office of infanticide, the destroyer, assuredly, of a living infant. This both Hippocrates had, and Asclepiades, and Erasistratus, and Herophilus too, dissector even of grown men, and the gentler Soranus himself — all certain that a living creature is conceived, and so, taking pity on so most unhappy an infancy of this kind, that it should be killed first, lest it be torn alive. Of which necessity of crime Hicesius too, I believe, did not doubt, while he superadds the soul to those already born from the stroke of cold air — since even the very word for soul among the Greeks answers to a cooling. Are then the barbarian and Roman nations animated otherwise, because they have named the soul by something other than ‘psychē’? And how many nations are reckoned under the most blazing pole, scorched even in their colour? Whence have they a soul, who have no rigour of air? I say nothing of the heat of bed-chambers and all the apparatus of warmth necessary there for women in labour, to be exposed to whose breath of air is rather a danger. In the very baths almost the fetus slips out, and at once the wail is heard. For the rest, if the rigour of air is the treasure of the soul, then outside the Germanies and Scythias and the Alps and the Argaean ranges no one ought to be born. And yet the peoples are more frequent in the eastern and southern temperature, and the talents more nimble, while all the Sarmatians too are torpid in mind. For minds, too, would come forth wiser out of rigours, if souls came out of little chills, since the power is together with the substance. These things being thus established beforehand, we can also call to mind those too who, drawn alive from their mother's womb cut open, drank the air — certain men named Liber and Scipio. But if anyone, like Plato, thinks accordingly that two souls cannot come together into one, just as neither can two bodies — to him I would show not only two souls heaped together into one, as also two bodies, in fetuses, but many other things too joined with the soul, namely a demon, and not one only, as in Socrates himself, but even of a sevenfold spirit, as in Magdalene, and of a legionary number, as in the Gerasene — so that the more easily soul will be joined with soul out of community of substance than an evil spirit out of diversity of nature. But the same man, in the sixth book of the Laws, admonishing us to beware lest the corruption of the seed, from some baseness of intercourse, should breed a blemish for body and soul, has, I know not, slipped more from his earlier or from this opinion. For he shows that the soul is brought in from the seed, which he warns must be cared for — not from the first inbreathing of the one born. Whence, I ask you, do we answer the likeness of the soul too to our parents in our talents, according to the testimony of Cleanthes, if we are not also brought out from the soul's seed? And why did even the ancient astrologers reckon a man's nativity from the beginning of conception, if from that point the soul too is not there? To which equally pertains, if anything does, the breath.

[96582] 26. But all the unevenness of human opinion reaches only as far as the boundaries of God. To our own lines now I shall gather my step, that what I have answered to the philosophers and physicians I may prove to the Christian. Build your faith, brother, on your own foundation. Look upon the living wombs of the most holy women, with the infants there not merely already breathing, but even prophesying. Behold, the bowels of Rebecca are disquieted, and the birth is still far off, and there is no impulse of air; behold, the twofold fetus tumultuates in the mother's parts, and there are nowhere as yet two peoples. A portentous petulance, perhaps, of an infancy contending before it lives, spirited before it is animated — if only it had troubled its mother by leaping. But when the birth is opened, and the number is inspected, and what was augured is recognized, then, I think, not only the souls of the infants are proved, but their battles too. He who had come first to be born was held back by the one forestalled, not yet more fully brought forth, but only born as to the hand. And if the first was drinking in his soul from the first inbreathing in the Platonic manner, or plucking it from the rigour of the air in the Stoic fashion, what of that one who was awaited, who was still detained within and already detaining the other without? Not yet breathing, I suppose, he had assailed his brother's heel; still hot with his mother, he desired to come forth before him. O infant both rival and strong and already contentious — alive, I believe! Look too upon singular conceptions, and indeed more monstrous ones, of the barren and of the virgin, who by this very thing might have brought forth imperfect ones, for the overthrowing of nature — since the one was numb to the seed, the other untouched. It were fitting, if anything, that those should be born without a soul who had not been duly conceived; but they too live, each in its own womb. Elizabeth exults — John had struck within; Mary glorifies the Lord — Christ had moved within. The mothers recognize each their own fetuses, themselves recognized in turn by them — assuredly living ones, which were not only souls, but spirits too. So too you read the voice of God to Jeremiah: ‘Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you.’ If God forms in the womb, He also breathes in, after the manner of the first origin: ‘And God formed man, and breathed into him the breath of life.’ Nor would God know man in the womb unless whole: ‘And before you came out of the womb, I sanctified you.’ And a body still dead? By no means whatever. For God is the God of the living, not of the dead.

[99226] 27. How then is the living creature conceived? Is the substance of both body and soul fused together at once, or does one of them precede? Nay, we say that both are at once conceived, and formed, and perfected, just as they are also brought forth; nor does any interval come in within the conception, by which an order might be arranged. For reconsider the first things from the last. If death is determined to be nothing other than the disjunction of body and soul, life, the contrary of death, will be defined as nothing other than the conjunction of body and soul. If the disjunction befalls both substances at once through death, this ought to be the form which conjunction too, equally befalling both substances through life, has dictated. Now we acknowledge life from conception, since we claim the soul from conception; for from that point life exists at which the soul does. Equally, therefore, those things are compacted into life which are equally separated in death. Then, if we give the primacy to one and the second place to the other, the times of the seed too must be distinguished according to the order of their rank. And when will the seed of the body be deposited, when that of the soul? Nay, if the times of the seeds are divided, then diverse materials too will be held, out of the distance of the times. For even though we shall confess two species of seed, the corporeal and the animal, yet we claim them to be undistinguished, and in this way contemporaneous and of the same moment. Let us therefore not be ashamed of a necessary interpretation. Nature is to be revered, not to be blushed at. It is lust, not the act, that has befouled intercourse; it is the excess, not the institution, that is unchaste, since the institution is blessed by God: ‘Increase and abound into a multitude.’ But the excess is cursed — adulteries and stuprations and brothels. In this solemn office of the sexes, then, which mingles male and female — in common intercourse, I mean — we know that both soul and flesh perform their function together, the soul by concupiscence, the flesh by act, the soul by instinct, the flesh by the deed. By a single impulse of both, then, the whole man being shaken, the seed of the whole man is foamed out, having from the corporeal substance its moisture, from the animal its warmth. And if the soul is a cold thing in the name the Greeks give it, why does the body grow cold when it is removed? In short — that I may now risk myself more by modesty than by proof — in that very heat of the last pleasure, by which the genital fluid is expelled, do we not perceive that something of the soul too goes out? And do we not accordingly grow faint and feeble, with a loss of light? This will be the animal seed, straightway from the distillation of the soul, just as that corporeal fluid too is the seed from the purging of the flesh. Most faithful are the examples of the first origin. From clay was the flesh in Adam. What else is clay than a rich liquid? Thence will be the genital fluid. From the inbreathing of God was the soul. What else is the inbreathing of God than a vapour of the spirit? Thence will be that which we exhale through that fluid. Since therefore in the first origin two diverse and divided things, clay and breath, had been compacted into one man, the two substances, now confused into one, mixed their own seeds too, and thence handed down a form for propagating the race; so that even now the two, though diverse, even united, flow forth equally, and equally insinuated into their furrow and field, equally make man bud forth out of both substances; in whom again its own seed is inborn after its kind, just as it has been prefixed for every generative condition. Therefore from one man comes all this redundancy of souls — nature, namely, observing the edict of God: ‘Increase and abound into a multitude.’ For even in the very preface of His one work, ‘Let us make man,’ the whole posterity was foretold in the plural: ‘And let them have dominion over the fishes of the sea.’ No wonder: the promise of the harvest is in the seed.

[103320] 28. What now is that old saying in the memory of Plato about the reciprocal coursing of souls — that those departing hence go thither, and again come hither and live, and thence depart from life, and again out of the dead are made alive? Pythagorean, as some will have it; divine, Albinus thinks it, of the Egyptian Mercury perhaps. But there is no divine saying except of the one God, with which the prophets, with which the apostles, with which Christ Himself thundered. Moses, much more ancient even than Saturn by about nine hundred years — let alone than his great-grandsons — and certainly much more divine, who set forth the descents of the human race from the beginning of the world too, through the several births, by name and by time, sufficiently proving the divinity of the work out of the divination of his utterance. But if the Samian sophist is Plato's authority concerning the relapse of souls, ever rolled back out of the alternate substitution of dead and living, certainly that Pythagoras, though good in other respects, yet, in order to build up this opinion, leaned upon a lie not only base but even rash. Learn, you who do not know, and believe with us. He feigns death, hides in an underground place, there condemns himself to seven years' endurance; meanwhile he learns from his only confidant and minister, his mother, those things concerning the recently dead which he was to relate for the credit of the matter; and when he has seemed to himself to have falsified his corporeality sufficiently for all the horror of an old corpse, he emerges from the recesses of his deceit, as one given back from the dead. Who would not believe that he whom he had believed dead had come to life again, hearing especially from him those things about the dead posterity which he could not seem to have learned except among the dead? So that the living are made out of the dead is an older saying. What of it, if it were even younger? Neither does truth desire antiquity, nor does falsehood shun novelty. I hold it plainly false, though noble in age; why not false, whose testimony too is from falsehood? How shall I believe that Pythagoras does not lie, who lies that I may believe? How will he persuade me that he was Aethalides and Euphorbus and Pyrrhus the fisherman and Hermotimus, before Pythagoras, in order to persuade me that the living are made out of the dead — he who again perjured himself to be Pythagoras? For by as much as it would have been more credible that he himself had once returned into life out of himself, than so often one and then another — by so much, in the harder matter, did he deceive, who lied in the softer. But he recognized the shield of Euphorbus once consecrated at Delphi, and said it was his own, and proved it by marks commonly unknown. Look to his underground vault, and, if it suffices, believe. For one who contrived such a trick, with injury to his good health, with the fraud of a life racked for seven years beneath the earth by fasting, sloth, and shadow — to whom the loathing of the sky was of such worth — what rashness would he not have approached, what curiosity would he not have attempted, to arrive at the mark of that shield? But what, if he found it in some more hidden histories? What, if he drank in some surviving little breaths of fame of a now decayed tradition? What, if, bribing the temple-keeper, he learned it by a secret inspection? We know too that it is permitted to magic to explore hidden things through the spirits of the underworld and the familiars and the Pythonic ones. For did not even Pherecydes, the teacher of Pythagoras, perhaps divine — not to say dream — by these arts? What, if the same demon was in him who in Euphorbus too had done the deeds of blood? In short, he who had proved himself Euphorbus from the argument of the shield, why did he recognize none of the Trojans, his fellow-soldiers, in like manner? For they too would by now have come to life again, if the living were made out of the dead.

[107321] 29. That the dead are made out of the living is established; yet not on that account also the living out of the dead. For from the beginning the living were prior; whence from the beginning equally the dead were posterior — from nowhere else than from the living. The former had something whence they might rather arise, provided it was not from the dead; the latter had nothing whence they could rather be derived, except from the living. Therefore, if from the beginning the living were not from the dead, why afterwards from the dead? Had that fount of origin, whatever it is, failed? Or did the form repent? And how is it preserved in the dead? Is it not the case that, because from the beginning the dead were from the living, therefore always from the living? For either in both directions the form of the beginning would have persevered, or in both it would have changed; so that, if afterwards the living ought to be made out of the dead, equally it ought also that the dead should be made not out of the living. If the faith of the institution were not to be equalized, contraries are not everywhere reformed by alternation out of contraries. For we too shall set against them the contrarieties of the born and the unborn, of sight and blindness, of youth and age, of wisdom and folly; and yet the unborn does not therefore come from the born because a contrary is made out of a contrary, nor sight again out of blindness because blindness befalls out of sight, nor youth revive again out of age because age withers out of youth, nor folly become blunted again out of wisdom because wisdom is sharpened out of folly. These things Albinus too, fearing for his Plato, subtly inquires into, distinguishing the kinds of contrarieties — as though these too were not as absolutely placed among contrarieties as those which are interpreted toward the opinion of his master, life, I mean, and death. And yet life is not given back out of death because death is brought down out of life.
