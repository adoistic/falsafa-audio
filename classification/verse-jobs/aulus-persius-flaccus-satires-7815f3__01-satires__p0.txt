[0] pr. Prologue I never wet my lips at the nag's spring,

[55] nor do I remember dreaming on two-peaked Parnassus,

[108] that I should step forth all at once a poet so.

[157] The Heliconian Muses and pale Pirene

[195] I leave to those whose portraits the clinging

[242] ivy licks: I myself, half a rustic outsider,

[288] bring my own song to the holy rites of the bards.

[339] Who taught the parrot his "good morning,"

[382] and schooled the magpie to attempt our speech?

[430] That master of skill, that lavisher of talent,

[478] the belly, an artist at chasing voices once denied;

[531] for let the hope of treacherous coin glitter,

[578] and you would believe that raven-poets and magpie-poetesses

[639] were singing the very nectar of Pegasus.

[681] 1. O the cares of men! O how much emptiness there is in things!

[746] "Who will read this?" Is it me you ask? Nobody, by Hercules. Nobody?

[816] Two perhaps, or nobody. A shameful, pitiable thing! Why so?

[877] Is it that Polydamas and the Trojan women may prefer

[931] Labeo to me? Trifles. No—if muddled Rome

[973] disparages anything, do not step in to correct

[1021] the faulty needle on her scales, and do not look for yourself outside yourself.

[1102] For at Rome who is not—if only it were permitted to say it—but it is permitted

[1182] then, when I look upon the grey hairs and that grim way of living of ours,

[1258] and on everything we do once the nuts of boyhood are set aside,

[1323] when we play the stern uncle: then, then forgive me—I will not—

[1388] what am I to do? But I have a saucy spleen—I burst out laughing.

[1454] We write shut indoors, one in measured verse, another in prose,

[1519] something grand, that the lung lavish with breath may pant out.

[1584] This, of course, you will read to the public, combed and in a fresh toga,

[1659] white-robed at last with your birthday sardonyx,

[1709] from a lofty seat, when you have rinsed your supple throat

[1769] with a liquid gargle, languid with a melting, lustful eye.

[1829] Then you may see great Tituses quivering, in no decent manner

[1892] nor with steady voice, when the verses enter their loins

[1950] and the inmost parts are tickled with the trembling line.

[2009] Do you, old man, gather morsels for other men's ears—

[2064] ears at which even you, ruined to the skin, would cry "Enough!"?

[2130] To what end have you learned, if this ferment, this wild fig

[2192] once sprung up within, should not break through and burst the liver?

[2262] Look at the pallor and the wasting! O for our morals—is knowing your own thing

[2342] worth so utterly nothing, unless another knows that you know it?

[2408] "But it is a fine thing to be pointed out and to have men say, 'That's the man';

[2490] do you reckon it nothing to have been the dictation-piece of a hundred curly-haired boys?"

[2582] Behold: over their cups the well-fed sons of Romulus

[2636] ask what the divine poems have to tell;

[2677] here some fellow, with a hyacinth-coloured cloak about his shoulders,

[2748] mouthing something rancid through a lisping nose,

[2799] filters out his Phyllises, his Hypsipyles, and whatever the bards make tearful,

[2880] and trips the words along his delicate palate.

[2928] The gentlemen approve: is not the poet's ash now

[2978] happy? Does not the tombstone now press more lightly on his bones?

[3046] The guests applaud: will not now from those shades,

[3099] now from the tomb and the blessed ashes,

[3141] violets spring up? "You mock," he says, "and you indulge too much

[3208] in a curled-up nose. Will there be a man who would refuse to wish

[3275] to have earned the public's praise, and—having spoken words worthy of cedar oil—

[3357] to leave behind poems that need fear neither mackerel nor incense?"

[3426] Whoever you are, you whom just now I made speak on the other side,

[3494] when I write, if by chance something rather apt comes out—

[3554] since this is a rare bird—still, if something rather apt does come out,

[3627] I would not be afraid of praise; for my fibre is not made of horn.

[3695] But I refuse that your "bravo" and your "charming" should be

[3757] the end and uttermost goal. For shake out this whole "charming":

[3823] what does it not contain within? Is not Attius' Iliad here,

[3884] drunk on hellebore? And whatever little elegies the crude

[3943] grandees have dictated? And whatever, in short, is written

[4003] on couches of citron-wood? You know how to set down a steaming sow's-belly,

[4080] you know how to bestow a worn-out cloak on a shivering client,

[4144] and "I love the truth," you say, "tell me the truth about myself."

[4212] How can that be? Shall I tell you? You trifle, baldhead, when

[4275] your fat paunch sticks out a foot and a half before you.

[4333] O Janus, whom no stork ever pecked from behind,

[4382] nor any nimble hand mimicked as white ass's ears,

[4433] nor any tongue, thrust out as far as a thirsty Apulian dog's!

[4496] You, O patrician blood, who must live

[4535] with a blind back of the head—turn round to face the grimace behind you.

[4609] What is the talk of the public? Why, what else but that verses

[4673] now at last flow with a soft measure, so that the join lets the stern

[4744] nails run smoothly over the surface? "He knows how to draw out a line

[4815] just as if he were guiding a red chalk-mark with one eye shut.

[4879] Whether the task is to speak against morals, against luxury, against the dinners of kings,

[4971] the Muse grants grand themes to our poet."

[5015] Behold, now we see men bring heroic sentiments forward

[5071] who used to trifle in Greek, with no skill to portray a sacred grove

[5141] or to praise a fruitful countryside—where there are baskets

[5202] and the hearth and pigs and the Palilia smoky with hay,

[5259] whence came Remus, and you, Quintius, wearing down your plough-share in the furrow,

[5344] when your trembling wife dressed you dictator before the oxen

[5407] and the lictor carried your ploughs home: "bravo, poet!"

[5465] There is the man now whom the swollen-veined book of Brisaean Accius detains,

[5544] there are those whom Pacuvius and his warty

[5589] Antiopa hold spellbound, "her grief-stricken heart propped on sorrows."

[5662] When you see bleary-eyed fathers pouring such lessons

[5717] into their boys, do you ask whence this hash of talk

[5771] has come upon their tongues, whence that disgrace by which

[5831] the smooth young dandy struts and preens before you across the benches?

[5904] Is it no shame that you cannot ward off perils from your grey head

[5972] without longing to hear this lukewarm "well said"?

[6024] "You are a thief," they say to Pedius. What does Pedius do? He weighs

[6095] his charges in balanced antitheses, and is praised

[6147] for having set out his learned figures. "How fine this is." This, fine? Are you wagging your tail, Romulus?

[6256] Should it move me? Should I, if a shipwrecked man sang, hold out

[6322] a copper? Do you sing, when you carry yourself painted on a broken plank,

[6397] slung from your shoulder? He who would have me bend with his lament

[6466] will weep with what is true, and not got up overnight.

[6522] "But grace and a smooth join have been added to the rough measures."

[6592] So Berecyntian Attis learned to close his line,

[6641] and "the dolphin that was parting blue Nereus";

[6690] so "we drew away a rib from the long Apennine."

[6739] "Arms and the man!"—is this not all froth and fat bark,

[6796] like an old branch dried out under its overgrown cork?

[6852] What, then, is tender, and to be read with relaxed neck?

[6910] "They filled their savage horns with Mimallonian boomings,

[6970] and the Bassarid, ready to bear off the head torn from a proud calf,

[7040] and the Maenad, about to guide her lynx with ivy-clusters,

[7100] redoubles her 'Euhion!'—and reparative Echo answers back."

[7160] Could such things be done, if any vein of our fathers' manhood

[7224] were alive in us? This nerveless stuff swims on the surface

[7285] of the lips, in spittle—Maenad and Attis are afloat in the wet,

[7350] and the man never beats his desk, nor smacks of bitten-down nails.

[7418] But what need to scrape tender ears with biting truth?

[7474] See to it, please, lest by chance the thresholds of the great

[7537] grow cold to you: here sounds the snarling letter of the dog's nose.

[7607] For all I care, henceforth let everything be white;

[7660] I make no objection. Bravo! Everyone, everyone—well done, you will all be marvellous things!

[7754] Does this please you? "Here," you say, "I forbid anyone to make a mess."

[7828] Paint two snakes: "boys, the place is sacred—relieve yourselves outside";

[7903] I withdraw. Yet Lucilius slashed the city—you, Lupus,

[7958] you, Mucius—and broke his back tooth on them;

[8005] sly Flaccus touches every fault of his laughing friend,

[8062] and, once let in, plays about the heart-strings,

[8112] clever at hanging the public from his well-blown nose:

[8168] and may I not so much as mutter? Not in secret? Not even into a ditch? Nowhere?

[8249] Yet here I will bury it. I have seen it, I myself have seen it, little book:

[8327] who does not have ass's ears? This buried secret of mine,

[8386] this laughter of mine, slight as it is—I would not sell it to you for any

[8461] Iliad. Whoever you are, breathed upon by daring Cratinus,

[8520] who grow pale over angry Eupolis and the grand old man,

[8577] look on these things too, if by chance you can hear something more refined.

[8654] From them let my reader come to a boil with a steamed ear—

[8714] not the man who itches to make sport of the Greeks' sandals,

[8776] a sordid fellow who can call a one-eyed man "One-Eye"

[8831] and, thinking himself somebody because, puffed up with Italian office,

[8903] he smashed the short measures as aedile of Arretium;

[8957] nor the man who is clever at laughing at the figures on the abacus

[9025] and the cones marked in the spread sand, ready to enjoy himself hugely

[9097] if some saucy streetwalker plucks the beard of a Cynic.

[9154] To such men I give the praetor's edict in the morning, Callirhoe after lunch.

[9233] 2. This day, Macrinus, count with the better pebble,

[9287] the bright day that adds to your gliding years;

[9336] pour the unmixed wine to your Genius. You are no man to ask in greedy prayer

[9414] for things you could entrust to the gods only by drawing them aside.

[9484] But a good share of our great men will pour their offering with a silent censer;

[9566] it is not easy for everyone to lift away the muttering and humble whispers

[9642] from the temples, and to live by an open prayer.

[9692] "A sound mind, good name, good faith"—this aloud, so even a stranger may hear;

[9772] but the rest he mutters within himself, beneath the tongue: "O if only

[9844] my uncle would bubble out his last—a splendid funeral!" And "O if only,

[9917] with Hercules's good favour, a jar of silver would ring out for me

[9985] under my hoe! Or that I could blot out the ward whose nearest heir

[10053] I am, treading on his heels—for he is scabby and swollen

[10111] with sharp bile! Already Nerius is burying his third wife!"

[10172] That you may pray for these things in holiness, you dip your head

[10239] in Tiber's eddy each morning, twice and thrice, and purge the night in the river?

[10322] Come now, answer me—it is the smallest thing I am trying to learn:

[10390] what do you think of Jupiter? Are you ready to set him above—

[10453] above whom? Above whom? Above Staius, say? Or do you hesitate?

[10517] What better judge, what fitter guardian for the fatherless?

[10578] Well then, that very thing by which you try to assail Jupiter's ear,

[10648] say it to Staius. "By Jupiter! O good god," he would cry,

[10707] "Jupiter!" And would not Jupiter himself cry out by his own name?

[10774] Do you suppose he has forgiven you, because when he thunders the holm-oak

[10849] is shattered with the sacred fire sooner than you and your house?

[10916] Or is it that, because you do not lie a grim corpse in a grove, a thing to be shunned,

[11004] at the bidding of sheep's entrails and Ergenna's word,

[11060] Jupiter therefore offers you his foolish beard to pluck?

[11118] Or what is the price by which you have bought the ears

[11174] of the gods? With lungs and fat tripe?

[11214] See—a grandmother, or an aunt fearing the gods, has taken the boy

[11281] from his cradle, and with the disgraceful finger and lustral spittle first

[11357] purifies forehead and moist lips, skilled at warding off the burning eyes;

[11433] then she dandles him in her hands, and with a suppliant prayer sends

[11503] her starveling hope now into Licinus's fields, now into the halls of Crassus:

[11582] "may a king and queen wish for him as son-in-law; may the girls

[11647] snatch at him; whatever he shall tread on, let it turn to roses."

[11714] But I do not entrust my prayers to a nurse. Refuse,

[11767] O Jupiter, these things to her, though she ask you robed in white!

[11835] You pray for strength to your sinews and a body trusty for old age:

[11904] so be it—agreed; but your great platters and rich sausages

[11964] have kept the gods from nodding assent, and hold Jupiter back.

[12028] You long to build your fortune by slaughtering an ox, and you summon

[12098] Mercury with the entrails: "grant prosperity to my household,

[12161] grant cattle, and increase to my flocks!" How, you wretch, can that be,

[12234] when the caul of so many heifers melts for you in the flames?

[12297] And yet he is bent on winning with offerings and rich cakes:

[12359] "now the field grows, now the sheepfold grows,

[12407] now it will be granted, now, now"—until, deceived and out of hope,

[12475] the coin at the bottom of his chest sighs in vain.

[12527] If I were to bring you bowls of silver and gifts chased

[12584] with thick gold, you would sweat, and from your left breast

[12645] your heart, trembling with joy, would shake out drops.

[12701] From this comes that practice: you overlay sacred faces

[12758] with triumphal gold; for among the bronze brothers, those

[12817] who send the cleanest dreams, free of rheum,

[12863] let them be foremost, and let theirs be the golden beard.

[12922] Gold has driven out the vessels of Numa and the Saturnian bronze,

[12989] and replaces the Vestal urns and the Tuscan earthenware.

[13047] O souls bowed to the earth and empty of heavenly things!

[13105] What good is it to bring our own morals into the temples

[13163] and to judge what is good for the gods from this sinful flesh?

[13227] This flesh has dissolved cassia for itself, ruining the oil,

[13289] and boiled the Calabrian fleece in tainted purple;

[13341] this it is that has bidden men scrape the pearl from the shell

[13405] and strain veins of glowing ore from the raw rock.

[13457] It sins, this flesh, it sins, yet it gets some use from its vice; but you,

[13533] tell me, you pontiffs: what is gold doing in a holy place?

[13593] Surely just what dolls dedicated to Venus by a girl are doing.

[13657] Why not give the gods what the bleary offspring of great Messala

[13723] could not give from his great dish:

[13760] right and law duly composed in the soul, and the holy recesses

[13824] of the mind, and a heart steeped through with noble honour.

[13885] Let me bring these to the temples, and I will win the gods' favour with meal.

[13964] 3. Is it always like this? Already the bright morning enters the windows

[14038] and widens the narrow chinks with its light;

[14084] we snore enough to sleep off the unmastered Falernian,

[14140] until the shadow touches the fifth line. "Now then, what are you about?

[14213] The mad Dog-star is baking the dry harvests this long while,

[14275] and every beast is under the spreading elm,"

[14321] says one of his companions. "Is it true? Is it so? Quick, let someone

[14392] come here—nobody?" The glassy bile swells in him:

[14443] he splits with shouting, so you would think the herds of Arcadia were braying.

[14523] Now the book is taken up, and the two-coloured parchment with its hair scraped off,

[14608] and the paper comes into his hands, and the knotty reed-pen;

[14670] then we complain that the ink hangs thick on the pen,

[14725] that the black cuttle-juice grows pale when water is poured in;

[14790] we complain that the pen-tip doubles its watered drops.

[14847] O wretch, and day by day more wretched! To this pass of things

[14911] have we come? Why not rather, like a tender pigeon

[14963] or like the sons of kings, demand your pap chopped fine,

[15021] and in a temper refuse to let mother sing you to sleep?

[15078] "Can I study with such a pen?" Whom are you fooling? Why do you

[15143] drone out these evasions? The game is at your own cost. You leak away, fool;

[15221] you will be despised. The half-baked jar, when struck, gives off a flawed sound,

[15303] and answers ill, made of unseasoned green clay.

[15352] You are wet and soft mud—now, now you must be hurried and shaped

[15418] without end on the sharp wheel. "But on my father's land

[15476] I have a modest store of grain, a clean and spotless salt-cellar—

[15543] what should you fear?—and a dish that guards the hearth in safety."

[15612] Is that enough? Or is it fitting to burst your lung with windy pride

[15682] because you draw your branch as the thousandth on a Tuscan stock,

[15749] or because you greet your own censor in your festal robe?

[15808] Off to the rabble with your trappings! I know you inside and to the skin.

[15883] Are you not ashamed to live by the loose ways of unbelted Natta?

[15949] But he is numb in his vice, and the fat has grown thick over his vitals;

[16023] he feels no fault, knows not what he is losing, and sunk deep below

[16092] no longer rises to bubble at the surface.

[16135] Great father of the gods, may it please you to punish cruel tyrants

[16204] in no other way than this, when dread lust,

[16249] steeped in burning venom, has stirred their nature:

[16302] let them see virtue, and pine for having forsaken it.

[16357] Did the bronze of the Sicilian bull groan more,

[16406] or did the sword hung from the gilded panelling more

[16460] terrify the purple necks beneath it, than when a man says to himself,

[16531] "down, down headlong we go," and pales within, unhappy,

[16588] at a thing his nearest wife must never know?

[16634] Often as a boy, I remember, I would touch my eyes with oil

[16694] when I did not wish to recite the grand dying words of Cato—

[16756] words much to be praised by a teacher who had no sense—

[16813] words my father, sweating, would hear, with his friends brought in;

[16882] for in truth this was my highest wish: to know what a lucky six

[16947] would bring, and how much the ruinous low throw

[16996] would scrape away; not to be cheated by the neck of the narrow jar,

[17065] nor have anyone more cunning at whipping the boxwood top.

[17124] To you it is no untried thing to catch out crooked characters,

[17188] and all that the wise Porch teaches—the Porch painted

[17243] with the trousered Medes—where the sleepless and close-cropped youth

[17313] keep vigil, fed on pulse and thick barley-porridge;

[17366] and the letter that parted the Samian branches in two

[17421] has shown you the rising path on the right-hand track.

[17477] Do you still snore? And does your slack head, its joints undone,

[17543] gape with yesterday's yawn, your jaws unhinged on every side?

[17606] Is there anything you aim at, anything you point your bow toward?

[17673] Or do you chase ravens everywhere, with potsherd and clay,

[17733] careless where your foot may carry you, and live from moment to moment?

[17806] In vain you will see men begging hellebore when the sick skin

[17869] is already swollen: meet the disease as it comes,

[17920] and what need to promise great mountains to Craterus?

[17975] Learn, O wretched men, and come to know the causes of things:

[18038] what we are, and for what kind of life we are born; what order

[18102] is given us, or where and whence the soft turn round the post;

[18166] what limit there is to wealth, what it is right to pray for, what use

[18237] the rough coin has; how much should be lavished on one's country and dear kin;

[18317] what God has bidden you to be, and in what part of human affairs you are placed.

[18399] Learn, and do not envy the man whose many a jar grows rank with fat

[18468] in the rich larder of the well-defended Umbrians,

[18519] with pepper and hams, the tokens of a Marsian client,

[18574] and a sprat that has not yet given out in the first jar.

[18632] Here some fellow of the goatish breed of centurions

[18685] may say: "What I understand is enough for me. I do not care

[18746] to be what the Arcesilauses and the careworn Solons were,

[18805] with head bowed sideways and eyes fixed on the ground,

[18861] while they gnaw their mutterings and rabid silences within,

[18922] and balance their words on a thrust-out lip,

[18968] brooding over the dreams of a sick old man: that nothing comes

[19032] from nothing, that nothing can return to nothing.

[19083] Is this what you grow pale over? Is this a reason for a man to go without lunch?"

[19166] At this the people laugh, and the brawny youth

[19214] redouble their quivering guffaws with wrinkling noses.

[19270] "Examine me—something flutters here in my chest, and a heavy breath

[19339] comes up from my ailing throat; examine me, please,"

[19393] says the man to his doctor. Ordered to rest, after

[19445] the third night had seen his blood run calm,

[19491] he asks for a mild Surrentine from a great house's cellar,

[19551] a flask thirsting only a little, to drink before his bath.

[19611] "Good fellow, you are pale." "It is nothing." "Look to that, though,

[19681] whatever it is: your yellow skin is quietly rising on you."

[19742] "But you are worse pale than I." "Don't play guardian to me.

[19804] Him I buried long ago: you remain." "Go on, I will say no more."

[19870] Swollen with feasting, and with a white belly, he goes to bathe,

[19936] his throat slowly breathing out sulphurous fumes.

[19987] But a tremor comes upon him amid the wine, and shakes the warm cup

[20055] from his hands; his bared teeth chatter,

[20097] and then the greasy morsels fall from his slack lips.

[20152] From this come the trumpet, the candles; and at last our little blessed one,

[20230] laid out on a high bier and plastered with thick perfumes,

[20290] stretches his stiff heels toward the door, and the new-made citizens,

[20361] freedmen of yesterday, with caps on their heads, take him up.

[20424] Touch your veins, wretch, and lay your right hand on your breast:

[20491] no heat here. Touch the tips of your feet and your hands:

[20550] they are not cold. But if the sight of money, or if

[20603] your neighbour's fair girl has smiled softly at you,

[20657] does your heart beat steadily? Coarse greens are set before you

[20722] in a cold dish, and meal sifted through the people's sieve:

[20783] let us try your throat. A rotten ulcer lurks in your tender mouth,

[20851] which it would not do to scrape with common beet.

[20902] You shiver when white fear has shaken the bristles up on your limbs;

[20972] now, when the torch is held beneath, your blood boils, and your eyes

[21042] flash with anger; you say and do such things as Orestes himself,

[21108] mad as he was, would swear under oath were not the acts of a sane man.

[21180] 4. "Do you handle the affairs of the people?"—imagine this is said

[21248] by the bearded master whom the dread draught of hemlock carried off.

[21318] On what do you rely? Tell me this, ward of great Pericles.

[21378] No doubt genius and a quick wisdom in affairs

[21425] came to you before your beard, and you are skilled in what to say and what to keep silent.

[21517] So, when the little mob seethes, stirred to bile,

[21568] your spirit prompts you to impose silence on the heated crowd

[21631] with a majestic gesture of the hand. What then will you say? "Citizens,

[21704] this, suppose, is not just; that is wrong; this other is more right."

[21775] For you know how to suspend justice on the twin scales

[21831] of the doubtful balance; you discern the straight where it comes

[21897] between crooked things, or when the rule deceives with its bent foot;

[21968] and you can set the black theta against a fault.

[22018] Why then, vain in your fine outward skin, do you not

[22072] stop wagging your tail before the fawning rabble before your time,

[22140] you who would do better to drink down neat doses of hellebore?

[22204] What is your highest good? To have lived forever on a greasy plate,

[22273] and to keep your little skin tended in the unfailing sun?

[22332] Wait—this old woman would answer no differently. Go now,

[22390] puff yourself up: "I am the son of Dinomache, I am fair." So be it,

[22459] provided ragged Baucis has no worse taste

[22502] when she has cried her basil to a slovenly slave.

[22553] How no one tries to descend into himself—no one—

[22603] but each man stares at the wallet on the back ahead of him!

[22664] Ask, "Do you know Vettidius's estates?" "Whose?" "His who,

[22724] rich, ploughs at Cures more land than a kite can range over."

[22787] You mean this man—this fellow, hateful to the gods and with a left-handed Genius,

[22870] who, whenever he fixes the yoke at the doused crossroad-shrine,

[22935] dreading to scrape the old crust from his little jar,

[22990] groans, "may all be well," as he bites the salted

[23041] onion in its skin, and—while the boys clap over the porridge-pot—

[23108] swallows the ragged dregs of vinegar going dead?

[23158] But if you idle anointed and pin the sun upon your skin,

[23216] there is a stranger at your elbow to nudge you and spit

[23273] his scorn: "such manners!—to weed the privates and the secrets of the loins,

[23351] and spread your flabby parts before the public!

[23400] When you comb the balsamed fleece on your jaws,

[23449] why does a shorn shame stick out below?

[23490] Though five wrestling-trainers pluck these saplings

[23543] and shake your boiled buttocks loose with the curved tongs,

[23604] still that bracken is tamed by no plough."

[23648] We strike, and in turn offer our own legs to the arrows;

[23706] this is how we live, this is the way we know. Below the groin

[23769] you have a hidden wound, but a sword-belt broad with gold
