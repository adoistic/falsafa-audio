[105611] 17.1. Here it befell me to see a sight of the following kind. When the army with John came into Picenum, there was, as was natural, much confusion among the people there. 17.2. And of the women, some fled suddenly wherever it had become possible for each, while others, being caught, were carried off in no orderly fashion by those who fell in with them. 17.3. In one of these places a certain woman who had just given birth happened to have left her child lying on the ground in its swaddling-clothes, and whether fleeing or caught by someone, she was no longer able to return there; for it befell her plainly to have vanished either from among men or from Italy. 17.4. So the child, left in this solitude, kept weeping. And a single she-goat, seeing it, took pity on it and, coming near (for she too happened to have just given birth), gave it the teat and guarded the child carefully, 17.5. lest a dog or some wild beast should harm it. And as much time was spent in the confusion, it came about that the child shared in this teat for a long while. 17.6. And when it became known to the people of Picenum afterward that the emperor's army had come there for the harm of the Goths, but that the Romans would suffer nothing unpleasant from it, 17.7. they all returned home straightway. And when in Urbisalia the women had come with the men, all who were Roman by race, when they saw the child surviving in its swaddling-clothes, being unable to account for what had happened, they were in great wonder that it lived. 17.8. And each one offered her teat who happened to be in a suitable condition for this. But the child would no longer take human milk, and the goat was by no means willing to let it go, but bleating most continually about the child, she seemed to the bystanders to be doing dreadful things because the women, coming close to the child, were thus troubling it, and to put it in a word, she laid claim to the infant as her own. 17.9. And from this the women no longer troubled the child, and the goat reared it more fearlessly and, taking care of it otherwise, guarded it. Wherefore the people of the place called this child Aegisthus. 17.10. And when it befell me to be present there, making a display of the marvel, they used to bring me to the child and on purpose, 17.11. in order that it might cry, troubled it. And it, distressed by those who troubled it, wept, and the goat, hearing it (for she was distant from it about a stone's throw), at a run and bleating loudly came to it, and coming and standing over it, took her stand, so that no one might be able to trouble it for the rest of the time. Such, then, are the things about this Aegisthus. 17.12. And Belisarius advanced through the mountains there. For being far surpassed in numbers by his opponents, he was not willing to come to close quarters with them in the open, since he saw too that the barbarians were resolved to die in their encounters; 17.13. and he thought that they, as soon as they should learn that an army of the enemy was advancing against them from all sides, would be mindful of no resistance, but would straightway turn to flight. And he hit upon the true opinion, having suspected nothing far from what was to be. 17.14. For when they had come into the mountains, about a day's journey distant from Ariminum, they fell in with a few Goths who were travelling by the road on some errand. 17.15. And these, falling unexpectedly upon an army of the enemy, were by no means able to turn aside from the road, until, being shot at by those who went in front, some of them fell there, while others, having become wounded, ran up onto some of the cliffs there and escaped notice. 17.16. From there, observing the Roman army pouring into all the difficult places, they supposed them to be far more than the true measure. 17.17. And there, seeing also the standards of Belisarius, they knew that he was leading this army. And night came on, and the Romans bivouacked there, while the wounded among the Goths went secretly to the camp of Wittigis. 17.18. And coming there about midday, they showed their wounds and affirmed that Belisarius with an army greater than could be numbered would presently be at hand. 17.19. And they were preparing as if for battle, turned toward the north wind of the city of Ariminum (for there they supposed the enemy would arrive), and they all kept looking continually toward the heights of the mountain. 17.20. But when, night having come on for them, they laid down their arms and were resting, they saw many fires to the east of the city, about sixty stades off, which the army with Martinus was burning, and they came into helpless fear; 17.21. for they suspected they would be encircled by the enemy at daybreak. So this night they bivouacked in such fear, and on the following day, with the sun rising, they saw a fleet of ships coming against them, prodigious in size; and falling into speechlessness, they rushed into flight. 17.22. And packing up quickly, they were held by so great a tumult and shouting that they neither heard the orders given nor took anything else into their mind than how each one might first get away from the camp and get inside the wall of Ravenna. 17.23. And if there had happened to be any strength or courage left to the besieged, they would have sallied out and killed very many of the enemy there, and the whole war would have ended there. 17.24. But as it was, this was prevented both by the great fear that had come upon them before and by the weakness, befalling many through the want of necessities. So the barbarians, by the excess of their tumult leaving some of their goods there, ran at full speed toward Ravenna.

[111300] 18.1. And first of the Romans, Ildiger and his men, coming to the camp of the enemy, made into the account of slaves those of the Goths who, held by some sickness, had remained there, and gathered all the goods which the Goths had left in their flight. 18.2. And Belisarius came with the whole army at midday. And when he saw John and his men pale and terribly squalid, hinting at the unreasoning quality of his rashness, he said that John owed thanks to Ildiger. 18.3. But he said he owed acknowledgement not to Ildiger but to Narses the steward of the emperor, hinting, I suppose, that Belisarius was not very willing, but had been persuaded by Narses to help them. And for the future the two used much suspicion toward each other. 18.4. Wherefore the friends of Narses would not even allow him to campaign with Belisarius, but persuaded him how shameful it would be for one who shared the secrets of the emperor not to be supreme commander of the army, but to obey a general. 18.5. For they declared that Belisarius would never willingly command the army on equal terms with him, but that, if he wished to lead the Roman army by himself, more soldiers and far better ones, with their commanders, would follow. 18.6. For they said that the Eruli and the guards and shield-bearers of Belisarius himself, and those whom Justinus and John himself commanded, together with the followers of Aratius and Narses, were no fewer than ten thousand, men brave and exceedingly good in matters of war, and that they wished that the conquest of Italy should be reckoned not to Belisarius alone, but that Narses too should bear his share. 18.7. For they thought he had left the company of the emperor not in order that by his own dangers he might strengthen the reputation of Belisarius, but in order that, displaying deeds of intelligence and bravery, he might become renowned among all men, as was likely. 18.8. And yet they said that Belisarius would not even be able to accomplish anything without them for the future. 18.9. For the most of the army which he commanded had already been left in forts and cities which he himself had taken, and they enumerated all in order, beginning from Sicily as far as Picenum. 18.10. And Narses, when he heard these things, was exceedingly pleased at the suggestion, and could neither restrain his thought nor remain in his present position any longer. 18.11. So often, when Belisarius saw fit to undertake some task, he withdrew on various pretexts at various times and rejected the proposal. 18.12. Belisarius, perceiving this, calling together all the commanders, spoke as follows: “I do not seem to myself to think the same things as you, men in command, about this war. 18.13. For I see that you despise the enemy as being altogether defeated. 18.14. But I think that, through this boldness of yours, we shall fall into manifest danger, since I know that the barbarians have been defeated by us not through any cowardice nor through fewness of numbers, but, outgeneralled by forethought and counsel alone, have been turned to flight from here. 18.15. And I fear lest you, being deceived by this untrue opinion, work irreparable harm both to yourselves and to the affairs of the Romans. 18.16. For those who seem to have conquered, lifted up by what has been done, are more easily destroyed than those who have failed contrary to expectation, but for the rest are gripped by fear and much anxiety toward their opponents. 18.17. For carelessness has destroyed some of those who were well established, but toil with care has profited many of the unfortunate. 18.18. Since the power of those who yield to negligence is wont for the most part to be lessened, but practice is naturally sufficient to put strength in. 18.19. Let each of you, then, consider that in Ravenna are Wittigis and many tens of thousands of Goths, and Uraïas is besieging Mediolanum and has surrounded all Liguria, and Auximus is already full of a large army worthy of account, and many other places are guarded against us by barbarians who are a match for us, as far as Urbiventus, which happens to be in the neighbourhood of Rome. 18.20. So that now more than before our affairs stand in danger, since we have fallen into a kind of encirclement by the enemy. 18.21. For I forbear to say that they declare the Franks too are arrayed with them in Liguria, a thing which it is sufficiently worth while for all the Romans to call to mind, not without great fear. 18.22. I say, then, that a part of the army ought to be sent to Liguria and Mediolanum, while the rest for the present should go against Auximus and the enemy there, to accomplish whatever God may grant; and then to manage the other operations of the war in whatever way may seem best and most excellent.” Belisarius said so much. 18.23. And Narses answered as follows: “In other respects, O general, no one would gainsay that all has been spoken by you with truth. 18.24. But to set apart this whole army of the emperor for Mediolanum and Auximus alone I think altogether inexpedient. 18.25. But that you should lead such of the Romans as you wish against these is in no way unfitting; we, however, will win for the emperor the land of Aemilia, which they say the Goths especially lay claim to, and will so throw Ravenna into confusion that you will deal with the enemy near you as you wish, they being shut off from all hope of those who might defend them. 18.26. For if we should choose to besiege Auximus along with you, I fear lest, when the barbarians come out from Ravenna, it may befall us both to be set between two enemies and, being cut off from the necessities, to be destroyed there.” Narses said so much. 18.27. And Belisarius, fearing lest, the Romans going into many parts, the affairs of the emperor should fall to ruin and be confounded by the disorder thence arising, showed a letter of the emperor Justinian which he had written to the commanders of the army. 18.28. And the writing made plain the following: “We have not sent Narses our steward into Italy in order that he should command the army; for we wish Belisarius alone to lead the whole army in whatever way may seem to him best, and it behoves you all to follow him for what is advantageous to our state.” Such, then, was the emperor's letter. 18.29. But Narses, laying hold of the very end of the letter, contended on the contrary that Belisarius was now deliberating for the advantage of the state; wherefore it was not necessary for them to follow him.

[117799] 19.1. Hearing this, Belisarius sent Peranius with a large army to Urbiventus, in order that they might besiege it, while he himself led the army against Urbinus, a city both strong and having a sufficient garrison of Goths (this is distant a day's journey from the city of Ariminum for an unencumbered man), and there followed him as he led the army both Narses and John and all the rest. 19.2. And coming near the city, they encamped at the foot of the hill in two divisions; for they were not arrayed with one another, but Belisarius and his men held the parts to the east of the city, while Narses and his men held those to the west. 19.3. Now Urbinus lies on a hill, round and exceedingly high. The hill, however, is neither precipitous nor altogether impassable, but only hard to traverse because it is very steep, especially as one goes very close to the city. 19.4. And it has one entrance on the level toward the north wind. So the Romans were arrayed for the siege thus. And Belisarius, thinking that the barbarians would more easily come over to him by agreement, inasmuch as they had been dismayed at the danger, sent envoys to them and, promising that many good things would come to them, exhorted them to become subjects of the emperor. 19.5. So the envoys, coming near the gates (for the enemy did not receive them into the city), said much that was very enticing, but the Goths, bold in the strength of the place and the abundance of provisions, would by no means admit the proposals, but ordered the Romans to depart from there quickly. 19.6. So Belisarius, hearing this, ordered the army to gather thick rods and to make from them a long shed. 19.7. Hiding inside it, they were to go very close to the gates with it, where the ground is most level, and to use it for their plot against the wall. And they did accordingly. 19.8. But some of the friends of Narses, joining him, said that Belisarius was doing endless things and devising the impossible. For John had already made trial of the place, and that too when a few men held the garrison at that time, and had perceived it to be altogether impregnable (and so it was); but he ought to recover for the emperor the regions in Aemilia. 19.9. Persuaded by this suggestion, Narses by night broke off the siege, although Belisarius entreated much both that he should stay there and that they should help take the city of Urbinus together. 19.10. These went quickly to Ariminum with the rest of the army. And Moras and the barbarians, when at daybreak they saw the enemy withdrawn by half, jeered from the wall, taunting those who had remained. 19.11. And Belisarius wished to assault the wall with the army that was left. And while he was deliberating these things, it befell that a marvellous piece of good fortune came about. 19.12. There was a single spring in Urbinus, from which all the inhabitants there drew water. This, drying up little by little of its own accord, was failing. 19.13. And in three days the water so failed it that the barbarians, drawing it up from there with mud, drank it. Wherefore they resolved to come over to the Romans. 19.14. But Belisarius, having by no means learned this, wished to make trial of the wall. And arming the rest of the army he placed them in a circle all about the hill, and ordered some men on the level ground to bring up the shed of rods; 19.15. for so they have been accustomed to call this engine. And they, slipping inside it, kept advancing and dragging the shed along, escaping the notice of the enemy. 19.16. So the barbarians, stretching out their right hands from the battlements, begged to obtain peace. But the Romans, not knowing anything of what had happened about the spring, suspected that they feared the war and the engine. Both sides, then, gladly abstained from the battle. 19.17. And the Goths surrendered both themselves and the city to Belisarius by agreement, on condition that they should remain unharmed, having become subjects of the emperor with the Roman army on equal and like terms. 19.18. And Narses, hearing this, regarded the matter both with wonder and as a misfortune. 19.19. And he himself still remained quiet in Ariminum, but ordered John to lead the whole army against Caesena. And they went carrying ladders. 19.20. And when they came near the fort, they made an assault and tried the wall. But as the barbarians defended themselves stoutly, many others fell there, and Phanitheus the leader of the Eruli. 19.21. And John, having then failed at the fort of Caesena, no longer saw fit to make trial, since it seemed to him impregnable, and with Justinus and the rest of the army drove forward. 19.22. And he took by a sudden assault an ancient city which is named Forocornelius, and the barbarians always withdrawing and by no means coming to close quarters, he recovered the whole of Aemilia for the emperor. These things, then, were carried on thus.

[122710] 20.1. And Belisarius, when he had taken Urbinus about the winter solstice, thought it inexpedient to go to Auximus immediately; for he suspected that much time would be spent for them in the siege of it. 20.2. For it was impossible to take the place by force because of the strength of its defences, and the barbarians, many and excellent, who held the garrison there, as has been shown by me before, having plundered a large district, had brought in for themselves a great quantity of provisions. 20.3. And he ordered Aratius with a large army to winter in Firmum and to keep watch that for the future the barbarians might not at their will, making sorties from there, more boldly do violence to the regions there; while he himself led the army against Urbiventus. 20.4. For Peranius urged him to this, since he had heard from the deserters that the Goths there were running short of provisions, and he hoped that, if in addition to the want of necessities they should see Belisarius present with the whole army, they would more easily give in; 20.5. which happened. For Belisarius, as soon as he came to Urbiventus, ordered all to encamp in a suitably situated place, while he himself, going about in a circle, looked to see whether somehow it might not be impossible to take it by force. And it seemed to him there was no device for taking the place by force. 20.6. But it seemed to him not altogether impracticable to gain mastery of it secretly through a plot. 20.7. For a certain hill rises alone out of a hollow land, level and flat at the upper parts, but precipitous below. And about the hill rocks of equal height make a kind of encirclement, not being very close, but distant about a stone's throw. 20.8. On this hill the men of old built the city, neither throwing walls around it nor making any other fortification, since the place seemed to them impregnable by nature. 20.9. For there happens to be one entrance to it from the rocks, which, if the inhabitants guard it, they need not fear any assault of the enemy elsewhere. 20.10. For apart from the place where nature has made the entrance to the city, as has been said, a river, always great and impassable, holds the space between the hill and the rocks of which I just made mention. 20.11. Wherefore the ancient Romans made a small building at this entrance. And there is a gate there, which the Goths then guarded. Such, then, is the situation regarding Urbiventus. 20.12. And Belisarius set himself with the whole army to the siege, having the hope either of doing them mischief by means of the river or of reducing the enemy by famine. 20.13. And the barbarians for a while were not altogether in want of the necessities, although they had them in less than sufficient measure; nevertheless they held out against the hardship beyond expectation, by no means coming to fullness of food, but using just so much of victuals each day as not to die of famine. 20.14. But when all the provisions had failed them, they soaked hides and skins in water for a long time and then ate them; for Albilas who commanded them, a man most highly esteemed among the Goths, fed them with empty hopes. 20.15. And when the season, coming round again, brought the summer time, the grain was already at its height in the fields of its own accord, not so much, however, as before, but much less. 20.16. For since it had been hidden in the furrows neither by ploughs nor by the hands of men, but happened to lie on the surface, the earth was able to bring only a small part of it to fruit. 20.17. And as no one reaped it, having come far past its prime it fell again, and nothing thereafter grew from it. And this same thing happened also in Aemilia. 20.18. Wherefore, leaving their homes, the people there went to Picenum, supposing that the regions there, because they were by the sea, would not be altogether oppressed by want. 20.19. And the famine laid hold of the Tuscans too none the less, from the same cause, but all of them who dwelt in the mountains, grinding the acorns of the oaks like grain and making bread from them, ate it. 20.20. And of all kinds of diseases, as was natural, most of them were seized, 20.21. but some, surviving, were saved. In Picenum, however, it is said that no fewer than fifty thousand of the people, Roman farmers, perished of famine, and many more still beyond the Ionian Gulf. 20.22. And of what appearance they became, and in what manner they died, I now proceed to tell, having myself been an eyewitness. 20.23. They became thin and pale, all of them; for the flesh, lacking nourishment, fed, according to the ancient saying, upon itself, and the bile, which now held the mastery over the body by its abundance, gave to these a colour resembling its own. 20.24. And as the evil advanced, all moisture failed them, and the skin, becoming exceedingly dry, was very like leather, giving the appearance of being fixed upon the bones. And changing from livid to black, they resembled torches burnt exceedingly. 20.25. And their faces were always full of amazement, and they always looked terribly somewhat frenzied. And some died from want of food, others even from coming too much to fullness of it. 20.26. For since all the heat which nature kindled within them had been quenched, if anyone fed them to satiety, and not little by little, like infants newly born, these, no longer being able to digest the food, perished much more quickly. 20.27. And some, the famine overpowering them, tasted of one another. And it is said that two women in a certain field above the city of Ariminum ate seventeen men, who happened to be the only ones left in the place. 20.28. For it came about that strangers travelling there lodged in the cottage where these women dwelt; and them, while they slept, the women killed and ate. 20.29. They say, then, that the eighteenth stranger, rising from sleep, just when these women were about to set upon him, leapt up and learned from them the whole story, and killed both of them. 20.30. This, then, they say happened thus. And most, gripped by the necessity of famine, if anywhere any herb chanced to be, would go to it with much eagerness, and kneeling down would try to pull it up from the ground. 20.31. Then (for they were not able, since all strength had failed them) falling over the herb and their hand, they died. 20.32. And no one hid them anywhere in the earth; for there was no one to whom even an account of burial might fall; yet no bird touched them, of which many are wont to feed on dead bodies, 20.33. since they had nothing on which they might rush. For all the flesh, as I have said before, had already happened to be consumed by the famine. Such, then, was the state of things regarding the famine.

[129448] 21.1. And Belisarius, when he heard that Uraïas and the barbarians were besieging Mediolanum, sent Martinus and Uliaris with a large army against them. 21.2. And they, coming to the river Padus, which is distant a day's journey from Mediolanum, encamped and remained there. And much time was spent by them there, while they deliberated about the crossing of the river. 21.3. And when Mundilas heard this, he sent to them one of the Romans, Paulus by name. 21.4. And he, escaping the notice of the enemy, came to the bank of the Padus. And finding no boat at the moment, he stripped and, swimming, made the crossing at great risk. 21.5. So being brought to the Roman camp and coming to the commanders, he spoke as follows: “Martinus and Uliaris, you are doing things neither just nor worthy of your own reputation, having come in word for the safety of the emperor's affairs, but in deed increasing the power of the Goths. 21.6. For this Mediolanum, which surpasses by far all the cities in Italy in size and in population and in the rest of its prosperity, and apart from these things, being a bulwark against the Germans and the other barbarians and set, so to speak, before the whole Roman empire, has fallen into great danger together with Mundilas and the emperor's army, being troubled by the enemy and neglected by you. 21.7. How greatly the emperor has been wronged by you at the present time, I forbear to say. For the occasion does not allow me to use many words, since I seek some swift help for the city, while there is still some hope left. 21.8. And I say that you ought immediately to defend the Mediolanenses who are in danger. For if you use any delay toward us at the present time, it will come about that we, having suffered the most bitter of all things, shall perish, and that you will have given over the emperor's power to the enemy. 21.9. For traitors, I think, are justly called not only those who throw open the gates to the enemy, but no less, if not more, those who, when it is in their power to defend their dearest who are besieged, choose the safe shrinking instead of the contest and thus give the mastery to the enemy, as is natural.” 21.10. Paulus said so much, and Martinus and Uliaris, promising to follow him immediately, sent the man away. 21.11. And he, again escaping the notice of the barbarians, entered Mediolanum by night, and lifting up the soldiers and all the Romans with hopes, strengthened them still more in their loyalty to the emperor. 21.12. None the less, Martinus and his men, gripped by shrinking, remained there, and much time was spent in this delay. 21.13. And afterward Martinus, wishing to free himself from blame, wrote to Belisarius as follows: “You sent us here to defend those in Mediolanum who are in danger, and we with much eagerness, just as you ordered, have come as far as the river Padus, which the army fears to cross, since we hear that there is a great force of Goths and a very great multitude of Burgundians with them in Liguria, against whom we think ourselves unable to contend alone. 21.14. But order John and Justinus as quickly as possible (for they are in our neighbourhood in the land of Aemilia), together with their followers, to share this danger with us. 21.15. For going from here in common, we shall both be safe ourselves and be able to do some harm to the 21.16. enemy.” So much Martinus's letter made plain. And Belisarius, when he had read it, ordered John and Justinus to go quickly to Mediolanum with Martinus and his men. But they said they would do nothing unless Narses gave them instructions. 21.17. Wherefore Belisarius wrote also to Narses as follows: “Consider the whole army of the emperor to be one body, which, if it does not display one purpose, like the limbs of a man, but wishes to act apart from one another, it will be left to us to perish, having accomplished none of the things that are needful. 21.18. Therefore let Aemilia alone, which has neither any fortification nor brings to the Romans at the present time any weight of advantage. 21.19. But order John and Justinus immediately to go straight against the enemy in Mediolanum together with Martinus and his men, being near and sufficient for the mastery over the barbarians. 21.20. For I have here no multitude of an army which I might send, and besides, I think it inexpedient for soldiers to go to Mediolanum from here. 21.21. For much time will be wasted, so that they will come to it later than the necessary opportunity, and they will by no means be able to use their horses against the enemy, on account of the length of the road, when they come to them. 21.22. But if these men go to Mediolanum together with Martinus and Uliaris, they will both prevail, as is likely, over the barbarians there, and will recover Aemilia again, with no one any longer withstanding them.” 21.23. When Narses saw this letter brought to him, he instructed John and Justinus to go to Mediolanum with the rest of the army. 21.24. And a little later John was sent to the coast in order to bring boats from there, so that the army might be able to cross the river. But a sickness befalling him prevented the carrying out of the plan. 21.25. While Martinus and his men were using their shrinking from the crossing, and John's men were waiting for the instructions of Narses, in the meantime it befell that a great deal of time was spent in the siege. 21.26. And the besieged, now exceedingly hard pressed by famine and the evil overpowering them, most of them tasted of dogs and mice and of other animals which never came to the food of man. 21.27. So the barbarians, sending envoys to Mundilas, ordered him to give over the city to them, on condition that he himself and the soldiers should remain unharmed. 21.28. And he agreed to do this, if those men should both give pledges on their behalf and that they would do nothing unpleasant to any of the inhabitants. 21.29. But when the enemy gave pledges to Mundilas and the soldiers, while it was clear that, being gripped by much anger against the Ligurians, they would destroy them all, Mundilas, calling together all the soldiers, spoke as follows: 21.30. “If ever any others, when it was in their power to live shamefully, chose rather to die gloriously, exchanging an honourable end of life for immediate safety, such men at the present time I should wish you also to be, and not, through love of life, to pursue a life with shame, and this too, from the teaching of Belisarius, in which, having shared from of old, it is unholy for you not to be noble and exceedingly bold. 21.31. For to all who come into the light one fortune comes forth, to die at the appointed times; but in the manner of death men for the most part differ from one another. 21.32. And the difference is this, that cowards, as is natural, all owing insult and mockery to their enemies first, then none the less fulfil their fate at the times appointed from above, but to noble men it befalls to suffer this together with valour and an abundance of good repute. 21.33. And apart from these things, if it were in our power, while saving these people, to be slaves to the barbarians, this at least would bring some pardon for our shameful safety. 21.34. But if it is necessary to behold so great a multitude of Romans destroyed by the hands of the enemy, what would one call more bitter than this death? 21.35. For we should seem to be doing nothing else than helping the barbarians to accomplish the terrible deed. While, therefore, we are still masters of ourselves, let us adorn necessity with valour, and account the fortune that has fallen upon us a glorious one. 21.36. And I say that we ought, all of us being armed as best we can, to go against the enemy who are not expecting it. 21.37. For one of two things will be ours: either to have wrought through fortune something better than hope, or, having met with a happy end, to be most gloriously released from our present evils.” 21.38. Mundilas said so much, but none of the soldiers was willing to undertake the danger, but on the terms which the enemy proposed they gave over both themselves and the city. 21.39. And the barbarians, doing them no harm, kept them in custody together with Mundilas, but razed the city to the ground, killing all the males, no fewer than three hundred thousand, and making the women into the account of slaves, whom they gave to the Burgundians, repaying them thus for their alliance. 21.40. And finding Reparatus, the prefect of the court, they cut him into small pieces and threw his flesh to the dogs. 21.41. But Cervatinus (for he happened to be inside Mediolanum) was conveyed through Venetia and the nations there, together with his followers, to Dalmatia. And he came from there to the emperor, announcing this great disaster which had befallen the Romans. 21.42. And from this the Goths took the other cities by agreement which happened to have Roman garrisons, and again held the mastery over the whole of Liguria. And Martinus and Uliaris with the army returned to Rome.

[138497] 22.1. These things, then, were thus. And Belisarius, who had not yet learned anything of what had happened in Liguria, when the winter was already ending, went with the whole army into Picenum. 22.2. And learning on this journey what had befallen Mediolanum, he made great mourning. 22.3. And he no longer permitted Uliaris to come into his sight for the future, but wrote to the emperor all that had happened. 22.4. But the emperor on account of these things did harm to no one, but, hearing of the disagreement between Belisarius and Narses, immediately summoned Narses and established Belisarius as supreme commander of the whole war. 22.5. Thus Narses returned to Byzantium, leading some few of the soldiers. But the Eruli no longer saw fit to remain in Italy when Narses withdrew from there, although Belisarius promised them that many good things would come to them from himself and from the emperor if they remained, but packing up they all first withdrew to Liguria. 22.6. There, falling in with the army of Uraïas, they sold to the enemy the slaves and other animals which they were bringing, and having received much money, they swore an oath never to array themselves against the Goths or to come to close quarters with them. 22.7. And thus making their withdrawal peaceful, they came to the regions about Venetia. There, joining Vitalius, they repented of their offences against the emperor Justinian. 22.8. And clearing themselves of the charge, they left Visandus, one of their commanders, with his followers there, while all the rest were conveyed to Byzantium, Aluith leading them and Philimuth, who, when Phanitheus had died at Caesena, held the command. 22.9. And Wittigis and the Goths with him, hearing that Belisarius would come against them and Ravenna at the beginning of spring, were in great fear and deliberated about their present situation; and after much deliberation (for they did not think themselves a match for the enemy by themselves) it seemed best to them to bring in the help of certain other barbarians. 22.10. From the Germans, then, having already had experience of their treachery and faithlessness, they abstained, being content if those men should not themselves come with Belisarius against them, 22.11. but should stand out of the way of both. And to the ruler of the Lombards, Vaces, they sent envoys, offering great sums of money and inviting them to alliance. 22.12. But these, when they learned that Vaces was a friend and ally to the emperor, withdrew without accomplishing anything. 22.13. So Wittigis, being at a loss before the present situation, as was natural, kept calling together many of the elders. From these he inquired frequently what course, in deliberating and acting, would make his affairs better. 22.14. So many opinions were spoken by those who came together for the deliberation, some in no way suited to the present situation, but others bringing something even worthy of account. 22.15. Among which this also came into the discussion, that never before had an emperor of the Romans been able to make war on the barbarians in the West, except indeed when a truce had been made with the Persians. 22.16. For both the Vandals and the Moors had then been destroyed, and the present disasters had befallen the Goths. So that, if anyone now also should set the king of the Medes against the emperor Justinian, the Romans, this nation being made hostile to them, would never thereafter be able to carry on another war against any men. 22.17. This pleased both Wittigis himself and the other Goths. It was resolved, therefore, to send envoys to Chosroes the king of the Medes—not Goths, however, in order that, becoming evident at once from this, they might not confound the matter, but Romans, who should make him hostile to the emperor Justinian. 22.18. Wherefore they persuaded with much money two of the priests in Liguria to this service. 22.19. Of whom the one, who seemed the more worthy, putting on the appearance and name of a bishop, though it in no way belonged to him, was appointed to the embassy, while the other followed him as his attendant. 22.20. And putting into their hands a letter written to Chosroes, Wittigis sent them. By these Chosroes, being moved, wrought irreparable deeds against the Romans during a truce, as I have related in the previous narrative. 22.21. But when the emperor Justinian heard that Chosroes and the Persians were deliberating these things, he resolved to bring the war in the West to an end as quickly as possible and to recall Belisarius, that he might march against the Persians. 22.22. So the envoys of Wittigis (for they happened to be still in Byzantium) he immediately sent away, promising that men would be sent by him to Ravenna who should make the truce with the Goths in whatever way might prove advantageous to both. 22.23. But Belisarius did not release these envoys to the enemy before they too released Athanasius and Peter and their men. 22.24. And when these had come to Byzantium, the emperor deemed them worthy of the greatest honours, establishing Athanasius as prefect of the praetorian guards in Italy, and granting to Peter the office of the so-called Master. 22.25. And the winter was ending, and the fourth year of this war which Procopius has written was coming to a close.

[143809] 23.1. And Belisarius wished first to take Auximus and Fisula, and thus to go against Wittigis and Ravenna, no enemy being any longer able either to stand in his way or to do mischief behind him. 23.2. So Cyprianus and Justinus with their followers and some of the Isaurians he sent to Fisula, and five hundred foot-soldiers from the regiment which Demetrius commanded, who, encamping about the fort, besieged the barbarians there. 23.3. And Martinus and John with their followers and another army, which John commanded—whom they called also Phagas—he sent to the river Padus. 23.4. And he ordered them to take care that Uraïas and his men should not come against them from Mediolanum, but if they should not be able to repel the assault of the enemy, to follow them secretly behind and come upon their rear. 23.5. And they, occupying the city of Dorthona, which is by the river and without a wall, encamped and remained there, while he himself came to the city of Auximus, having eleven thousand men. 23.6. Now this Auximus is the first of the cities in Picenum, which the Romans have been accustomed to call its capital. And it is distant about eighty-four stades from the shore of the Ionian Gulf, and from the city of Ravenna a journey of three days and eighty stades. 23.7. And it lies on a certain high hill, having no entrance on the level ground, and from this it is altogether inaccessible to the enemy. 23.8. There Wittigis had established as garrison whatever was of repute among the Goths, judging that the Romans, if they did not first take this, would never dare to march against Ravenna. 23.9. And when the Roman army came to Auximus, Belisarius ordered all to encamp in a circle about the lower parts of the hill. 23.10. And they, gathering in companies, pitched their huts here and there about the place; but the Goths, observing them to be at a long distance from one another and not easily able to come to one another's aid, since it was a great plain, suddenly about late afternoon advanced against the enemy, toward the parts to the east of the city, where Belisarius happened to be still making a camp with his guards and shield-bearers. 23.11. And they, taking up their arms from what was at hand, defended themselves against the assailants, and very easily thrusting them back by their valour, routed them, and pursuing the fugitives came to the middle of the hill. 23.12. There the barbarians, turning about and trusting in the strength of the place, stood facing the pursuers, and shooting them from above killed many, until night, coming on, prevented them. So, separating, both sides bivouacked that night. 23.13. Now it happened that on the day before this action some of the Goths had been sent at deep dawn to the regions there for the gathering of provisions. 23.14. And these, having learned nothing about the presence of the enemy, returned at nightfall. And suddenly catching sight of the fires of the Romans, they came into great wonder and fear. 23.15. And many of them, daring to undertake the danger and escaping the notice of the enemy, entered Auximus. But all who, being terrified, immediately hid themselves in certain woods, in order to go to Ravenna, these, not long afterward coming under the power of their opponents, were destroyed. 23.16. And Belisarius, observing that Auximus was most strong in its safe position, and that it was altogether impossible for him to make an assault on the wall, thought he would never take the place by force, but he had hope, by a strict siege, of bringing the enemy into want of necessities and of making them subject in time. 23.17. Not far from the wall the earth, sending up much herbage, brought every day an occasion of engagement to the Romans and the Goths. 23.18. For when the Romans saw their opponents always cutting this for the sake of their horses, going up onto the hill at a run, and coming to close quarters with the enemy, and displaying deeds worthy of valour, they by no means allowed them to carry off the herbage, and always destroyed many of their opponents in this place. 23.19. And the barbarians, being worsted by the valour of the enemy, devised the following. Taking off the wheels of the wagons with the axles alone, they kept them in readiness, and beginning to cut the herbage, when they saw the Romans now coming up into the middle of the hill, they let the wheels go to rush down upon them from the height. 23.20. But by some chance it befell that these wheels came down to the level ground without touching any man. So having failed in this attempt, the barbarians for the moment fled inside the wall, but afterward did as follows. 23.21. Setting an ambush of their notable men in the ravines which are close to the wall, a few of them showed themselves to the enemy about the herbage; and when the battle was at close quarters, those who were hidden, leaping out of the ambushes, both far surpassing their opponents in numbers and dismaying them because they had not perceived it beforehand, killed very many and always turned the rest to flight. 23.22. And those of the Romans who stood in the camps saw the enemy rising from the ambushes, but, calling their comrades with much shouting, accomplished nothing, since the combatants by no means heard their cry, being separated from them by the length of the hill, and the barbarians always on purpose clashing their arms against them. 23.23. And while Belisarius was at a loss before the present situation, Procopius, who wrote this, coming to him, said: “Those who anciently used trumpets in the Roman army, O general, understood two notes, of which the one resembled most a command bidding the soldiers rush to battle, while the other called the combatants back to the camp, whenever this seemed best to the general. 23.24. And in this way the generals always ordered the soldiers what was fitting, and they were able to carry out the deeds enjoined. 23.25. For a shout in engagements is in no way able to signify anything clearly, since a din, as is natural, resounds everywhere, and fear confounds the senses of the combatants. 23.26. But since at the present time, through ignorance, such an art has slipped away, and it is impossible with a single trumpet to make both signals clear, do you henceforth do as follows. 23.27. With the cavalry trumpets command the soldiers to fight the enemy, and with the infantry trumpets call the men back to the retreat. 23.28. For it is impossible for them not to understand each of the two sounds, since the one comes from leather and exceedingly thin wood, the other from some thick bronze.” 23.29. Procopius said so much. And Belisarius was pleased with the suggestion, and calling the whole army together, spoke as follows: “Eagerness for this work I think both advantageous and worthy of much praise, so long as, being moderate, it brings no harm to those who have it. 23.30. For by excess all good things are wont always to turn to the worse. Do you, then, no longer be tripped up by contentiousness; for there is no shame in fleeing one who is doing mischief. 23.31. But he who goes unconsidered into manifest evil and is saved from it, if so it chance, incurs the charge of folly; but he is noble who plays the man in necessities. 23.32. The barbarians, then, since they are not able to fight us in the open, undertake to destroy us by ambushes. But for us to undertake the danger is more blameworthy than to escape their ambush. 23.33. For there is nothing more shameful than to yield to the designs of the enemy. For me, then, it will be a care that you do not fall into the ambushes of the enemy. 23.34. But it would be your task, whenever I give the signal, to go quickly to the retreat. And this signal for you will be from the infantry trumpet, O soldiers.” Belisarius said so much. 23.35. And the soldiers, observing the enemy about the herbage, went against them at a run, and killed some of them in the first charge. 23.36. Among whom one of the Moors, seeing one wearing gold, and laying hold of the hairs on his head in order to strip him, kept dragging the corpse. 23.37. But a certain Goth, hitting him with a javelin and chancing to strike both the muscles which are behind the shins, by the fixing of the javelin bound both his feet together. 23.38. But none the less the Moor, holding to the hairs, kept dragging the corpse. And in the meantime the barbarians set their ambushes in motion, and Belisarius, seeing from the camp what was being done, ordered the infantry whose task this was to sound their trumpets quickly. 23.39. And the Romans, perceiving this, now little by little began to withdraw, taking up the Moor with the javelin. And the Goths no longer dared to follow them, but withdrew without accomplishing anything.

[152572] 24.1. As time went on and the want of provisions among the barbarians advanced greatly, they deliberated to report their situation to Wittigis. 24.2. And since none of them dared to set out on this task (for they thought they would never escape the notice of the besiegers), they devised the following. 24.3. Watching for a moonless night and making ready the men whom they purposed to send to Wittigis, and putting letters into their hands, when it was far into the night, they all shouted out prodigiously at many parts of the wall. 24.4. One would have guessed that they had fallen into confusion, the enemy pressing hard upon them and the city being taken contrary to expectation; 24.5. but the Romans, being altogether unable to conjecture what was happening, by the resolve of Belisarius remained quiet in the camps, suspecting both that there would be some plot from the city and that an army had come from Ravenna to the aid of the enemy and was coming against them. Fearing these things, they thought it better for them to be saved by remaining quiet in safety than to go into manifest danger on a moonless night. 24.6. So thus the barbarians, escaping the notice of the enemy, sent the men to Ravenna. And they, coming into the sight of no enemy man, reached Wittigis on the third day and showed the letters. 24.7. And the writing made plain the following: “When, O king, you established us in the garrison at Auximus, you said that you were depositing in our keeping the keys both of Ravenna itself and of your kingdom. 24.8. Wherefore you charged us to guard with all our might, in order that we, on our part, might not give over the power of the Goths to the enemy, and you affirmed to us when we begged that you would come to our aid of your own accord with the whole army. 24.9. We, then, until now, fighting against both famine and Belisarius, have become faithful guardians of your kingdom, but you have by no means resolved to help us in any way whatever. 24.10. Consider, therefore, lest the Romans, taking Auximus and taking up the keys, which you here neglect though they lie before you, shut you off from all your possessions for the future.” So much the letter made plain. 24.11. And when Wittigis saw it brought to him, for the moment he sent the men back, promising that he would come to the aid of Auximus with the whole army of the Goths; but after much consideration he remained quiet. 24.12. For he both suspected that John and his men would follow behind and place them between two enemies, and thinking that there was a great force of warlike men with Belisarius, he fell into helpless fear. 24.13. But most of all the famine disturbed him, since he had nowhere from which he might furnish provisions for the army. 24.14. For the Romans, holding mastery of the sea and possessing the fort at Ancon, depositing all the necessities there from both Sicily and Calabria, easily brought them from there in due season. 24.15. But to the Goths, if they should march into the land of Picenum, perceiving that there would be no means of provisions, he fell into helplessness. 24.16. So the men who had been sent from Auximus to Wittigis brought back his promise, escaping the notice of the enemy, to Auximus, and strengthened the barbarians there with empty hopes. 24.17. And Belisarius, hearing these things from the deserters, ordered the guard to be kept still more strictly, in order that nothing of this kind might happen again. These things, then, were thus. 24.18. And Cyprianus and Justinus, besieging Fisula, were by no means able either to assault the wall or to go anywhere near it; for this fort was hard to approach from all sides. And as the barbarians often sallied out against them, being more willing to settle matters with them by battle than to be hard pressed by the want of necessities, the engagements at first were evenly matched; but afterward the Romans, now having the advantage, shut the enemy up within the wall and kept them safely guarded, 24.19. so that no one could go anywhere from there. So the barbarians, being in want of the necessities and at a loss before the present situation, sent secretly to Wittigis, begging him to help them as quickly as possible, since they could no longer hold out for any longer time. 24.20. And Wittigis ordered Uraïas with the army in Liguria to go to Ticinum; for thus he himself affirmed that he would come to the aid of the besieged with the whole power of the Goths. 24.21. And he did accordingly, and moving all the army with him, went to Ticinum; and crossing the river Padus they came somewhere near the camp of the Romans. 24.22. There they too encamped and sat facing the enemy, distant from them about sixty stades. And neither side began hostilities. 24.23. For to the Romans it seemed sufficient if they should stand in the way of their opponents, so as to prevent them from going against the besiegers; and the barbarians shrank from fighting the enemy there, reckoning that, if they should fail in this engagement, they would destroy all the affairs of the Goths. 24.24. For they would no longer be able, by joining Wittigis, to help the besieged together with him. With such a purpose both sides remained quiet.

[157790] 25.1. In the meantime the Franks, hearing that both Goths and Romans had been worn down by the war, and thinking that on this account they would most easily win for themselves the most of Italy, made it a grievous thing that, while others carried on a war for so long a time about the rule of a land so near them, they themselves, remaining quiet, should stand out of the way of both. 25.2. So, immediately forgetting their oaths and agreements which a little before they had made both with the Romans and with the Goths (for this nation is the most faithless of all men in matters of good faith), gathering at once to the number of a hundred thousand, with Theudibert leading them, they marched into Italy, having some few horsemen about the leader, 25.3. who alone bore spears, while the rest, all foot-soldiers, had neither bows nor spears, but each bore a sword and a shield and one axe. The iron of this was thick and exceedingly sharp on both sides, while the wooden handle was very short. 25.4. This axe they are accustomed always to throw at a single signal in the first charge, and to break the shields of the enemy and to kill the men. 25.5. Thus the Franks, crossing the Alps which separate the Gauls and the Italians, came into Liguria. 25.6. And the Goths, who had before been distressed at their faithlessness, because, though promising for alliance both much land and great sums of money, the Franks had often by no means been willing to fulfil the promise, when they heard that Theudibert was present with a large army, rejoiced, being lifted up in their hopes to the highest degree, and thought they would for the future prevail over the enemy without a battle. 25.7. And the Germans, so long as they were in Liguria, did nothing unpleasant to the Goths, in order that no hindrance to the crossing of the Padus might come to them from these men. 25.8. But when they came to the city of Ticinum, where the ancient Romans had built a bridge over this river, those who guarded it both served them in other ways and let them cross the Padus at their will. 25.9. And laying hold of the bridge, the Franks sacrificed the children and women of the Goths whom they found there and threw their bodies into the river as the first-fruits of the war. 25.10. For these barbarians, although they have become Christians, preserve the most of their ancient belief, using sacrifices of human beings and other unholy offerings, and by this they make their divinations. 25.11. And the Goths, observing what was being done, fell into helpless fear and, gripped by flight, got inside the wall. So the Germans, having crossed the river Padus, came to the camp of the Goths, and the Goths at first gladly watched them coming against them in small numbers, supposing that the men had come for their alliance. 25.12. But when a great throng of Germans poured in and set to work and, hurling their axes, were already doing much harm, the Goths, turning their backs, rushed into flight, and going through the camp of the Romans, ran toward Ravenna. 25.13. And the Romans, seeing them fleeing, supposed that Belisarius had come to their aid and had taken the camp of the enemy and was driving them out, having conquered in battle. Wishing to join him, they took up their arms and went quickly. 25.14. But falling unexpectedly upon an army of the enemy, they came to close quarters by no means willingly, and being defeated by far in the battle, they were no longer able to return to the camp, but all fled toward Tuscany. 25.15. And when they were now in safety, they reported all that had befallen them to Belisarius. 25.16. And the Franks, having conquered both, as has been said, and taken both camps altogether destitute of men, for the moment found provisions there, but in a short time having consumed all because of their multitude, they had nothing else to bring to themselves in a land destitute of men, except oxen and the water of the Padus. 25.17. And being unable to digest this meat with the abundance of water, most of them were seized by flux of the stomach and the disease of dysentery, from which they were by no means able to free themselves through the want of provisions. 25.18. They say, then, that the third part of the Frankish army perished in this way. Wherefore, being by no means able to go forward, they remained where they were. 25.19. And Belisarius, hearing both that the army of the Franks was present and that Martinus and John, defeated in battle, had fled, fell into helplessness, fearing both for the whole army and especially for those who were besieging at Fisula, since he learned that these barbarians were especially closer to them. He immediately wrote to Theudibert as follows: 25.20. “For a man who lays claim to virtue not to be false, especially the ruler of nations so many in number, I think, O noble Theudibert, 25.21. is not seemly. But to disregard, by wronging oaths written down, the agreements would not befit even the most dishonoured of men. These things you yourself at the present time know that you are doing wrong, although you agreed lately that you would help us in this war against the Goths. 25.22. But now you have not only stood out of the way of both, but have also so unconsideredly taken up arms and come against us. Do not you, most excellent man, thus insult a great emperor, who, I dare say, is not likely to requite the insult except in the greatest of measures. 25.23. And it is better for one to hold his own possessions in safety than, laying claim to what does not belong to him, to set himself into some danger concerning the most necessary things.” 25.24. When Theudibert had read this letter, being now both at a loss before the present situation and reproached by the Germans because they were dying from no cause in a deserted land, breaking up, he withdrew to his home with the survivors of the Franks with much speed.

[163683] 26.1. Thus Theudibert, having marched into Italy, made his departure. And Martinus and John returned none the less, in order that the enemy might not make some assault upon those who were besieging. 26.2. But the Goths in Auximus, having learned nothing about the coming of the Franks, and despairing of the hope from Ravenna which was thus delaying, again purposed to call Wittigis to witness, but being unable to escape the guard of the enemy, they mourned. 26.3. After this, seeing one of the Romans, a Bessian by race, Burcentius by name, ranged under Narses the Armenian, alone keeping guard at midday, lest anyone from the city should go out to take the herbage, they came to a parley with him, coming nearer, and giving pledges that they would do him no harm, they bade him join them, promising that great sums of money would come to him from them. 26.4. And when they had come to the same place, the barbarians begged the man to convey a certain letter to Ravenna, fixing for him at once a stated sum of gold, and promising to give more when the letters of Wittigis should come back to them. 26.5. And the soldier, persuaded by the money, agreed to perform this and fulfilled the promise. So taking the sealed letters, he came quickly to Ravenna. And coming into the sight of Wittigis, he put the letter into his hands. 26.6. And it made plain the following: “In what condition our present affairs are you will know clearly by inquiring who in the world is the conveyer of the letter. 26.7. For it is impossible for a Goth to get outside the wall. And the most accessible of our foods is the herbage by the wall, of which we cannot now even touch as much as a little, unless we lose many in the struggle for it. To what end these things will come for us you and the Goths in Ravenna must consider.” 26.8. When Wittigis had read this, he answered as follows: “Let no one think, O dearest of all men, that we have given up, nor that we have come to such a degree of baseness as to throw away the affairs of the Goths through carelessness. 26.9. For lately the preparation for the sally had been made by me as fully as possible, and Uraïas, summoned from Mediolanum with the whole army, had come. 26.10. But the assault of the Franks, falling upon us unexpectedly, has checked all our preparations, for which I would not justly bear the blame. 26.11. For whatever is greater than human power both grants to those who have failed to be free from blame, fortune always drawing to herself the charges arising from what has been done. 26.12. Now, however (for we hear that Theudibert has been removed from our path), we shall be with you not long hence, God willing, with the whole army of the Goths. 26.13. And you ought to bear what befalls you both manfully and in a way suited to the necessity, reckoning your valour, for the sake of which I selected you from all and established you in Auximus, and respecting the reputation in which all the Goths, holding you in regard, have set you forth as a defence both of Ravenna 26.14. and of their own safety.” Having written so much, Wittigis presented the man with much money and sent him away. And he, coming to Auximus, went to his comrades, and pretending that some sickness had befallen him, and that on this account he had been idling at a certain church not far off, he again took his station at the watch where he was wont, and, escaping the notice of all, gave the letters to the enemy; which, being read aloud to the multitude, strengthened all still more, 26.15. although they were hard pressed by famine. Wherefore they were by no means willing to come over to Belisarius, who was constantly trying to win them. And again (for no army was reported to have come out from Ravenna for them, and they were now prodigiously distressed by the want of necessities) they sent Burcentius once more, making plain only this in the letter, that within five days they were no longer able to fight against the famine. And he returned to them again, having a letter of Wittigis that buoyed them up with the same hopes. 26.16. But the Romans, none the less distressed because they had made the siege so long in a deserted land, were at a loss, seeing the barbarians not giving in to them amid so many evils. 26.17. Wherefore Belisarius was eager to take alive one of the notable men among the enemy, in order that he might learn for what reason the barbarians were enduring these terrors, and Valerianus agreed to perform such a thing for him easily. 26.18. For he said there were some among his followers of the nation of the Sclaveni, who are accustomed to hide themselves behind a small stone or any chance bush and snatch up an enemy man. 26.19. And this they always display by the river Ister, where they are settled, both against the Romans and against the other barbarians. Belisarius was pleased with the proposal and ordered him to attend to the matter quickly. 26.20. So Valerianus, selecting one of the Sclaveni who was well off in greatness of body and exceedingly energetic, instructed him to bring an enemy man, affirming that great rewards would be his from Belisarius. 26.21. And he said he would do this easily in the place where the herbage happens to be; for the Goths for a long time, in want of necessities, were feeding there. 26.22. So the Sclavenian, coming at deep dawn close to the wall, hiding himself in a certain bush and drawing his body into a small space, lay concealed about the herbage. 26.23. And at daybreak a Goth came there and quickly began gathering the plants, suspecting nothing unpleasant from the bush, but looking frequently toward the camp of the enemy, lest anyone should come from there against him. 26.24. And the Sclavenian, falling upon him suddenly from behind, snatched him up, gripping the man firmly with both hands around the middle, and carried him into the camp, and bringing him put him into the hands of Valerianus. 26.25. And when he inquired in whom the Goths were trusting and what stronghold they had that they were by no means willing to come over to them, but willingly held out against the most terrible things, the barbarian told the whole story about Burcentius and convicted him to his face when he came into his sight. 26.26. And Burcentius, when he perceived that he had already become known, hid nothing of what had been done. Wherefore Belisarius gave him over to his comrades to deal with as they wished, and they not long afterward burnt him alive, the enemy watching what was being done. Thus Burcentius reaped the fruit of his love of money.

[170249] 27.1. And Belisarius, seeing that the barbarians were holding out against the hardship none the less, purposed to set the plot against the water, thinking that in this way he would take the enemy more easily and with less trouble. 27.2. And, since there was a certain spring of Auximus toward the north wind in a precipitous place, distant about a stone's throw from the wall, which let its stream little by little into a certain cistern there from of old, and the cistern, having become full from this small inflow, allowed those in Auximus to draw water with no toil—a thought came to Belisarius that, if the water did not collect there, the barbarians, being shot at by the enemy, would never be able in a long time to fill their jars from the inflow. 27.3. Wishing, then, to destroy the cistern, he devised the following. Arming the whole army, and drawing it up in a circle about the wall as if for battle, he gave his opponents the impression that he was about immediately to assault the wall from all sides. 27.4. Wherefore the Goths, dreading the assault, remained quiet on the battlements, intending to defend themselves from there against the enemy. 27.5. In the meantime Belisarius brought into the cistern five Isaurians experienced in building, with axes and other tools suited for the cutting of stones, hidden by a multitude of shields, and ordered them to destroy and break up the walls with all their might quickly. 27.6. And the barbarians for a while suspected that the men were going against the wall, and kept quiet, in order that, when they should come very close, they might shoot them easily, having no thought of what was being done; but when they saw the Isaurians inside the cistern, they hurled all their stones and arrows at them. 27.7. Then all the rest of the Romans withdrew at a run, but the five Isaurians alone, when they had got into safety, set to work; for a vault had been made over the water for the sake of shade by the men of old there. 27.8. And getting inside this, they took no heed of the enemy, although they shot many missiles. 27.9. Wherefore the Goths no longer endured to remain inside the wall, but opening the postern there, all went against the Isaurians, gripped by much anger and tumult. 27.10. And the Romans, Belisarius urging them on, met them with much eagerness. So a fierce battle took place, having for a long time a shoving and much slaughter on both sides. 27.11. But of the Romans more in number fell. For the barbarians, defending themselves from above, few though they were, overpowered many, and prevailing in the shoving, killed more than they themselves died. 27.12. The Romans, however, were by no means willing to give in, ashamed before Belisarius who was present and urging them on with shouting. 27.13. Then indeed a missile came at the belly of the general with a great rush, from one of the enemy, whether by some chance or by design shot at that point. 27.14. This Belisarius did not at all see. For he was able neither to guard against it nor to turn aside in any way. But a certain guard, Unigastus by name, standing by him, perceived it not far from the belly of Belisarius, and throwing forward his right hand, saved the general from the unexpected, but himself, struck by the arrow, immediately, being in great pain, withdrew. 27.15. And afterward, the tendons being cut, he was no longer able to use the hand. And the battle, having begun in the morning, came to midday. 27.16. And seven Armenian men, ranged under Narses and Aratius, displayed deeds worthy of valour, running about the difficult ground, which was most steep, as if on the level, and killing those of the enemy who always withstood them, until, thrusting back the barbarians there, they routed them. 27.17. And the rest of the Romans, seeing the enemy now giving in, pursued them, and the rout became brilliant, and the barbarians got inside the wall again. 27.18. So the Romans thought that the cistern had been destroyed and that the whole work had been accomplished by the Isaurians, but they had not been able to take away even so much as a pebble from there. 27.19. For the ancient craftsmen, taking the greatest care for valour in their works, had so wrought this structure too that it yielded neither to time nor to the plotting of men. 27.20. So the Isaurians, having accomplished nothing, when they saw the Romans masters of the place, departing from the cistern, withdrew to the camp. 27.21. Wherefore Belisarius ordered the soldiers to throw into the water the dead bodies of animals and herbs of a kind naturally sufficient for the destruction of man, and to throw in there and slake quicklime burnt most thoroughly (which they anciently were accustomed to call titanos, but now asbestos). 27.22. And they did so, but the barbarians during this time used a well inside the wall which had a very small amount of water, in less measure than their need. 27.23. And for the rest Belisarius was eager neither to take the place by force nor to display any plot concerning water or anything else, having the hope of prevailing over the enemy by famine alone. 27.24. And from this he most strongly attended to the guard. And the Goths, still waiting for the army from Ravenna, kept quiet in much want of the necessities. 27.25. And now the besieged in Fisula were most hard pressed by famine, and being unable to bear the evil, and despairing also of the hope from Ravenna, resolved to come over to their opponents. 27.26. So coming to a parley with Cyprianus and Justinus, and taking pledges concerning their lives, they surrendered both themselves and the fort by agreement. And Cyprianus and his men, taking them along with the Roman army, and establishing a sufficient garrison in Fisula, came to Auximus. 27.27. And Belisarius, constantly showing their commanders to the barbarians in Auximus, exhorted them to let go of their madness, shaking off the hope from Ravenna; for there would never be any profit for them either, but, after being most grievously worn down by hardship, they would none the less come to the same fortune as those in Fisula. 27.28. And they, after much deliberation among themselves, since they could no longer hold out against the famine, admitted his words and saw fit to surrender the city, on condition that they themselves should march to Ravenna unharmed with their goods. 27.29. Wherefore Belisarius was at a loss before the present situation, thinking it inexpedient that enemies of such valour and such numbers should mingle with those in Ravenna, but being by no means willing to be too late for the opportunity, and to go against Ravenna and Wittigis while affairs were still in suspense. 27.30. For the Franks disturbed him, being very likely to come immediately to the help of the Goths. Being eager to forestall their coming, he was unable to break off the siege while Auximus was not yet taken. 27.31. And the soldiers would not allow him to concede their goods to the barbarians, displaying the many wounds which they had received there from them, and enumerating all the toils that had befallen them in this siege, of which, they affirmed, the spoils of the conquered were the prizes. 27.32. At last both the Romans, compelled by the sharpness of the opportunity, and the Goths, forced by famine, came to an agreement with one another, on condition that the Romans should distribute half of the goods among themselves, and the Goths, keeping the remainder, should be subjects of the emperor. 27.33. Both, then, gave pledges concerning these things—the commanders of the Romans that the agreements should be binding, and the Goths that they would hide nothing whatever of the goods. 27.34. And thus they distributed all the goods, and the Romans held Auximus, and the barbarians mingled with the emperor's army.

[178064] 28.1. And when Belisarius had taken Auximus, he was eager to besiege Ravenna and led the whole army there. And sending Magnus with a large multitude beyond Ravenna, he ordered him always to go about the bank of the river Padus and keep guard, in order that for the future the Goths might not bring in provisions from there. 28.2. And Vitalius too, coming to him with an army from Dalmatia, guarded the other bank of the river. There befell them a certain chance, making manifest at once that fortune herself directs the affairs of both sides. 28.3. For the Goths, having before collected many boats in Liguria, had let them down into the Padus, intending, after filling them with grain and the other provisions, to sail to Ravenna. 28.4. But the water of this river at that time so failed that it was altogether impossible to sail upon it, until the Romans, coming up, took the boats with all their cargoes. 28.5. And the river not long afterward, returning to its proper flow, became navigable again for the rest of the time. And we know by report that this never happened to it before. 28.6. And now the barbarians began to be in want of provisions. For they could neither bring anything in through the Ionian Gulf, the enemy holding mastery of the sea everywhere, and they had been shut off from the river. 28.7. And the rulers of the Franks, perceiving what was being done, and wishing to make Italy their own, sent envoys to Wittigis, offering a promise of alliance, 28.8. on condition that they should rule the land together with him. When Belisarius heard this, he too sent envoys to oppose the Germans, among others Theodosius, who was set over his own household. 28.9. So first the envoys of the Germans, coming into the sight of Wittigis, spoke as follows: “The rulers of the Germans have sent us, being distressed because they have heard that you are being besieged by Belisarius, and being eager to help you as quickly as possible according to the alliance. 28.10. Now we think that an army of fighting men no fewer than fifty thousand has already crossed the Alps, with whom we boast that we shall overwhelm the whole Roman army with our axes in the first charge. 28.11. And you it behoves to follow in purpose not those who would enslave you, but those who set themselves to the danger of war out of goodwill toward the Goths. 28.12. And besides, if you take up arms with us, no hope will be left to the Romans of coming to close quarters with both armies, but from the very outset, with no toil, we shall bind on the mastery of the war. 28.13. But if the Goths array themselves with the Romans, not even so will they hold out against the nation of the Franks (for the contest will not be of equal forces), but it will be your lot to be defeated along with the bitterest enemies of all. 28.14. And to go to manifest evil, when it is possible to be saved free from danger, is much folly. And altogether the race of the Romans is faithless to all barbarians, since it is by nature hostile. 28.15. We, then, will rule the whole of Italy together with you, if you wish, and will administer the land in whatever way may seem best. But it is fitting for you and the Goths to choose whatever is going to be advantageous to you.” The Franks said so much. 28.16. And the envoys of Belisarius, coming forward, spoke as follows: “That the multitude of Germans, with which these men see fit to frighten you, will do no harm to the emperor's army, why should one speak at length among you, to whom, through long experience, it has befallen to know thoroughly every turn of war, and that valour is least wont to be inferior to a throng of men? 28.17. For we forbear to say that to the emperor above all it is possible to prevail over the enemy by a multitude of soldiers. And the good faith of these men, which they boast they use toward all barbarians, except indeed the Thuringians and the nation of the Burgundians, has been displayed by them toward you their allies. 28.18. For we would gladly ask the Franks by what god they intend to swear that they affirm they will give the assurance of good faith to you. 28.19. For the god already sworn by them, in what manner they have honoured him, you doubtless know; they who, having received such great sums of money, and all the Gauls, from you for alliance, not only by no means resolved to share the danger with you, but also took up arms so wantonly against you, if any account survives among you of what happened by the Padus. 28.20. And what need is there, by mentioning what is past, to convict the impiety of the Franks? Nothing could be more abominable than their present embassy. 28.21. For, as if having forgotten what they themselves agreed and swore by the treaties, they see fit to share everything in common with you. 28.22. And if they should obtain this too from you, to what end the insatiableness of their love of money will come for them, it is fitting to consider.” 28.23. So much the envoys of Belisarius also said. And Wittigis, after much consultation with the best of the Goths, chose the agreements with the emperor and dismissed the envoys of the Germans without accomplishing anything. And for the rest the Goths and Romans now treated with one another, but Belisarius none the less kept watch that the barbarians might not bring in provisions. 28.24. And he ordered Vitalius, going into Venetia, to bring in as much as possible of the places there, while he himself, sending Ildiger, guarded the Padus on both sides, in order that the barbarians might the more, through want of necessities, give in and make the agreements in the way he wished. 28.25. And when he learned that much grain was still stored in public buildings inside Ravenna, he persuaded with money one of the inhabitants there to set fire secretly to these buildings together with the grain. 28.26. And they say that these things were destroyed by the resolve of Matasuntha, the wife of Wittigis. And when the grain had been suddenly burnt, some suspected that the deed had been done by design, while others suspected that the place had been struck by lightning. 28.27. And reckoning both, the Goths and Wittigis fell into helplessness still more, no longer able to trust even themselves and thinking that they were being warred upon by God. These things, then, were thus done. 28.28. In the Alps which separate the Gauls and the Ligurians, which the Romans call the Cottian Alps, there happen to be many forts. 28.29. There Goths, many and excellent, settled from of old together with their wives and children, held the guard, whom, when Belisarius heard that they wished to come over to him, he sent one of his followers, Thomas by name, with some few men, in order that, giving pledges, they might win over the barbarians there by agreement. 28.30. And when they had come to the Alps, Sisigis, who commanded the watch-stations there, receiving them in one of the forts, both came over himself and induced each of the others to this. 28.31. In the meantime Uraïas, selecting four thousand from the Ligurians and from the forts in the Alps, went quickly to Ravenna to help. 28.32. And these, when they learned what Sisigis had done, fearing for their own families, 28.33. saw fit to go there first. Wherefore Uraïas went with the whole army to the Cottian Alps and besieged Sisigis together with Thomas and his men. And when John, the nephew of Vitalian, and Martinus learned this (for they happened to be very close to the river Padus), they quickly came to the aid with the whole army, and falling by a sudden raid upon some of the forts in the Alps, took them, and enslaved those who dwelt there, among whom it befell that there were many children and wives of those serving under Uraïas. 28.34. For very many of them, setting out from these forts, followed him. 28.35. And these, when they learned that their own families had been taken, deserting the camp of the Goths suddenly, resolved to come over to John and his men, and from this Uraïas was able neither to accomplish anything there nor to help the Goths in danger in Ravenna, but coming with a few men to Liguria without accomplishing anything, remained quiet. And Belisarius at his ease shut up Wittigis and the notable Goths in Ravenna.

[186312] 29.1. And then envoys came from the emperor, Domnicus and Maximinus, both of senatorial rank, in order to make peace on the following terms: 29.2. that Wittigis should carry off half of the royal wealth, and should rule the land which is beyond the river Padus; and that half of the goods should be the emperor's, and that he should make subject in the payment of tribute all that is within the river Padus. 29.3. And the envoys, showing the emperor's letters to Belisarius, were conveyed to Ravenna. And when the Goths and Wittigis had learned for what they had come, they gladly agreed to make the treaty on these terms. 29.4. When Belisarius heard this, he was distressed, regarding it as a great misfortune if anyone should not allow him, when it was possible with no toil, both to win the mastery of the whole war and to lead Wittigis a captive to Byzantium. 29.5. And when the envoys came to him from Ravenna, he was by no means willing to confirm the agreements with letters of his own. 29.6. The Goths, perceiving this, suspected that the Romans were offering them peace with treacherous intent, and used great suspicion toward them, and now declared openly that without the letters and oaths of Belisarius they would never make a treaty with them. 29.7. And Belisarius, hearing that some of the commanders reviled him as plotting against the emperor's affairs and being by no means willing to bring the war to an end, called all together, in the presence of Domnicus and Maximinus, and spoke as follows: 29.8. “That the fortune of war stands least of all in safety, I myself know, and I think that each of you knows the same as I about it. 29.9. For the hope of victory, when it is most expected to come to them, has tripped up many, and to those who seemed to have been unfortunate among their enemies it has befallen, contrary to expectation, to survive. 29.10. Wherefore I say that those who deliberate about peace ought not to put forward the good hope alone, but to keep in mind that the issue will be on either side, and thus to make the choice of their resolve. 29.11. These things being so, it seemed best to me to bring in you my fellow-commanders and these envoys of the emperor, in order that, choosing freely at the present time whatever may seem advantageous to the emperor, you may never afterward make any reproach against me concerning these affairs. 29.12. For it would be most absurd to keep silent while it is possible to choose the better course, but, when looking to the issue of fortune, to make accusations. 29.13. What, then, has been resolved by the emperor and is desired by Wittigis for the ending of the war, you doubtless know. 29.14. But if these things seem advantageous to you too, let each one come forward and say so. If, however, you think you are able to recover the whole of Italy for the Romans and to gain the mastery over the enemy, nothing will hinder you from saying it, holding back nothing.” When Belisarius had said this, 29.15. all openly declared that the emperor's plans were best, and that they would not be able to do the enemy any further harm. 29.16. And Belisarius, pleased with the opinion of the commanders, saw fit that they should make it plain in writing, in order that they might never deny it. And they, writing it in a little book, made plain that they were not able to prevail in the war over their opponents. 29.17. These things were happening in the Roman camp. But the Goths, hard pressed by famine and no longer able to hold out against the hardship, were distressed with the rule of Wittigis, as having been most unfortunate, but shrank from coming over to the emperor, fearing nothing else than lest, having become slaves of the emperor, they should be compelled to remove from Italy and, going to Byzantium, to settle there. 29.18. Deliberating, then, among themselves, whatever was uncorrupt among the Goths, they resolved to proclaim Belisarius emperor of the West. And sending to him secretly, they begged him to set himself in the kingship; for in this way they affirmed they would gladly follow him. 29.19. But Belisarius was by no means willing to set himself in power without the will of the emperor; 29.20. for he was prodigiously distressed at the name of tyrant, and had previously been bound by the most dreadful oaths to the emperor that he would never, while he lived, attempt a revolution. But in order that he might manage the present situation as well as possible, he resolved gladly to admit the words of the barbarians. 29.21. Perceiving this, Wittigis was afraid, and saying that the Goths had deliberated most excellently, he himself too secretly advised Belisarius to go into the kingship; 29.22. for no one would stand in his way. And then Belisarius again, calling together both the envoys of the emperor and all the commanders, asked whether it seemed to them worthy of much account to make all the Goths together with Wittigis captives, and to plunder all the goods, 29.23. and to recover the whole of Italy for the Romans. And they said this would be a lofty and prodigious piece of good fortune for the Romans, and saw fit for him to do whatever he was able as quickly as possible. 29.24. So immediately Belisarius sent some of his friends to Wittigis and the notable Goths, ordering them to fulfil what they had promised. 29.25. For the famine did not allow them to put off the matter to any other time, but, pressing still harder, urged them to this. 29.26. Wherefore they sent envoys to the Roman camp again, to say one thing to the multitude, but secretly to receive pledges from Belisarius that he would do harm to none of them, and that he himself for the future would be emperor over both the Italians and the Goths, and that thus he would come with them and the Roman army to Ravenna. 29.27. And Belisarius swore to all the other things, just as the envoys saw fit, but said that he would swear concerning the kingship to Wittigis himself and to the commanders of the Goths. 29.28. And the envoys, thinking that he would never reject the kingship but desired it above all things, ordered him immediately to go to Ravenna with them. 29.29. And then Belisarius ordered Bessas and John and Narses and Aratius (for these he suspected to be most ill-disposed to him) to go off, each in a different direction, with their followers, to provide themselves with the necessities; for he said it was no longer possible for him to bring provisions into this place for the whole army. 29.30. And they did so, together with Athanasius the prefect of the praetorian guards, who had just come from Byzantium; while he himself with the rest of the army went with the envoys of the Goths to Ravenna. 29.31. And filling a fleet of ships with grain and the other provisions, he ordered them to sail quickly into the harbour of Classes; for so the Romans call the suburb of Ravenna where the harbour is. 29.32. And to me at that time, as I observed the entrance of the Roman army into Ravenna, a thought came that affairs are accomplished by no means through the wisdom of men or any other excellence, but that there is a certain divine power which always turns their minds and leads them there, where there will be no hindrance to the things being accomplished. 29.33. For the Goths, far surpassing their opponents in numbers and power, and neither settled by battle, since they were in Ravenna, nor enslaved in spirit by anything else whatever, became captives of those inferior to them and held the name of slavery in no insult at all. 29.34. And the women (for they happened to have heard from their husbands that the enemy were great in body and superior in number), when they saw all of them sitting at the gate, spat in the faces of their husbands, all of them, and pointing with their hands at the victors, reproached them for their cowardice. 29.35. And Belisarius kept Wittigis in custody, not without honour, but ordered those of the barbarians who dwelt within the river Padus, who were going to their own fields, 29.36. to attend to them at their will. For he suspected that no enemy would arise there, nor would the Goths ever combine there, since he had previously established many of the Roman army in those regions. And they went off quickly and gladly. 29.37. And thus the Romans were now in safety; for they were no longer worsted by the Goths in Ravenna in numbers. And after this he took the goods in the Palatium, which he was to convey to the emperor. 29.38. For he himself plundered none of the Goths, nor permitted anyone else to plunder, but each of them preserved his property according to the agreements. 29.39. And when those of the barbarians who held the guard in the strongest of the places heard that Ravenna and Wittigis were held by the Romans, they sent envoys to Belisarius, seeing fit to surrender both themselves by agreement and what they guarded. 29.40. And he, providing pledges to all most readily, won over Tarbesion and any other fortification there was in Venetia. For Caesena alone in Aemilia was left, which he had previously won over together with Ravenna. 29.41. And all the Goths who commanded these places, as soon as they received pledges, came to Belisarius and remained there; but Ildibadus, a man of repute, who commanded the garrison in Verona, sent envoys indeed to Belisarius on the same terms as the others, since Belisarius had found and held his children in Ravenna, yet he neither came to Ravenna nor became subject to Belisarius. For a certain fortune befell him which I shall presently make plain.

[195868] 30.1. Some of the commanders of the Roman army, having envy toward Belisarius, slandered him to the emperor, bringing against him a charge of tyranny that in no way belonged to him. 30.2. But the emperor, not so much persuaded by these slanders, but because the Median war was now pressing upon him, summoned Belisarius as quickly as possible, in order that he might march against the Persians; and he ordered Bessas and John with the others to take care of Italy, and instructed Constantianus to go from Dalmatia to Ravenna. 30.3. And the Goths who were settled beyond the river Padus and Ravenna, hearing that the emperor was summoning Belisarius, at first regarded the matter as unreasonable, supposing that Belisarius would never set the kingship of Italy below his loyalty to Justinian. 30.4. But when they learned that the preparation for his departure was great, taking counsel together, whatever was still uncorrupt among them, they went to Ticinum to Uraïas the nephew of Wittigis, and after first weeping much with him, spoke as follows: 30.5. “No other has been more responsible for the present evils to the nation of the Goths than you. For we should long ago have removed your uncle, who led us so basely and unfortunately, from his rule, as we did Theudatus the nephew of Theoderic, had we not, out of respect for your seeming energy, resolved to concede to Wittigis the name of the kingship, but in deed to hand over to you alone the rule of the Goths. 30.6. But the goodwill that then seemed ours has turned about, now appearing as folly and the cause of these our misfortunes. 30.7. For of the Goths, as you know, O dear Uraïas, it befalls that most and the best have died in the war, and of the survivors, whatever best is left, Belisarius will go off taking, together with Wittigis and all the goods. 30.8. And that this same thing will befall us too a little later, being few and exceedingly pitiable, no one would gainsay. 30.9. With such terrors, then, standing about us, it will be more profitable to die with good repute than to behold our children and wives being led off by the enemy to the ends of the earth. 30.10. And we shall do something, as is likely, worthy of valour, if we have you as leader of our deeds.” The Goths said so much. 30.11. And Uraïas answered as follows: “That in the present terrors we ought to choose danger before slavery, it befalls me to think the same as you. 30.12. But for me to set myself in the kingship of the Goths I think altogether inexpedient; first, because, being the nephew of Wittigis, a man so unfortunate, I should seem contemptible to the enemy, since men think that like fortunes are always passed on to kinsmen; 30.13. and second, I should seem to be doing what is unholy, mounting to the rule of my uncle, and on this account I should, as is likely, have most of you distressed at me. 30.14. But I say that we ought to set Ildibadus into this danger as ruler of the Goths, a man who has come to the height of valour and is exceedingly energetic. 30.15. And it is not unlikely that Theudis, who is his uncle and the ruler of the Visigoths, will join in the war because of the kinship. Wherefore we shall carry on the contest against our opponents with better hope.” 30.16. Having said so much, Uraïas too seemed to all the Goths to be speaking what was advantageous. And Ildibadus came to them immediately, summoned from Verona. 30.17. And throwing the purple about him, they proclaimed him king of the Goths and begged him to set their present affairs well in order. Thus Ildibadus came into power. 30.18. And a little later, calling together all the Goths, he spoke as follows: “I know that all of you, fellow-soldiers, are experienced in many wars, so that we shall never with reason go straight to making war. For experience, bringing reckoning, is least wont to be bold. 30.19. And it is worth while for you, remembering all that has happened before, thus now to deliberate about the present. 30.20. For forgetfulness, coming upon many men of what has already gone before, has lifted up their thoughts not as it ought, through ignorance, 30.21. and has sufficiently tripped them up in the greatest matters. Wittigis, then, not against your will nor while you resisted, gave himself into the hands of the enemy, but having then given up before the oppositions of fortune, you thought it most advantageous for yourselves to sit at home and obey Belisarius, rather than to risk your bodies endlessly. 30.22. But now, having heard that he is being sent to Byzantium, you have resolved to undertake a revolution. And yet each of you ought to reckon that not all things turn out for men as they think, but the issue of affairs has often gone contrary to expectation, in the opposite direction to what was resolved. 30.23. For fortune and repentance are wont for the most part to set things right from the unexpected; and that this will happen now too to Belisarius is in no way unlikely. 30.24. It is better, then, first to inquire of him, and to try to set the man back to the agreements made before, and thus for you to proceed to the second course in your actions.” 30.25. When Ildibadus had said this, he seemed to the Goths to have deliberated well, and he sent envoys to Ravenna quickly. And these, coming into the sight of Belisarius, reminded him of the agreements made with them and reviled him as a breaker of what had been agreed, calling him a self-chosen slave, and reproaching him because he did not blush at choosing slavery before kingship, and saying many other such things, exhorted him to the rule. 30.26. For thus, they affirmed, Ildibadus too would come of his own accord, to lay down the purple at his feet and do obeisance to Belisarius as emperor of both Goths and Italians. 30.27. The envoys said these things, thinking that the man would draw to himself the name of kingship without any delay at once. 30.28. But he, contrary to their expectation, openly refused, saying that Belisarius would never, while the emperor Justinian lived, mount to the name of kingship. 30.29. And they, hearing this, departed as quickly as possible and reported the whole matter to Ildibadus. 30.30. And Belisarius went off toward Byzantium; and the winter was ending, and the fifth year of this war which Procopius has written was coming to a close.
