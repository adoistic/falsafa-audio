WORK: Thus Spake Zarathustra  (slug: friedrich-nietzsche-thus-spake-zarathustra-125858)

CHAPTER: 33-xxxii-the-dance-song



[0] One evening went Zarathustra and his disciples through the forest; and when he sought for a well, lo, he lighted upon a green meadow peacefully surrounded with trees and bushes, where maidens were dancing together. As soon as the maidens recognised Zarathustra, they ceased dancing; Zarathustra, however, approached them with friendly mien and spake these words:

[364] Cease not your dancing, ye lovely maidens! No game-spoiler hath come to you with evil eye, no enemy of maidens.

[477] God’s advocate am I with the devil: he, however, is the spirit of gravity. How could I, ye light-footed ones, be hostile to divine dances? Or to maidens’ feet with fine ankles?

[655] To be sure, I am a forest, and a night of dark trees: but he who is not afraid of my darkness, will find banks full of roses under my cypresses.

[801] And even the little God may he find, who is dearest to maidens: beside the well lieth he quietly, with closed eyes.

[918] Verily, in broad daylight did he fall asleep, the sluggard! Had he perhaps chased butterflies too much?

[1023] Upbraid me not, ye beautiful dancers, when I chasten the little God somewhat! He will cry, certainly, and weep—but he is laughable even when weeping!

[1174] And with tears in his eyes shall he ask you for a dance; and I myself will sing a song to his dance:

[1276] A dance-song and satire on the spirit of gravity my supremest, powerfulest devil, who is said to be “lord of the world.”—

[1399] And this is the song that Zarathustra sang when Cupid and the maidens danced together:

[1487] Of late did I gaze into thine eye, O Life! And into the unfathomable did I there seem to sink.

[1583] But thou pulledst me out with a golden angle; derisively didst thou laugh when I called thee unfathomable.

[1691] “Such is the language of all fish,” saidst thou; “what THEY do not fathom is unfathomable.

[1783] But changeable am I only, and wild, and altogether a woman, and no virtuous one:

[1865] Though I be called by you men the ‘profound one,’ or the ‘faithful one,’ ‘the eternal one,’ ‘the mysterious one.’

[1980] But ye men endow us always with your own virtues—alas, ye virtuous ones!”

[2055] Thus did she laugh, the unbelievable one; but never do I believe her and her laughter, when she speaketh evil of herself.

[2178] And when I talked face to face with my wild Wisdom, she said to me angrily: “Thou willest, thou cravest, thou lovest; on that account alone dost thou PRAISE Life!”

[2343] Then had I almost answered indignantly and told the truth to the angry one; and one cannot answer more indignantly than when one “telleth the truth” to one’s Wisdom.

[2510] For thus do things stand with us three. In my heart do I love only Life—and verily, most when I hate her!

[2617] But that I am fond of Wisdom, and often too fond, is because she remindeth me very strongly of Life!

[2719] She hath her eye, her laugh, and even her golden angle-rod: am I responsible for it that both are so alike?

[2828] And when once Life asked me: “Who is she then, this Wisdom?”—then said I eagerly: “Ah, yes! Wisdom!

[2929] One thirsteth for her and is not satisfied, one looketh through veils, one graspeth through nets.

[3028] Is she beautiful? What do I know! But the oldest carps are still lured by her.

[3108] Changeable is she, and wayward; often have I seen her bite her lip, and pass the comb against the grain of her hair.

[3226] Perhaps she is wicked and false, and altogether a woman; but when she speaketh ill of herself, just then doth she seduce most.”

[3355] When I had said this unto Life, then laughed she maliciously, and shut her eyes. “Of whom dost thou speak?” said she. “Perhaps of me?

[3490] And if thou wert right—is it proper to say THAT in such wise to my face! But now, pray, speak also of thy Wisdom!”

[3606] Ah, and now hast thou again opened thine eyes, O beloved Life! And into the unfathomable have I again seemed to sink.—

[3726] Thus sang Zarathustra. But when the dance was over and the maidens had departed, he became sad.

[3823] “The sun hath been long set,” said he at last, “the meadow is damp, and from the forest cometh coolness.

[3929] An unknown presence is about me, and gazeth thoughtfully. What! Thou livest still, Zarathustra?

[4026] Why? Wherefore? Whereby? Whither? Where? How? Is it not folly still to live?—

[4105] Ah, my friends; the evening is it which thus interrogateth in me. Forgive me my sadness!

[4195] Evening hath come on: forgive me that evening hath come on!”

[4257] Thus sang Zarathustra.
