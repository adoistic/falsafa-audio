CHAPTER: 08-book-8



[0] 1.1. All that I have related up to this point has been written in the way that it was possible to do, dividing and arranging the accounts according to the regions in which the actions of war happened to take place; and these accounts, already published, have been made known everywhere throughout the Roman empire. But from here on it will no longer be composed by me in the manner described.

[394] 1.2. For I could no longer fit what happened afterwards into the books that have been published to the world at large; rather, all that came to pass in the course of these wars, and likewise concerning the nation of the Medes—since I have already brought out my earlier accounts—shall be written by me in this one book, and it will of necessity be a varied history of these matters.

[778] 1.3. Now all that took place down to the fourth year of the five-year truce which had been made between the Romans and the Persians has been related by me in the earlier books; but in the year that followed, a great host of the Median army invaded the land of Colchis.

[1048] 1.4. Over them was set a Persian named Chorianes, a man very experienced in many wars, and there followed him many barbarian allies of the Alan nation.

[1201] 1.5. When this army arrived in a district of Lazica which is called Mocheresis, they encamped in a suitable place and waited.

[1328] 1.6. There flows here a river, the Hippis, neither large nor navigable, yet passable both for horsemen and for foot-soldiers; and on the right of it they made their stockade, not beside the bank, but at a considerable distance from it.

[1565] 1.7. Now in order that the regions of Lazica may be clear to those who read this, and the various nations of men that are settled about it, and that they may not be forced, like men fighting against shadows, to discuss matters obscure to themselves, it has seemed to me not unseasonable to set down here in my account in what manner men inhabit the so-called Euxine Sea; for I am not unaware that these things have been written of by some of the older writers, yet I think that not everything has been told by them with accuracy.

[2096] 1.8. For some of them have said that the neighbours of the Trapezuntines are either the Sani—who are now called Tzani—or that they are Colchians, while they give the name of Lazi to others, who even now are called by that name.

[2325] 1.9. Yet neither of these is the case. For the Tzani, being as far as possible from the coast, dwell next to the Armenians in the interior, and many mountains hang between, exceedingly impassable and altogether precipitous, and a great deal of country forever empty of men, and ravines from which there is no exit, and wooded hills and impenetrable caverns—by all of which the Tzani are prevented from being a people of the sea.

[2755] 1.10. And it is impossible that the Colchians should not be the Lazi, since they dwell along the river Phasis; and only the name 'Colchians' has now been changed to that of 'Lazi,' just as has happened with many other nations of men.

[2990] 1.11. Apart from this, the great length of time that has succeeded those who recorded those matters, ever bringing changes along with events, has had the power to alter most of the institutions formerly established, through migrations of nations and through successions of rulers and of names.

[3285] 1.12. These things it has seemed to me most necessary to measure out, not reporting the legendary tales about them or otherwise their antiquities, nor where in the Euxine Pontus the poets say that Prometheus was bound;

[3505] 1.13. for I think that legend is far removed from history. But I shall go through with accuracy both the names and the facts, all those that now belong to each of those places.

[3683] 2.1. This Pontus, then, begins at Byzantium and Chalcedon, and ends at the land of the Colchians.

[3782] 2.2. And as one sails into it, on the right are settled the Bithynians, and next to them the Honoriatae and the Paphlagonians, who hold, among other places, the seaside cities of Heraclea and Amastris; and after them those called the Pontici, as far as the city of Trapezus and its borders. Here are settled other seaside towns, and Sinope and Amisus; and very close to Amisus is the place called Themiscyron and the river Thermodon, where they say the camp of the Amazons was. But concerning the Amazons it will be written by me not far hereafter.

[4332] 2.3. The borders of the Trapezuntines extend to the village of Susurmena and to the place called Rhizaeum, which is distant from Trapezus a journey of two days through the coast-land for one going into Lazica.

[4543] 2.4. And since I have made mention of Trapezus, I must not pass over a thing that happens here, most contrary to reason. For the honey in all the regions about Trapezus turns out bitter, here alone the reputation concerning it being in dispute.

[4789] 2.5. On the right of these regions the mountains of Tzanica all rise up, and beyond them are settled the Armenians who are subjects of the Romans.

[4937] 2.6. From these Tzanic mountains there comes down a river named the Boas, which, passing through very many thickets and circling about a hilly country, is carried very close to the regions of Lazica, and makes its outlet into the so-called Euxine Pontus—not, however, called the Boas.

[5223] 2.7. For when it comes near the sea, it lets go this name and from that point receives another appellation, getting its name from what comes upon it.

[5374] 2.8. For the natives henceforth call it the Acampsis, for this reason: that it is impossible for it, when mingled with the sea, to bend; for it makes its outlet with such force and swiftness of current, working a great disturbance of the surge in front, that, going very far out into the sea, it makes the passage there impossible; and those who sail in this part of the Pontus, whether sailing straight for Lazica or setting out from there, can no longer sail through in a continuous course.

[5868] 2.9. For they cannot in any way bend the current of the river, but, putting out very far from the open sea there, and going to about the middle of the Pontus, in this way they are able to get clear of the river's outlet. Such, then, are the facts concerning the river Boas.

[6143] 2.10. After Rhizaeum the borders of independent men succeed, who are settled between the Romans and the Lazi. And a certain village, Athens by name, is settled there—not because colonists of the Athenians, as some suppose, established themselves here, but because a certain woman named Athenaea was in early times mistress of the country, whose tomb is here even down to my own time.

[6528] 2.11. And after Athens, Archabis is settled, and Apsarus, an ancient city, which is distant from Rhizaeum a journey of about three days.

[6666] 2.12. This was anciently named Apsyrtus, having become of the same name as the man because of his calamity. For there, the natives say, by the plot of Medea and Jason, Apsyrtus was made to vanish from among men, and on this account the place received its name; for he died there, and the place was named after him.

[6982] 2.13. But a very great length of time flowing on afterwards, and coming to its height through countless successions of men, has had the power to destroy the form of the matter from which this name is composed, and to change the appellation of the place into the manner now apparent.

[7266] 2.14. And the tomb of this Apsyrtus is on the side of the city toward the rising sun. This city was anciently populous, and a great circuit of wall surrounded it, and it was adorned with a theatre and a hippodrome and with all the other things by which the greatness of a city is wont to be shown. But now nothing else of these is left, except the foundations of the structures.

[7646] 2.15. So that one might with reason wonder at those who say that the Colchians are neighbours of the Trapezuntines. For in that case Jason, after he had plundered the fleece together with Medea, would not appear to have fled toward Hellas and his ancestral homes, but rather backward, toward the river Phasis and the barbarians of the interior.

[7992] 2.16. They say, then, that in the times of Trajan the emperor of the Romans, registers of Roman soldiers were stationed both here and as far as the Lazi and the Saginae.

[8163] 2.17. But at the present time men dwell here who are subjects neither of the Roman nor of the Lazic king, except indeed that, being Christians, the bishops of the Lazi appoint priests for them.

[8358] 2.18. And they themselves, wishing to be at peace and on friendly terms with both, have agreed to escort continually those who are at any time sent from either side to the other; and this they appear to do down to my own day.

[8585] 2.19. For in their own boats they convey, by sailing, the envoys sent from one king to the other. Tributaries, however, they have never become down to the present time.

[8755] 2.20. On the right of these regions there hang very steep mountains, and a desert country extends a very great distance. And above it are settled those called the Persarmenians, and the Armenians who are subjects of the Romans, extending as far as the borders of Iberia.

[9027] 2.21. From the city of Apsarus to the city of Petra and the borders of the Lazi, where the Euxine Pontus ends, is a journey of one day. And the Pontus, coming to an end here, makes the shore crescent-shaped.

[9236] 2.22. And the passage across this crescent extends to about five hundred and fifty stades, and all the region behind it is Lazica, and so named.

[9382] 2.23. And after them, in the interior, are Scymnia and Suania. These nations happen to be subjects of the Lazi. And the men there have rulers of their own kin; but whenever the appointed day of life comes upon one of the rulers, it is the custom that another be appointed for them in his place by the king of the Lazi, always.

[9710] 2.24. On the flank of this country, close beside Iberia itself, are settled the Meschi, subjects of the Iberians from of old,

[9837] 2.25. having their dwellings in the mountains. And the mountains of the Meschi are neither harsh nor barren of fruits, but they abound in all good things, since the Meschi are also skilful farmers, and there happen to be vineyards there.

[10076] 2.26. Upon this country there lie mountains exceedingly high and dense and terribly impassable. And these extend as far as the Caucasus mountains; and behind them, toward the rising sun, is Iberia, extending as far as the Persarmenians.

[10314] 2.27. Through the mountains that rise here the river Phasis comes down, beginning from the Caucasus, and it empties into the middle of the crescent of the Pontus.

[10478] 2.28. And here some suppose that it separates the two continents. For the parts on the left of the descending current are Asia, and those on the right are named Europe.

[10648] 2.29. Now in the portion of Europe it happens that all the dwellings of the Lazi are situated, but on the other side the Lazi have neither town nor any other fortress nor village worthy of mention, except that the Romans built Petra there in former times.

[10905] 2.30. And somewhere in this portion of Lazica was stored away, as the natives say, that famous fleece, on account of which the poets tell the tale that the Argo was fashioned. But they say these things, in my judgment, with very little truth.

[11149] 2.31. For Jason, I think, would not have got away from there unobserved by Aeetes, with Medea and the fleece, unless the royal palace and the other dwellings of the Colchians had been separated from the place where that fleece happened to lie by the river Phasis—a thing which the poets too who recorded such matters indicate.

[11477] 2.32. The Phasis, then, flowing in the way I have described, empties into the very end of the Euxine Pontus. And of the crescent, at the one end, which is in Asia, the city of Petra happened to be; while on the opposite shore, in the portion of Europe, is the country of the Apsilii;

[11762] 2.33. and the Apsilii are subjects of the Lazi and have been Christians from of old, just like all the other nations which I have mentioned down to this point in my account.

[11937] 3.1. Above this country is the Caucasus mountain. And this mountain, the Caucasus, rises to such a height that neither rains nor snows ever touch its summits; for they happen to be higher than all the clouds. But the middle parts, up to the topmost snows, are full of snow continually.

[12224] 3.2. And the spurs that descend from it happen to be exceedingly high, in no way inferior to the crags in the other mountains.

[12352] 3.3. And of the spurs of the Caucasus mountain, those turned toward the north wind and the setting sun extend to the Illyrians and the Thracians, while those toward the rising sun and the south wind reach to the very passes which lead the Hunnic nations settled there into the land of the Persians and the Romans.

[12667] 3.4. Of these passes the one is called Tzur, and the other was of old called the Caspian Gate. This country, which extends from the Caucasus mountain to the Caspian Gates, the Alans hold, an independent nation, who for the most part are allies of the Persians and march against the Romans and other enemies. Such, then, are the conditions about the Caucasus mountain.

[13036] 3.5. And the Huns called Sabeiri are settled here, and certain other Hunnic nations. From here, they say, the Amazons set out, and encamped about Themiscyron and the river Thermodon, as I have just said,

[13241] 3.6. where there is now the city of Amisus. But now nowhere in the regions about the Caucasus mountain is any memory or name of the Amazons preserved, although many accounts have been given of them by Strabo and certain others.

[13470] 3.7. But those seem to me to have spoken most of all, in the matter of the Amazons, with the true account, who said that there never was a race of warlike women, nor did human nature in the Caucasus mountain alone depart from its own laws; but that barbarians from these regions marched into Asia with a great army together with their own women, and, making a camp about the river Thermodon, left their women there, while they themselves overran the greater part of Asia; and, the inhabitants there confronting them, they all perished from among men, and not one of them at all returned to the women's stockade; and thereafter these women, compelled by fear of their neighbours and by want of provisions, put on the manly garb, not willingly, and, taking up the equipment of arms left by the men in the camp, and armed with this as well as they could, displayed deeds of manly valour with excellence, necessity driving them to this, until it befell that they were all destroyed.

[14450] 3.8. That these things happened in some such way, and that the Amazons campaigned together with men, I too believe, drawing my evidence from things that happened to occur in my own time.

[14638] 3.9. For the practices of those who have gone before, passed on to their descendants, become an image of their nature.

[14758] 3.10. For when the Huns had often overrun the Roman empire and come to blows with those who confronted them, it happened that some of them fell there; and after the withdrawal of the barbarians the Romans, examining the bodies of the fallen, found women among them.

[15025] 3.11. No other army of women, however, has anywhere appeared as native to Asia or Europe. Nor indeed do we know by report that the Caucasus mountains have ever been empty of men. Concerning the Amazons, then, let this much be said.

[15258] 3.12. After the Apsilii and the other end of the crescent, along the coast are settled the Abasgi, extending as far as the Caucasus mountains. And the Abasgi were subjects of the Lazi from of old,

[15456] 3.13. and they always had two rulers of their own kin. Of these, the one was established in the western part of the country, the other in the part toward the rising sun.

[15627] 3.14. And these barbarians, down to my own time, revered groves and woods; for in a kind of barbarian simplicity they supposed the trees to be gods.

[15777] 3.15. And from their own rulers they suffered the most terrible things through the greatness of their love of money. For both their kings, whatever boys they saw in this nation who were comely of countenance and beautiful of body, these they would, with no hesitation at all, drag away from their parents and, making them eunuchs, sell them into the land of the Romans to those who wished to buy them, for great sums of money.

[16205] 3.16. And the fathers of these boys they would kill at once, lest any of them should ever undertake to avenge upon the king the wrong done to their children, nor that any of the subjects there should be suspect to them. And so the comeliness of their sons turned out to their destruction;

[16495] 3.17. for the wretched men were destroyed, having been afflicted with the deadly beauty of their children. And from this it came about that most of the eunuchs among the Romans, and not least those in the emperor's court, happened to be Abasgi by race.

[16749] 3.18. But under the present emperor Justinian everything has come to be changed for the Abasgi to a gentler condition.

[16869] 3.19. For they chose the doctrines of the Christians, and the emperor Justinian, sending to them one of the eunuchs of the Palace, an Abasgian by race, named Euphratas, forbade their kings expressly that anyone henceforth in this nation should be deprived of his manhood, his nature forced with iron. And this the Abasgi gladly heard,

[17205] 3.20. and, taking courage from the command of the Roman emperor, they now hindered this deed with all their might. For each of them feared lest he should ever become the father of a comely child.

[17402] 3.21. Then indeed the emperor Justinian both built a temple of the Mother of God among the Abasgi, and, appointing priests for them, brought it about that they were taught thoroughly all the customs of the Christians; and the Abasgi, deposing both their kings, immediately thought to live in freedom. These things, then, went thus.

[17735] 4.1. After the borders of the Abasgi, by the Caucasus mountain are settled the Bruchi, being between the Abasgi and the Alans, while along the coast of the Euxine Pontus are settled the Zechi.

[17929] 4.2. And for the Zechi the emperor of the Romans in old times used to appoint a king, but now these barbarians obey the Romans in nothing at all.

[18076] 4.3. And after them the Saginae dwell, and a portion of their coast the Romans have held from of old.

[18179] 4.4. And, building two seaside forts, Sebastopolis and Pityus, distant from one another a journey of two days, they stationed there from the beginning a guard of soldiers.

[18352] 4.5. For formerly registers of Roman soldiers held all the regions along the coast from the borders of Trapezus as far as the Saginae, as I have said; but now only these two forts were left to them, where they held the garrisons even down to my own time, since Chosroes, the king of the Persians, when the Lazi had brought him into Petra, was eager to send an army of Persians there, both to hold these forts and to settle in the garrisons there.

[18800] 4.6. But when the Roman soldiers were able to learn this beforehand, getting the start, they burned the dwellings and razed the walls to the ground, and, embarking with no delay in the boats, went straight to the mainland opposite and to the city of Trapezus, harming the Roman empire by the destruction of the forts, yet procuring for it a great gain, in that the enemy did not become masters of the country. For thereupon the Persians returned to Petra without accomplishing anything. These things, then, happened thus.

[19323] 4.7. Beyond the Saginae are settled many Hunnic nations. And the country from there is named Eulysia, and barbarian men hold both its coastland and its interior, as far as the so-called Maeotic Lake and the river Tanais, which empties into the Lake.

[19574] 4.8. And this Lake makes its outlets into the shore of the Euxine Pontus. And the men who are settled here were anciently named Cimmerians, but now are called Utigurs.

[19743] 4.9. And above them, toward the north wind, are settled the boundless nations of the Antae. And beside the very place where the outlet of the Lake begins are settled the Goths called Tetraxitae, not many in number, who reverence and observe the customs of the Christians no less than any others.

[20040] 4.10. (The natives call this outlet too the Tanais, which, beginning from the Maeotic Lake, extends as far as the Euxine Pontus, a journey, they say, of twenty days. But also the wind that blows from here they call the Tanaite.)

[20270] 4.11. Whether these Goths were ever of the Arian doctrine, like the other Gothic nations, or whether some other thing was practised by them in the matter of doctrine, I cannot say, since they themselves do not know it; but in simplicity now and in great freedom from meddling they honour their doctrine.

[20575] 4.12. These men, a little before—I mean when the emperor Justinian was holding the imperial power for the twenty-first year—sent four envoys to Byzantium, begging that a bishop be given them; for the man who had been their priest had died not long before, and they had learned that the emperor had also sent a priest to the Abasgi; and the emperor Justinian, most readily fulfilling their request, sent them away.

[20990] 4.13. But these envoys, through fear of the Huns, the Utigurs, openly—since many were within hearing—uttering for what reason they had come, declared nothing else than the matter of the priest to the emperor, but, meeting with him as secretly as possible, told everything that was likely to be of advantage to the Roman empire, the barbarians who are their neighbours being forever at strife with one another. But in what manner the Tetraxitae, and from where they removed and settled here, I now go on to tell.

[21503] 5.1. Of old a great multitude of Huns, then called Cimmerians, occupied these regions which I have just mentioned, and one king was set over them all.

[21655] 5.2. And once a certain one of them held the rule, to whom were born two sons, the one named Utigur, the other Cutrigur.

[21777] 5.3. These, when their father had measured out his life, divided the rule between themselves and gave their own names to their subjects;

[21915] 5.4. for the one are called Utigurs, the other Cutrigurs, even to my own day. These all dwelt here, having all their pursuits in common, but not mingling with the men who were settled on the other side of the Lake and the outflow from it; for they never crossed these waters, nor did they suppose them to be passable, being afraid of the easiest of things, because they never made trial of them at all, but were wholly unpractised in the crossing.

[22364] 5.5. But to one who crossed over the Maeotic Lake and the outlet from it, immediately upon its very shore the Goths called Tetraxitae dwelt of old, whom I have just mentioned; and far away from them were settled the Goths and the Visigoths and the Vandals and all the other Gothic races.

[22653] 5.6. These too in earlier times were called Scythians, since all the nations that held those regions are called Scythian in common, while some of them were called Sauromatae or Melanchlaeni, or by some other name.

[22868] 5.7. As time went on, they say—if the account is sound—some young men of the Cimmerians were occupied in a hunt, and a single doe, fleeing from them, leaped into these waters.

[23045] 5.8. And the young men, whether held by some ambition or rivalry, or whether some divine power compelled them, followed after this doe, and let her go by no device, until together with her they came to the shore opposite.

[23268] 5.9. And the thing that was pursued, whatever it was, immediately vanished (for it seems to me that for no other reason did it appear here than that ill might befall the barbarians settled there); but the young men failed of their hunt, yet found occasion for battle and for plunder.

[23553] 5.10. For returning as quickly as possible to their ancestral homes, they made it plain to all the Cimmerians that these waters were passable for them. So, taking up their arms at once and crossing in full force, they were with no delay on the mainland opposite—the Vandals having already removed from there and settled in Libya, and the Visigoths having taken up their dwelling in Spain.

[23943] 5.11. Falling, then, suddenly upon the Goths who dwelt in the plains here, they killed many, and put all the rest to flight.

[24069] 5.12. And those who were able to escape them, removing from there with children and wives, left their ancestral homes, and, ferrying across the river Ister, came to be in the land of the Romans.

[24265] 5.13. And they did many terrible things to those settled there, but afterwards, the emperor granting it, they settled in the regions of Thrace, and partly fought as allies with the Romans, receiving their pay, like the other soldiers, from the emperor every year, and being called foederati; for thus the Romans then called them in the Latin tongue, indicating this, I think: that the Goths had not been conquered by them in war, but were on certain terms in a state of treaty with them—

[24754] 5.14. for the Latins call treaties made in war foedera, as I have explained in the earlier books; and partly they carried on war against them, on no plea at all, until they went off, departing into Italy, with Theoderic leading them. Such, then, was the course of the affairs of the Goths.

[25045] 5.15. But the Huns, having killed some of them, and, as has been said, driven out others, held the country. And of these the Cutrigurs, sending for their children and wives, settled there,

[25235] 5.16. where they dwell even to my own day. And many gifts they receive from the emperor every year, and yet, crossing the river Ister, they forever overrun the emperor's country,

[25415] 5.17. being at once in a state of treaty and at war with the Romans. But the Utigurs, with their leader, returned home, intending thenceforth to settle there alone.

[25581] 5.18. These, when they came near the Maeotic Lake, encountered there the Goths called Tetraxitae.

[25680] 5.19. And at first the Goths, fencing themselves with their shields, took their stand opposite the assailants as if to defend themselves, trusting in their own strength and in the strength of the place; for they themselves are the bravest of all the barbarians there.

[25949] 5.20. And the first outlet of the Maeotis, where the Tetraxite Goths were then settled, coming together into a crescent-shaped gulf, and surrounding them for the most part, provided to the assailants a single entrance against them, not very wide.

[26197] 5.21. But afterwards—for neither did the Huns wish to waste any time there, and the Goths in no way hoped to hold out for long against the multitude of the enemy—they came to terms with one another, on condition that, mingling together, they should make the crossing in common, and the Goths should settle on the mainland opposite, close beside the very shore of the outlet, where they are now settled, and, being friends and allies of the Utigurs thenceforth, on equal and like terms, should live there for all time.

[26716] 5.22. Thus, then, these Goths settled here, and, the Cutrigurs, as I have said, being left in the land on the other side of the Lake, the Utigurs alone held the country, causing the Romans as little trouble as possible, since they do not dwell near them, but, being separated from them by many nations that lie between, they keep clear of them through an involuntary inactivity.

[27096] 5.23. Now to one who has crossed over the Maeotic Lake and the river Tanais, over the greater part of the plains there the Cutrigur Huns settled, as I have said; and after them Scythians and Tauri hold all the country there, a portion of which is even now called the Tauric land, where they say there was the temple of Artemis, over which once Iphigenia the daughter of Agamemnon presided.

[27487] 5.24. And yet the Armenians say that this temple was in the country among them called Celesene, and that all the men there were then called Scythians, drawing their evidence from the things I have related concerning Orestes and the city of Comana in that part of my account.

[27763] 5.25. But concerning these matters let each man speak as it pleases him; for of many things that have happened elsewhere, and perhaps have nowhere happened at all, men are fond of claiming them as their ancestral traditions, taking offence if all do not follow their own opinion.

[28044] 5.26. After these nations a seaside city is settled, Bosporus by name, which became subject to the Romans not long ago.

[28165] 5.27. And as one goes from the city of Bosporus to the city of Cherson, which lies on the coast and is itself subject to the Romans from of old, barbarians, Hunnic nations, hold all the country between.

[28369] 5.28. And two other towns near Cherson, called Cepi and Phanaguris, were subject to the Romans both from of old and down to my own day. These, not long ago, certain of the neighbouring barbarians captured and razed to the ground.

[28600] 5.29. And from the city of Cherson to the outlets of the river Ister, which they also call the Danube, is a journey of ten days, and barbarians hold all the country there.

[28773] 5.30. And the river Ister flows from the Celtic mountains, and, going around the borders of Italy and being carried to the regions of the Dacians and the Illyrians and the regions of Thrace, empties into the Euxine Pontus. And all the country from there as far as Byzantium belongs to the emperor of the Romans.

[29086] 5.31. Such, then, is the circuit of the Euxine Pontus from Chalcedon to Byzantium.

[29170] 5.32. But how great is the extent of this circuit, I cannot say with accuracy in everything, since, as I have said, so great a multitude of barbarians are settled here, and there is no intercourse on their part with the Romans except what is by way of embassy, and perhaps not even that; for neither does it happen that anything accurate has been said by those who earlier undertook to measure these things.

[29579] 5.33. This, however, is clear: that the parts of the Euxine Pontus on the right—that would be from Chalcedon to the river Phasis—are a journey of fifty-two days for a well-girt man; from which, not unreasonably drawing his evidence, one might say that the other portion of the Pontus too is not far from this measure.

[29898] 6.1. But since we have come to this point in the account, it has seemed to me not unseasonable to set down all the disputes which those skilled in these matters wage with one another concerning the boundaries of Asia and Europe.

[30128] 6.2. For some of them say that the river Tanais separates these continents, maintaining that the divisions must be natural, and drawing their evidence from this: that the sea, advancing from the western parts, is carried toward the eastern portion, while the river Tanais, carried from the northern regions toward the south wind, proceeds between the two continents; and conversely, the Egyptian Nile, going from the south toward the north wind, is carried between Asia and Libya.

[30610] 6.3. But others, going contrary to them, maintain that the account is not sound. For they say that these continents were from the beginning separated by the strait at Gadeira, which issues from the Ocean, and by the sea advancing from there; and that the parts on the right of the strait and the sea are named Libya and Asia, while all the parts on the left were called Europe, as far indeed as the ending of the so-called Euxine Pontus.

[31049] 6.4. These things being so, the river Tanais, born in the land of Europe, empties into the Maeotic Lake, and the Lake makes its outlets into the Euxine Pontus neither at its ending nor indeed at its middle, but still further on.

[31279] 6.5. And the parts on the left of this Pontus are reckoned to the portion of Asia. Apart from this, the river Tanais issues from the mountains called the Rhipaean, which are in the land of Europe, as even those who recorded these matters from of old agree.

[31537] 6.6. And from these Rhipaean mountains the Ocean happens to be very far distant. The parts, then, behind both these mountains and the river Tanais must of necessity be European on both sides.

[31730] 6.7. From where, then, the Tanais begins to separate the two continents is not easy to say. But if some river is to be named as separating both continents,

[31887] 6.8. this would be that Phasis. For, carried opposite the strait at Gadeira, it proceeds through the middle of the two continents, since the strait, issuing from the Ocean and making this sea, has these continents on either side, while the Phasis, going to about the very ending of the Euxine Pontus, empties into the middle of the crescent, manifestly receiving the section of the land from the sea.

[32289] 6.9. These things, then, both parties advance and dispute. But that not only the former account, but also this one which we have just stated, has been embellished by length of time and by the opinion of certain very ancient men, I shall make plain—knowing this, that for the most part all men, if they have first gained their learning from some ancient account, are no longer willing to toil, dwelling in the search for the truth, nor to learn afresh some newer opinion concerning it, but always to them the older seems sound and honourable, while what belongs to their own day is thought contemptible and passes into the ridiculous.

[32924] 6.10. Besides this, the inquiry now is not concerning anything intellectual or conceptual or otherwise unseen, but concerning a river and a country—things which time has been able neither to change nor in any way to hide.

[33147] 6.11. For both experience is at hand, and sight is most sufficient for testimony, and I think that those who are eager to find the truth will be hindered in nothing.

[33314] 6.12. Now Herodotus of Halicarnassus says in the fourth book of his Histories that the whole earth is one, but is reckoned to be divided into three portions and appellations, Libya and Asia and Europe.

[33517] 6.13. And of these, between Libya and Asia is carried the Egyptian river Nile, while Asia and Europe are separated by the Colchian Phasis. And knowing that some held this opinion concerning the river Tanais, he added this too afterwards.

[33756] 6.14. And it has seemed to me not unseasonable to insert into the account the very words of Herodotus, which are somewhat as follows: 'Nor can I conjecture for what reason, the earth being one, three names lie upon it, bearing the appellations of women, and the Egyptian river Nile and the Colchian Phasis were set as boundaries to it.'

[34094] 6.15. 'And others say that the river Tanais the Maeetian and the Cimmerian ferries are the boundary.' But also the tragic poet Aeschylus, in his Prometheus Unbound, immediately at the beginning of the tragedy calls the river Phasis the boundary of the land of Asia and of Europe.

[34375] 6.16. And this too let it be said by me at present, that of those skilled in such matters, some suppose that the Maeotic Lake makes the Euxine Pontus, and that part of it goes on the left, part on the right of the Lake, and that for this reason the Lake is called the mother of the Pontus.

[34666] 6.17. And they say these things, drawing their evidence from this: that from the place called the Hieron the outflow of this Pontus comes down toward Byzantium like a river, and from this they suppose that this is the end of the Pontus.

[34904] 6.18. But those who arraign the account declare that the whole sea is one, issuing from the Ocean and nowhere else ending, and extends to the land of the Lazi—except, they say, if one should call the difference in the names a difference of substance, in that from there the sea has been named the Pontus.

[35210] 6.19. And if the currents come down from the so-called Hieron toward Byzantium, this is no matter. For the phenomena that occur in all straits appear to yield to no reason, nor has anyone ever been competent to explain them.

[35436] 6.20. But also Aristotle of Stagira, a man wise above most, having gone for this very reason to Chalcis in Euboea, and observing the strait there, which they call the Euripus, and wishing to investigate accurately the natural cause—how and in what manner sometimes the currents of this strait are carried from the west, sometimes from the rising of the sun, and according to these all the ships there happen to sail; and if ever, the current going from the rising sun, and the sailors having begun from there to sail with the inflow of the surge, as was their custom, the current should come against them, which often is wont to happen there, these ships are turned back at once to where they set out, while the others sail from the west to the other side, although not even the slightest wind has blown upon them, but there is a deep calm there and a windlessness—these things Aristotle of Stagira, pondering and turning over for a length of time, in distress of mind through his perplexity, came to the measure of his life.

[36463] 6.21. Nevertheless, in the strait too which separates Italy and Sicily, many things are wont to happen against reason. For the current seems to go there from the so-called Adriatic Sea,

[36650] 6.22. and yet the advance of the sea is from the Ocean and Gadeira. But also frequent whirlpools suddenly there, from no cause apparent to us, destroy the ships.

[36813] 6.23. And on this account the poets say that the ships are swallowed by Charybdis, all those that happen to be at that time in this strait.

[36954] 6.24. And these men suppose that all these things, occurring most against reason in all straits, occur from the proximity of the mainland on either side; for they say that the surge, forced by the narrowness, proceeds into certain strange and unreasonable constraints.

[37224] 6.25. So that even if the current seems to be carried from the so-called Hieron toward Byzantium, one would not reasonably maintain that the sea and the Euxine Pontus end here.

[37402] 6.26. For this account does not stand upon any firm nature, but here too let the narrowness prevail. Yet this is not altogether so.

[37535] 6.27. For the fishermen of these regions say that the current does not come down toward Byzantium as a whole, but that its upper parts, which are visible to us, go in this direction, while the parts beneath, where there is and is called the abyss, expressly go the opposite to the upper parts, and are always carried contrary to what appears.

[37879] 6.28. Whereby, indeed, whenever in pursuit of a catch of fish they cast their nets somewhere here, these are always forced by the current to be carried toward the Hieron.

[38051] 6.29. But in Lazica the land on all sides, beating back the advance of the sea and checking its course, makes it end there, first and alone—the Creator, evidently, having set their bounds for them there.

[38256] 6.30. For the sea, touching the shore there, neither advances nor rises to any greater height, although it is forever flooded on all sides by the outlets of rivers numberless and exceedingly vast, but, retreating backward, it comes back again, and, measuring out its own proper measure, preserves the bound set for it, as though fearing some law, and constrained accurately by the necessity from it, lest it should appear to have transgressed any of the ordinances.

[38723] 6.31. For all the other shores of the sea happen to lie not opposite to it but at an angle. But concerning these matters let each man know and say as is dear to him.

[38890] 7.1. For what reason Chosroes was eager to lay claim to Lazica has been told by me before; but what carried him and the Persians most of all to this I shall now make plain, since I have described this whole country and made the account concerning it clear.

[39148] 7.2. Often these barbarians, with Chosroes leading them, invading the land of the Romans with a great army, inflicted upon their enemies sufferings not easy to describe, which have been told by me in the books concerning them; but to themselves, carrying off no profit at all from there, it happened that they suffered loss both in money and in men; for they always withdrew from the land of the Romans after losing many.

[39571] 7.3. Wherefore, returning to their ancestral homes, they reviled Chosroes most secretly, and called him the destroyer of the Persian race.

[39711] 7.4. And once, returning from Lazica, when they had met there with certain incurable sufferings, they were about to combine openly against him and to put him to death by a most pitiable end, had he not, learning it beforehand, guarded himself, getting round the most notable men among them by much flattery.

[40020] 7.5. And from this, wishing to make a defence against the accusation, he was eager to procure some great advantage for the empire of the Persians. At all events, having at once attempted the city of Daras, he was beaten back from there, as I have said, and came altogether to despair of mastering the place.

[40329] 7.6. For neither could he take it thenceforth by assault, the garrisons there being so on their guard, nor indeed by besieging it did he hope by any device to get the better of them.

[40513] 7.7. For both the other provisions are always purposely stored up in abundance in the city of Daras, so that they may suffice for a great length of time, and a spring, springing up close at hand in a precipitous spot, makes a great river which is carried straight for the city, those who desire to plot against it being unable either to turn it aside anywhere else or in any other way to force it, on account of the difficulty of the ground.

[40956] 7.8. And whenever it comes within the circuit-wall, going round the whole city and filling the cisterns there, it then goes out, and, very close to the wall, falling into a chasm, it vanishes. And where

[41160] 7.9. it issues forth from there has become clear to no one down to the present time. And this chasm did not happen to come into being in old times, but a long time after the emperor Anastasius built this city, nature, of itself, made it in the place; and on this account it befalls those who wish to besiege the city of Daras to be hard pressed by great want of water.

[41530] 7.10. Failing, then, of this attempt, as I have said, Chosroes came to the thought that, even if it were possible to subdue some other city of the Romans, yet he could never settle in the midst of the Romans, with many strongholds left behind to the enemy.

[41788] 7.11. For it was for this very reason that he razed Antioch to the ground when he captured it, and then departed from the land of the Romans. Wherefore, lifted up in his thought, he was borne to a longer hope, searching out impossible deeds.

[42031] 7.12. For having heard by report in what manner, on the left of the Euxine Pontus, those barbarians who are settled about the Maeotic Lake overrun the land of the Romans without fear, he said that thus too, for the Persians who held Lazica, it would be possible with no toil, whenever they wished, to go straight for Byzantium, in no way ferrying across the sea, as the other barbarian nations settled here forever do.

[42451] 7.13. For these reasons the Persians lay claim to Lazica. But I return to the point from which I made this digression.

[42571] 8.1. Chorianes, then, and the Median army encamped about the river Hippis. And when Gubazes, the king of the Colchians, learned this, and Dagisthaeus, who commanded the Roman army, taking common counsel, they led the army of the Romans and the Lazi against the enemy.

[42840] 8.2. And, coming to be on the other side of the river Hippis and encamping there, they took counsel about the present situation, whether it would profit them more to remain and receive the enemy as they came on, or whether they should go against the enemy, in order that, making a display of their own courage to the Persians, and making it plain to their opponents that they advanced upon them with contempt, and beginning the engagement themselves, they might be able to enslave the spirit of their adversaries. And when the opinion of those who urged advancing upon the enemy prevailed,

[43431] 8.3. they all set out against them at once. Thereupon the Lazi no longer thought fit to be drawn up with the Romans, putting forward this: that the Romans entered the contest with no danger to their fatherland or their nearest kin, while for themselves the danger was on behalf of children and wives and ancestral land; so that they would even blush before their own women,

[43806] 8.4. if it should befall them to be conquered by their adversaries. By this necessity, then, they expected to improvise on the spot the valour which they did not possess.

[43978] 8.5. And first they themselves were eager to go against the enemy by themselves, in order that the Romans might not throw them into confusion in the work, since they were not equally eager for the danger.

[44184] 8.6. When the Lazi had made this bold show, Gubazes, exceedingly delighted, called them together a little apart from the Romans and exhorted them as follows.

[44343] 8.7. "An exhortation rousing to good courage, men, I know not whether it is needful to make to you. For those whom the necessity of affairs rouses to eagerness need, I think, no exhortation at all—which has befallen us also at the present moment.

[44591] 8.8. For on behalf of wives and children and ancestral land, and, to speak simply, on behalf of all that is ours, is the danger, on behalf of which the Persians come against us.

[44770] 8.9. And to those who would by force take away anything of one's possessions, no one of all men yields, nature compelling us to fight in defence of what belongs to us.

[44939] 8.10. And you are not ignorant that for the Persians, who have come into the power of acting as they will, nothing stays their greed; nor will they merely rule us, or impose tribute, or in other respects make us subjects, if we have not forgotten what Chosroes attempted upon us not long ago, should they now get the better of us in the war.

[45282] 8.11. But let not even the report of the attempt of the Persians advance beyond words to deeds for me, nor let the name of the Lazi fail. And the struggle against the Medes is not hard for us, men, since we have often come to blows with them and surpassed them in battle.

[45555] 8.12. For to one accustomed to it the difficulty is in no way present, the hardship of the task having been spent beforehand through practice and experience. So that for this reason it will be needful for us to despise the enemy as men who have been conquered in engagements and are not equally bold against us.

[45868] 8.13. For a spirit once enslaved is by no means wont to run back again. Reckoning these things, then, with good hope go against your adversaries."

[46016] 8.14. Having said this much, Gubazes led out the army of the Lazi, and they were drawn up as follows. First the cavalry of the Lazi, drawn up, advanced opposite; and behind, not close at hand, but as far off as possible,

[46238] 8.15. the Roman cavalry followed them. And of these Romans the leaders were Philegagus, a Gepid by race, an energetic man, and John the Armenian, exceptionally good in matters of war, the son of Thomas, whom they called by the surname Guzes, of whom I have made mention in the earlier books as well.

[46539] 8.16. And behind followed Gubazes the king of the Lazi and Dagisthaeus the Roman general, with the foot-soldiers of both peoples, reckoning that, if it should befall the cavalry to be routed, they would most easily be saved by retiring upon them.

[46787] 8.17. The Romans, then, and the Lazi were drawn up in this manner, while Chorianes, picking out a thousand men of his followers, cuirassed and otherwise armed as well as possible, sent them forward on reconnaissance, while he himself with all the rest of the army came on behind, leaving a guard of a few men in the camp.

[47110] 8.18. But the cavalry of the Lazi, getting the start, dishonoured their promises by what they did, belying their former hopes in the actual deeds.

[47258] 8.19. For, having encountered the enemy's advance-guard suddenly, and not enduring the sight of them, they at once turned their horses and, in no order, returned backward, and, pressing toward the Romans, mingled with them, not disdaining to flee for refuge to those with whom formerly they had shrunk from being drawn up.

[47582] 8.20. And when both sides came close to one another, neither began the fighting first nor closed, but each, as the others advanced, drew back, and as they retreated, pressed on, and in retreats and renewed pursuits and quick-turning changes they wore away a great deal of time.

[47861] 8.21. Now there was a certain man, Artabanes by name, in this Roman army, a Persarmenian by race, who had deserted long before to the Armenians who are subjects of the Romans—not simply, but by the slaughter of a hundred and twenty Persian fighting men he had given the Romans a sure pledge of his faith toward them.

[48179] 8.22. For, coming into the presence of Valerian, who was then commanding in Armenia, he begged him to give him fifty Roman men; and obtaining what he wished, he went to a fort situated in Persarmenia,

[48381] 8.23. where a garrison of a hundred and twenty Persians received him with his followers under the fort, not yet aware that, having changed his allegiance, he was setting about a revolution.

[48572] 8.24. And he, having killed the hundred and twenty and plundered all the goods in the fort, which were exceedingly great, came to Valerian and the Roman army, and, appearing thereby trustworthy to the Romans, thenceforth campaigned with them.

[48816] 8.25. This Artabanes, in this battle, taking two of the Roman soldiers with himself, came into the no-man's-land, where some of the enemy too arrived.

[48968] 8.26. Against these Artabanes rushed, and one of the Persians, fairly great both in excellence of soul and in strength of body, he at once killed with his spear, and, hurling him from his horse, dashed him to the ground.

[49190] 8.27. But one of the barbarians, standing beside the fallen man, struck Artabanes on the crown of the head with his sword, a blow not mortal. And the other of those who followed Artabanes, a Goth by race, this man, while his hand was still on Artabanes' head, hitting him on the left flank, dispatched him.

[49498] 8.28. And the thousand, panic-stricken at what had happened, retired backward, and waited for Chorianes with the rest of the army of the Persians and Alans, and not long after mingled with them.

[49694] 8.29. And now the foot-soldiers about Gubazes and Dagisthaeus too came to their own cavalry, and the battle was hand to hand on both sides.

[49835] 8.30. Then indeed Philegagus and John, thinking themselves too few to bear the onset of the barbarian cavalry, and especially since they had given up hope of the Lazic force, leaping down from their horses, compelled all the Romans and Lazi to do the same.

[50093] 8.31. And drawing themselves up in as deep a phalanx as possible, they all stood on foot, front to front, opposite the enemy, holding their spears extended before them.

[50263] 8.32. And the barbarians, not knowing what to do—for they could neither ride down their opponents, who were now on foot, nor were they able to throw their phalanx into confusion, since their horses, distressed by the points of the spears and the clashing of the shields, reared up—all looked to their bows, confident in the hope that by the multitude of their missiles they would most easily turn the enemy to flight.

[50682] 8.33. And the Romans with all the Lazi did this same thing. And from both sides frequent volleys of arrows went against one another, and on both sides many fell.

[50845] 8.34. The Persians, then, and the Alans especially discharged their missiles much more continuously than their opponents. But most of them happened to be turned aside by the shields. And in this battle it befell Chorianes the commander of the Persians to be struck.

[51112] 8.35. By whom this man was struck has become clear to no one; for by some chance, coming out of the throng, the shaft, fixing itself in the man's neck, dispatched him straightway, and by the death of one man the battle was turned and the victory passed to the Romans.

[51381] 8.36. For he, falling from his horse upon his face to the ground, lay there, and at a great run the barbarians went toward the stockade, and the Romans with the Lazi, following after, killed many, hoping to take the camp of their adversaries at the first onset.

[51644] 8.37. But a certain one of the Alans, well endowed in courage and bodily strength, and exceptionally skilled in shooting many arrows on either side, stood in the entrance of the stockade, which was very narrow, and proved, beyond expectation, an obstacle for a very long time to the assailants.

[51940] 8.38. But John, the son of Thomas, alone, coming very close beside him, suddenly killed the man with his spear, and thus the Romans and Lazi gained possession of the camp. And of the barbarians very many were destroyed there, and the rest were carried off to their ancestral homes, as it was possible for each.

[52252] 8.39. This invasion of the Persians, then, into the land of Colchis ended thus. And another army of Persians, having strengthened the garrison in Petra with an abundance of provisions and all other things, withdrew.

[52659] 9.2. For they maintained that he, won over by the Persians, had not wished, when the circuit-wall of Petra had fallen, to mount upon it, and that the enemy, filling sacks with sand in the meantime and using these instead of stones as their building-material, had repaired all the parts of the wall that had fallen there.

[52981] 9.3. And they said that Dagisthaeus, whether brought to this by money or by negligence, had put off the attack to some other time, and let slip in the very instant the height of the opportunity, which he was no longer able to lay hold of.

[56269] 9.17. Fleeing to it, they always beat off the attacks of the enemy, who can in no way force the difficult ground. And there is a single entrance leading both into this fort and into the rest of the country of the Abasgi, which happens to be impassable for men coming two together.

[56551] 9.18. For there is no device by which one may go from here except man by man and on foot with difficulty. And above this path there hangs an exceedingly harsh ravine extending from the fort as far as the sea.

[56761] 9.19. And the place bears an appellation worthy of the ravine, since the men there, speaking Greek, call it Trachea (the Rough Place).

[56897] 9.20. Now the Roman fleet put in between the borders of the Abasgi and the Apsilii, and John and Uligagus, disembarking the soldiers onto the land, went on foot, while the sailors with all the boats followed the army along the shore.

[57132] 9.21. And when they came very close to the Trachea, they saw all the Abasgi armed and standing in array along the whole ravine above the path which I have just mentioned, and they were held by great helplessness, having no way at all to deal with the situation before them, until John, reckoning much within himself, found a certain cure for the trouble.

[57488] 9.22. For leaving Uligagus there with half the army, he himself, taking the rest, manned the boats. And rowing, they went round the whole Trachea and crossed it, and got behind the enemy there.

[58102] 9.24. And the Romans, following after them on both sides and overtaking them, killed many. And at a run, coming with the fleeing men to the fort, they found the gate there still open; for the guards had no way to close the doors, but were still receiving the fugitives.

[58373] 9.25. And the fugitives being mingled with the pursuers, all rushed for the gates, some in the desire of being saved,

[58492] 9.26. the others of taking the fort. Finding, then, the gates open, they burst in through them upon one another. For the gatekeepers could neither distinguish the Abasgi from the enemy, nor close the gates, the throng pressing over them by force.

[58740] 9.27. And the Abasgi, glad to be inside the circuit-wall, were captured together with the fort; but the Romans, thinking they had got the better of their adversaries, here met with a more troublesome task.

[58947] 9.28. For, the houses being many and not very far from one another, but in the manner of a wall fenced together on all sides, the Abasgi, going up onto them and defending themselves with all their might, struck the enemy from above, held by toil and fear and pity for their children and wives and the helplessness from this, until it came into the mind of the Romans to set fire to the houses.

[59342] 9.29. Kindling fire, then, upon them on all sides, they gained the complete mastery of this contest. Opsites, then, the ruler of the Abasgi, with a few men was able to flee, and withdrew to the neighbouring Huns and to the Caucasus mountain.

[59585] 9.30. But the rest were either burned to ashes together with the houses, or came under the hands of the enemy. And the Romans took alive too the wives of the rulers with all their offspring, and razed the circuit-wall of the fort to the ground and made the country empty of men for the most part. For the Abasgi, then, their revolt ended thus; and among the Apsilii the following things occurred.

[59983] 10.1. The Apsilii happen to be subjects of the Lazi from of old. And there is a certain fort in this country, exceedingly strong; the natives call it Tzibile.

[60143] 10.2. And a certain one of the notable men among the Lazi, Terdetes by name, who held the office of the so-called magister in this nation, having quarrelled with Gubazes the king of the Lazi and being ill-disposed, agreed secretly with the Persians to hand over this fort, and, bringing an army of Persians for this enterprise, went into Apsilia.

[60491] 10.3. And when they came near the fort, he himself, getting the start, with his Lazic followers came within the circuit-wall, since those who held the garrison there could in no way distrust the ruler of the Lazi, being held by no suspicion against him. And so Terdetes received the Persian army when it arrived at the fort.

[60817] 10.4. And from this the Medes supposed that not only Lazica but also Apsilia was then held by them. But neither the Romans nor the Lazi, hard pressed by occupation about Petra and the Median army, were able to defend the Apsilii.

[61048] 10.5. Now there was a certain wife of the ruler of the garrison there, an Apsilian by race, exceedingly comely of countenance. With this woman the commander of the Persian army, falling suddenly into an out-of-place passion, at first began to make trial of her; then afterwards, when nothing went forward for him on the woman's part, with no delay he undertook to do her violence.

[61430] 10.6. At this the woman's husband, in sharp anger, killed by night both him and all those who had come with him into the fort, who had become the waste-offering of the ruler's lust, and held the fort himself. And the Apsilii on this account revolted from the Colchians, charging that, when they were being ill-used by the Persians, they had in no way been willing to defend them.

[61811] 10.7. But Gubazes sent against them a thousand Romans and John the son of Thomas, of whom I have just made mention; and he, by much taming them, won them over without a battle and made them subjects of the Lazi again. So, then, the affairs concerning both the Apsilii and the fort of Tzibile went forward.

[62118] 10.8. About this time it befell Chosroes that not even his offspring remained untouched by his own inhumanity. For the eldest of his sons, Anasozadus by name (and this means in the Persian tongue 'the immortal one'), happened to have quarrelled with him, having sinned in many other ways by his lawlessness in his manner of life, and especially by going to bed with his father's women with no hesitation at all. At first, then, Chosroes punished this son with exile.

[62586] 10.9. And there is a certain country in Persia, Vazaine, exceedingly good, where there is settled a city called Belapaton, distant from Ctesiphon a journey of seven days.

[62758] 10.10. There, at his father's command, this Anasozadus was spending his time. And then it befell Chosroes to fall most grievously ill, so that it was even said that he had vanished from among men; for Chosroes was sickly by nature.

[62991] 10.11. In fact he often gathered physicians about himself from all quarters, among whom was Tribunus the physician, a Palestinian by race.

[63131] 10.12. Now this Tribunus was learned and in the matters of the medical art inferior to none, and otherwise temperate and god-loving and reaching the height of fairness.

[63301] 10.13. And once, having cured Chosroes when he was in a bad state of body, he departed from the land of the Persians, having received many gifts worthy of mention from the man.

[63479] 10.14. When, then, this former truce was made, Chosroes asked the emperor Justinian to give him this physician to live with him for a year. And when his request had been fulfilled, as I have said before, Chosroes ordered Tribunus to ask whatever he wished.

[63737] 10.15. And he asked for nothing of all riches except that Chosroes should release some of the Roman captives.

[63848] 10.16. And Chosroes released to him three thousand others, and as many notable men among the captives as he asked for by name; and from this deed Tribunus won great fame among all men. These things, then, happened thus.

[64069] 10.17. And when Anasozadus learned what had befallen Chosroes his father in the matter of his illness, aiming at the kingship, he was setting about a revolution.

[64232] 10.18. And though his father had recovered, none the less, having himself caused the city to revolt and taken up arms, he went to war at the height of his power.

[64395] 10.19. Hearing this, Chosroes sent an army and a general, Phabrizus, against him. Phabrizus, then, conquering in the battle and making Anasozadus subject, brought him to Chosroes not long after.

[64591] 10.20. And he maimed his son's eyes—not taking away his sight, but turning the eyelids, upper and lower, inside out with much unsightliness.

[64733] 10.21. For, having heated an iron pin red-hot, and with it smearing the outer parts of the boy's closed eyes, he thus was able to mar the comeliness of the eyelids.

[64899] 10.22. And Chosroes did this for this reason only, that for his son the hope of the kingship might be cut off. For a man held by mutilation the law no longer permits to be appointed king of the Persians, as has been said in the earlier books too.

[65147] 11.1. For Anasozadus, then, the matters both of his fortune and of his character ended thus; and the fifth year of the truce was completed.

[65288] 11.2. And the emperor Justinian sent Peter, a man of patrician rank, who held the office of magister, to Chosroes, in order that they might altogether settle the treaty concerning the East.

[65479] 11.3. But Chosroes sent him away, promising that a man would follow him not long after to arrange these matters in the way that would profit both sides.

[65633] 11.4. And he sent Isdigousnas again not long after, a man arrogant and held by an unspeakable boastfulness, whose conceit and swagger seemed bearable to no one of the Romans.

[65809] 11.5. And he brought with him his wife and his daughters and his brother, and a very great multitude of followers and attendants. One would have likened the men to those going into battle array.

[66005] 11.6. And there followed him also two of the most notable men among the Persians, who even wore golden diadems upon their heads.

[66135] 11.7. And it galled the men in Byzantium that the emperor Justinian thought fit to honour him not as an envoy, but much more, with both kindliness and magnificence.

[66301] 11.8. Bradukius, however, did not come to Byzantium with him, for they say that Chosroes made him vanish from among men, bringing nothing else against the man except that he had been a table-companion of the emperor of the Romans.

[66533] 11.9. "For," he said, "being an interpreter, he would not have come to such a height of rank from the emperor, unless he had betrayed the affairs of the Persians." But some say that Isdigousnas himself slandered him, on the ground that he had come secretly to a conference with the Romans.

[66824] 11.10. Now at first this envoy, meeting with the emperor, said nothing small or great about the peace, but accused the Romans of having done wrong against the truce, saying that Arethas and the Saracens who are subjects of the Romans had injured Alamundarus during the truce, and bringing other charges not worthy of mention, of which it seemed to me by no means necessary to make mention.

[67215] 11.11. In Byzantium, then, these things were being done. But Bessas, with all the Roman army, set about the siege of Petra. The Romans, then, were digging about the wall, at the place where Dagisthaeus too had formerly made his mine and brought down the circuit-wall there. But for what reason they dug at the same spot, I shall make plain.

[67557] 11.12. Those who originally built this city set the foundations of the circuit-wall for the most part upon rock, but in places it happened that they lay over an earthen mound.

[67734] 11.13. Now there was a certain portion of the wall on the western side of the city, not very wide, on each side of which they had built the foundations of the circuit-wall upon a certain hard and intractable rock.

[67949] 11.14. This portion, then, Dagisthaeus formerly and now Bessas dug at alike, the nature of the place not allowing them to go further, but measuring out for them the length of the mine and reasonably determining it.

[68165] 11.15. When, then, the Persians, after the withdrawal of Dagisthaeus, wished to rebuild this fallen part of the wall, they did not make the building in the former manner, but in the following way.

[68363] 11.16. Filling the emptied space with gravel, they set thick beams above it, which, having very thoroughly smoothed and made altogether even, they joined together over a great breadth, and, making these the base instead of foundations of the circuit-wall, they skilfully constructed the building above them. And the Romans, not understanding this, supposed they were making the mine below the foundations.

[68770] 11.17. And, emptying out the whole space from the beams which I have just mentioned over a great extent of the earth, they were able to bring down the circuit-wall to a great degree, and a portion of it suddenly fell; yet neither did this fallen part lean to one side, nor was any of the courses of stones thrown into confusion, but, sound and entire, by a straight descent, as if by a machine, going down into the emptied space, it stood, and kept its own place, not to such a height as before, but to something less.

[69290] 11.18. For the whole space under the beams being emptied, it befell them to settle there together with all the building above them.

[69423] 11.19. But not even so had the wall become passable for the Romans. For the throng of the Persians, when many came there with Mermeroes, putting a great mass upon the former building, constructed an exceedingly lofty circuit-wall.

[69655] 11.20. The Romans, then, when they saw the shaken part of the circuit-wall standing again, were at a loss and held by great helplessness.

[69794] 11.21. For they could neither dig any longer, the mine being shut off for them at this point, nor could they in any way use a ram, since they were assaulting the wall on rising ground, and this machine cannot be drawn except in a place level and very low.

[70051] 11.22. But by some chance it happened that there were in this Roman army a few of the barbarian Sabeiri, for the following reason.

[70183] 11.23. The Sabeiri are a Hunnic nation, and they are settled about the Caucasus mountains, being an exceedingly great multitude, but divided fairly into many rulerships.

[70354] 11.24. And of these rulers some are acquainted of old with the emperor of the Romans, others with the king of the Persians. And each of the two kings used to send a fixed sum of gold to his own allies—not yearly, however, but whenever the need led him to this.

[70616] 11.25. At that time, then, the emperor Justinian, calling upon those of the Sabeiri who were friendly to him for the alliance, sent a man to carry the money to them.

[70783] 11.26. And he—since, the enemy being between, he could in no way go to the Caucasus mountains in safety, especially carrying money—came to Bessas and the Roman army, which was set about the siege of Petra, and, sending to the Sabeiri, ordered some of them to come to him as quickly as possible to receive the money; and the barbarians, choosing three of their rulers, at once sent them with a few men into Lazica; and these, coming there with the Roman army, took part in this assault on the wall.

[71282] 11.27. These men, when they saw the Romans despairing and at a loss how to deal with the situation, devised a machine of a sort that had come into the mind of no Roman or Persian since men came into being, and yet a great throng of craftsmen has always existed, and now exists, in each state.

[71576] 11.28. And often in all time both have had need of this machine, when assaulting strongholds situated in hard and difficult places; but to none of them had this device come which has now come to these barbarians. So always, as time goes on, the nature of men is fond of devising new contrivances along with new affairs.

[71897] 11.29. For these Sabeiri improvise a ram, not in the way it was wont to be made,

[71979] 11.30. but innovating in another manner. For they did not put into this machine beams, neither upright nor crosswise, but, binding together certain thick rods to one another and fitting them everywhere instead of beams, and covering the whole machine with hides, they preserved the shape of the ram, suspending only a single beam, in the usual way, by certain loose chains in the middle of the machine, the end of which, made sharp and covered with iron like the point of a missile, was to be driven frequently against the circuit-wall.

[72517] 11.31. And they made the machine so light that it was no longer necessary for it to be drawn or pushed by the men inside, but forty men, who were to raise the beam up and drive it against the circuit-wall, being inside the machine and covered by the hides, carried the ram on their shoulders with no toil.

[72824] 11.32. Three such machines these barbarians made, taking the beams together with the iron from the rams which the Romans, having in readiness, were unable to drag up to the wall; and getting under each of them, no fewer than forty Roman soldiers, picked out for their valour, set them as close as possible to the wall.

[73144] 11.33. And on either side of each machine others stood, both cuirassed and with their heads accurately covered by helmets, holding poles whose ends had been fitted with hook-shaped iron pieces, prepared for this reason: that, whenever the impact of the ram against the circuit-wall should disjoint the courses of stones, they might be able with these poles to remove the loosened stones and throw them down.

[73553] 11.34. The Romans, then, were at the work, and the wall was now being shaken by frequent blows, while those on either side of the machines, with the hook-shaped poles, threw down the stones loosened from the structure of the building, and the city was very straightway likely to be taken.

[73843] 11.35. But the Persians devised this. A wooden tower, which had been prepared by them long before, they set above the circuit-wall, full of the most warlike men among them, who had girt their heads and the rest of their bodies with iron nails and cuirasses.

[74102] 11.36. And, filling vessels with sulphur and bitumen and the drug which the Medes call naphtha, and the Greeks the oil of Medea, and kindling these with fire, they cast them upon the machines of the rams, which they nearly all but set ablaze.

[74346] 11.37. But those standing beside these, as I have said, with the poles which I have just mentioned, most assiduously removing what was thrown and clearing it away, threw everything at once from the machines to the ground.

[74569] 11.38. Yet they suspected they could not hold out long against this work; for the fire, wherever it touched, burned at once, unless straightway thrown off. These things, then, were being done thus.

[74768] 11.39. But Bessas himself, cuirassed and having armed the whole army, brought up many ladders to the fallen part of the wall.

[74895] 11.40. And after exhorting them only so far as not to blunt the edge of the opportunity, he assigned the rest of the encouragement to deeds. For a man more than seventy years old and already altogether past his prime was the first to mount the ladder.

[75148] 11.41. Thereupon a battle and a display of valour took place between Romans and Persians such as I think happened nowhere in this period.

[75287] 11.42. For the multitude of the barbarians came together to two thousand three hundred, while the Romans happened to be about six thousand.

[75428] 11.43. And of these, on both sides, all who were not killed became wounded, almost; and it befell very few to survive with bodies unscathed. The Romans, then, forced the ascent with all their power, while the Persians beat them off with much toil.

[75677] 11.44. And, many being killed on both sides, the Persians came not far from repelling the danger; for, much pushing taking place at the topmost rungs of the ladders, many other Romans were dying, fighting as they were against enemies above them, and Bessas the general fell and lay on the ground.

[75975] 11.45. And then indeed, an extraordinary outcry arising on both sides, the barbarians, streaming from all sides upon him, struck at him; but his bodyguards rushed together eagerly around him, and, having helmets on their heads and all wearing cuirasses, and moreover, fencing themselves above with their shields and coming close to one another, they made the shape of a roof for him, and hid the general most safely, and beat off the missiles with all their might.

[76441] 11.46. And there was much clatter of the missiles ever sent and shattering against the shields and the other arms, and each man was held by shouting and panting and toil.

[76613] 11.47. And all the Romans, eager to defend the general, shot at the wall, letting no opportunity slip, and thereby checked the enemy.

[76748] 11.48. Then indeed Bessas (for he could not rise, his armour resisting, and especially since his body was not well-knit, for this man was fleshy and, as has been said, an extreme old man), did not fall into helplessness, although he had come into such danger, but on the sudden contrived something by which he was able to save both himself and the affairs of the Romans.

[77120] 11.49. For he ordered his bodyguards to drag him off by the foot and pull him as far as possible from the wall.

[77233] 11.50. And they did accordingly. And some of them dragged him, while others retired with him, holding their shields above themselves over one another, and making just so much pace as he was dragged, so that he should not be uncovered and struck by the enemy.

[77493] 11.51. And when Bessas was in safety, he rose and, exhorting them, went toward the wall, and, mounting the ladder again, started for the ascent.

[77639] 11.52. And all the Romans, following after, displayed deeds against the enemy worthy of valour. And the Persians, becoming exceedingly fearful, begged their opponents to grant them some opportunity, so that, packing up, they might depart, giving up the city.

[77899] 11.53. But Bessas, suspecting that they were contriving deceits, in order that meanwhile they might strengthen the fortification of the circuit-wall, said that he was unable to stop the engagement, but that those wishing to meet with him about the agreement, while the armies fought, might none the less go with him to some other part of the wall, and he showed them a certain place.

[78284] 11.54. And, they not accepting the proposal, again a fierce battle took place and much pushing; and while the engagement was still in even balance, it befell the wall to fall suddenly elsewhere, at the place where the Romans had previously made their mine. There, then, many from both sides streamed together.

[78595] 11.55. And the Romans, surpassing the enemy greatly in number, although divided in two, pressed much more vehemently upon their adversaries, both shooting and pushing most stoutly.

[78777] 11.56. But the Persians no longer held out alike, being forced on both sides, but their scantiness of men, divided into both parts, was manifest.

[78924] 11.57. And while both armies were still toiling thus, and neither the Persians being able to beat off the enemy pressing upon them, nor the Romans being altogether able to force the entrance, a young man, an Armenian by race, John by name, son of Thomas, whom they called by the surname Guzes, leaving the fallen parts of the circuit-wall and the pushing there, took a few of the Armenians who followed him through the precipitous part, where all supposed the city to be impregnable, and, forcing his way, mounted upon the guards there.

[79462] 11.58. And, coming to be among the battlements, he killed with his spear one of the Persians defending there, who seemed to be the most warlike; and so it befell the Romans to enter in the following manner.

[79670] 11.59. The Persians who stood in the wooden tower kindled a great mass of the fire-bearing vessels, in order that with what was left of the missiles they might be able to burn the machines together with the men, since the defenders could not push everything off with their poles.

[79951] 11.60. But a wind of the south, harsh and exceedingly violent, suddenly falling upon them from the opposite quarter with much crashing, somehow caught one of the planks of the tower.

[80135] 11.61. And the Persians there not understanding it at once (for all were held by toil and tumult and fear and boundless confusion, and necessity took away their perception), the flame, rising little by little, by means of the oil named after Medea and the other things with which it had been prepared, burned the whole tower and the Persians there.

[80485] 11.62. And, charred to ashes, they all fell, some inside the circuit-wall, others outside it, where the machines and the Romans about them stood; and thus the rest of the Romans too, all those who were fighting at the fallen parts of the circuit-wall, the enemy yielding to them and having fallen into negligence, got inside the circuit-wall, and Petra was taken by storm.

[80859] 11.63. Of the Persians, then, about five hundred, running up to the citadel and seizing the stronghold there, remained quiet, while the rest the Romans, all whom they did not kill in the engagement, took alive—being about seven hundred and thirty in number.

[81118] 11.64. And of these they found eighteen unscathed, but all the rest happened to be wounded. And of the Romans too there fell many, and the best, and John the son of Thomas, struck on the head with a stone at the entrance into the city by one of the barbarians, after he had displayed wondrous deeds against the enemy.

[81437] 12.1. On the following day the Romans, besieging the barbarians who had seized the citadel, made proposals, offering them their safety and agreeing to give them pledges concerning this, in this way expecting that the Persians would surrender themselves to them.

[81700] 12.2. But they, not accepting the proposals, looked to resistance, although they thought they could not hold out long against the hardship, but were ready to die for valour's sake.

[81882] 12.3. And Bessas, wishing to turn them from this purpose and set them instead toward the desire for safety, ordered one of the Roman soldiers to go as near to them as possible and make some exhortation to the men, indicating what he wished to say to them.

[82139] 12.4. And he, coming very close, spoke as follows: "What have you suffered, best of the Persians, to strain so toward this destruction of your own selves, pursuing what is deadly with senseless eagerness and manifestly dishonouring the pursuits of valour? For it is not manly to resist the impossible, nor sensible to be unwilling to yield to the victors; nor indeed is it without honour to live in submission to the fortune that is present.

[82582] 12.5. For necessity, even when it has met with no good hope, justly escapes dishonour, even if it be clothed with the most shameful of deeds; for forgiveness is wont to follow the evil that is unavoidable.

[82789] 12.6. Do not, then, emulate desperation in manifest danger, nor exchange safety for boastfulness, but consider that it is impossible for the dead to come to life again, while the survivors will in time destroy themselves later too, if indeed this should seem to be the better course.

[83074] 12.7. Take counsel, then, the last counsel, and look round for what is profitable, knowing this: that those would be the best of counsels in which it shall be in the power of the counsellors to repent.

[83277] 12.8. For we pity you even while you struggle, and spare you when you would die, and think it right to have mercy on you as you balk and shrink before life, in accordance with the law of the Romans, who are Christians.

[83497] 12.9. And nothing else will remain to you, except that, changing your allegiance for the better, you will have Justinian as master instead of Chosroes; for concerning these things we agree to give you pledges.

[83708] 12.10. Do not, then, destroy your own selves, when it is possible to be saved. For it is not fortunate to dwell in dangers to no profit at all, since this is not to play the man, but to court death.

[83908] 12.11. And he is noble who endures the most terrible things, when he expects something useful from it. For men do not praise voluntary death, where a hope greater than the danger in it lies; but the overthrow of life is a useless and rash folly, and senseless boldness toward death seems to the prudent no seemly mask for the energetic man.

[84250] 12.12. And yet this too must be reckoned, lest you should seem also to be ungrateful toward the divine. For if it had wished to destroy you, men, it would not, I think, have handed you over to those who are eager to save you.

[84477] 12.13. Such, then, are our offers; but you, clearly, will take counsel of what it befalls you to be worthy."

[84587] 12.14. The exhortation was somewhat thus. But the Persians would not even so much as receive the words by hearing, but feigned deafness on purpose and pretended that they did not understand.

[84779] 12.15. And then, at the general's command, the Romans cast fire into the citadel, thinking in this way the enemy would surrender their bodies to them.

[84931] 12.16. And, the flame advancing greatly, the barbarians, the calamity being before their eyes, well knowing that they would very straightway be reduced to ashes, having no hope, nor knowing in what way they could be saved by defending themselves, yet not even so were willing to come under the hands of the enemy, but together with the citadel all at once, while the Roman army marvelled at what was being done, were burned up and destroyed.

[85374] 12.17. And with what great eagerness Chosroes was then carrying on the affairs of Lazica became plain; for he had picked out the most notable of all his soldiers and stationed them in the garrison of Petra, and had stored up arms in such quantity that, when the Romans plundered them, there fell to each soldier the equipment of five men—and yet much had happened to be burned in the citadel too.

[85772] 12.18. And there was found also a great quantity of grain and salted meats and of the other provisions, such as would suffice all the besieged for five years.

[85932] 12.19. But wine the Persians had not happened to store up there, except sour wine and an abundant supply of beans.

[86048] 12.20. And when the Romans found also water flowing in there from the aqueduct, they fell into great wonder and were at a loss, until they learned the whole account concerning the hidden aqueducts. And what this is I shall now make plain.

[86288] 12.21. When Chosroes, having taken Petra, set up the garrison there, knowing well that the Romans would besiege it by every device, and would at once attempt with no delay to cut the aqueduct, he devised the following.

[86508] 12.22. This water which was led into the city he divided into three parts, and, making a very deep trench, he constructed three aqueducts, the one below, at the bottom of this trench, covering it with dung and stones up to the middle of the trench; and there, having hidden the second, above it he built the third, which was above ground and visible to all; so that the aqueduct was secretly threefold.

[86912] 12.23. And the Romans, not understanding this at the beginning of the siege, cut this visible aqueduct, and, not carrying further the toil upon the mine, but ceasing from the work before the loss from there, supposed that the besieged were short of water, the negligence that goes with shrinking from toil deceiving their judgment.

[87245] 12.24. But, the siege being prolonged, the Romans, capturing some of the enemy, learned that the besieged drew their water from the aqueduct.

[87388] 12.25. Digging, then, into the place, they find the second aqueduct lying there somewhere, and, cutting this, at once supposed that thereby they had altogether overthrown the power of the enemy, not even being taught the former lesson by the second instance in the instruction it gave.

[87675] 12.26. And when, having taken the city, they saw the water flowing, as I have said, from the aqueduct, they marvelled and were held by great helplessness.

[87831] 12.27. And, learning what had happened from the captives, they perceived, after the event, both the enemy's diligence in their works and their own negligence in the matter of toil.

[88013] 12.28. All the captives, then, Bessas at once sent to the emperor, and razed the circuit-wall of Petra to the ground, so that the enemy might not again cause them trouble.

[88186] 12.29. And the emperor praised him exceedingly for the valour he had shown and for his good counsel, in that he had razed the whole wall.

[88325] 12.30. Bessas, then, again became, both for his successes and for the display of valour he had made, a man looked up to among all men.

[88461] 12.31. For when he had been stationed at the garrison of Rome, the Romans had great hope in him as one who had passed his earlier life most bravely.

[88611] 12.32. But when it befell him to fail there—Rome being thus captured by the Goths, as I have said in the earlier books, and the Roman race for the most part destroyed—the emperor Justinian, when he returned to Byzantium, thus appointed him general against the Persians.

[88882] 12.33. And almost all, so to speak, disparaged the act and mocked the emperor's counsel, that he should commit the Median war to this Bessas, who had been utterly defeated by the Goths and had become an aged man at the grave's edge, at the setting of his life.

[89144] 12.34. But, though almost all had thought this, it befell this general to enjoy such good fortune and such valour. So, then, human affairs are administered not in the way that seems good to men, but by the impulse from God, which men are wont to call fortune, not knowing for what reason events come forward in the way that becomes apparent to them.

[89495] 12.35. For to what seems to be contrary to reason the name of fortune is wont to attach. But let these things seem to each as is dear to him.

[89638] 13.1. But Mermeroes, fearing lest something untoward should befall Petra and the Persians left there through the length of time, set out with all his army and went there, since the season after the time of winter led him to this.

[89869] 13.2. But meanwhile, learning all that had happened, he refrained altogether from this road, knowing well that, outside the river Phasis, there was no other place belonging to the Lazi than the one at Petra.

[90078] 13.3. And turning back and seizing the entrances from Iberia into the land of Colchis, where the Phasis is fordable, and crossing it himself on foot, and likewise a river named the Rheon, which is not navigable there either, and coming to be on the right of the Phasis here, he led the army against a city named Archaeopolis, which is the first and greatest among the Lazi.

[90453] 13.4. And they were all, except a few, cavalry, and eight elephants followed them, upon which the Persians, standing, were to strike the enemy from above, as from towers.

[90625] 13.5. So that one might with reason marvel at the toil and ingenuity of the Persians in matters of war, who made the road leading from Iberia into Colchis—a road shut in everywhere by precipitous glens and difficult wooded places, and covered with woods so dense that even to a well-girt man it had formerly seemed impassable—so level that not only all their cavalry could go from there with no toil, but they could even campaign there bringing along as many of the elephants as they wished.

[91118] 13.6. And there came to them also as allies twelve thousand Huns of those called Sabeiri.

[91209] 13.7. But Mermeroes, fearing lest these barbarians, being so great a multitude, should neither be willing to obey him when he gave commands, but should even do some irreparable thing to the Persian army, allowed four thousand of them to campaign with them, and the rest, having presented them with much money, he let go to their ancestral homes.

[91556] 13.8. And the Roman army was twelve thousand, but not all gathered into one; rather, in the garrison at Archaeopolis there were three thousand, whom Odonachus and Babas commanded, both good in matters of war;

[91766] 13.9. while the rest, encamping within the outlets of the river Phasis, remained, with this purpose: that if at any point the enemy's army should make an attack, they themselves, rising up from there, might bring aid with all their power.

[92006] 13.10. And these were commanded by Benilus and Uligagus; and with them was also Varazes the Persarmenian, just come from Italy, whom eight hundred Tzani followed.

[92170] 13.11. For Bessas, as soon as he had taken Petra, was no longer willing to toil, but, withdrawing to the Pontici and the Armenians, gave his attention as much as possible to the revenues from his command, and by this petty avarice he again brought to ruin the affairs of the Romans.

[92454] 13.12. For if, having conquered straightway then, as I have said, and taken Petra, he had gone to the borders of the Lazi and the Iberians and fenced off the difficult places there, no Persian army, it seems to me, would any longer have gone into Lazica.

[92710] 13.13. But now this general, neglecting this toil, had all but handed over Lazica with his own hands to the enemy, caring little for the emperor's anger.

[92865] 13.14. For the emperor Justinian was wont to be indulgent in most cases toward commanders who sinned, and from this they were for the most part caught transgressing the law both in their manner of life and in their administration.

[93097] 13.15. Now there were two forts of the Lazi close to the very borders of Iberia, Scanda and Sarapanis, which, lying in certain difficult and altogether troublesome places, happened to be exceedingly hard of access.

[93313] 13.16. These the Lazi anciently guarded with much toil, since there grows nothing edible there at all, but men, carrying the provisions on their shoulders, brought them in.

[93487] 13.17. But the emperor Justinian, at the beginning of this war, having removed the Lazi from there, set up a guard of Roman soldiers.

[93622] 13.18. And these, not long after, hard pressed by want of necessities, abandoned these forts, since they could in no way subsist on millet for a long time, like the Colchians, this not being their custom; and the Lazi, going a long road and carrying all the provisions to them, no longer held out.

[93921] 13.19. And the Persians, seizing them, held them; and in the treaty the Romans got them back, making the exchange for the fort of Bolus and Pharangium, as all these things have been told by me in the earlier books.

[94137] 13.20. The Lazi, then, razed these forts to the ground, so that the Persians might not have them as fortified posts against themselves. But the Persians, building one of them, the one which they call Scanda, again held it, and Mermeroes led the Median army forward.

[94404] 13.21. Now there was a city in the plain, Rhodopolis by name, which was the first to confront those invading Colchis from Iberia, exceedingly easy of approach and most assailable.

[94585] 13.22. Wherefore the Lazi, fearing long before the attack of the Persians, had razed it to the ground. And when the Persians learned this,

[94725] 13.23. they went straight for Archaeopolis. But Mermeroes, learning that the enemy were encamping about the outlets of the river Phasis, marched against them.

[94885] 13.24. For it seemed to him better, having first destroyed these, thus to set about the siege of Archaeopolis, lest they, going behind, should harm the Persian army.

[95052] 13.25. And coming very close to the circuit-wall of Archaeopolis, he greeted, jesting with, the Romans there, and made some boastful threat that he would very straightway return to them.

[95240] 13.26. For he said that he wished first to address the other Romans, who were encamped about the river Phasis.

[95352] 13.27. And they, answering, bade him go where he wished, but maintained that, if he met the Romans there, he would never return to them.

[95490] 13.28. When the commanders of the Roman army learned this, they were terrified, and, thinking themselves too few to bear the power of the assailants, embarking in the boats prepared for them, they all ferried across the river Phasis, putting into the boats such of the provisions present with them as they could carry, and casting the rest into the river, so that the enemy might not be able to revel in them.

[95901] 13.29. Mermeroes, then, coming there with all his army not long after, and seeing the camp of the enemy altogether empty, was vexed and, being at a loss, was distressed.

[96072] 13.30. And, burning the stockade of the Romans and boiling with rage, he turned back at once and led the army against Archaeopolis.

[96205] 14.1. Archaeopolis lies upon a certain exceedingly hard hill, and a river flows past it, coming down from mountains which are above the city.

[96348] 14.2. And of its gates the lower ones lead to the foothills of the hill, not inaccessible, however, but only that the ascent to them from the plain is not level; while the upper ones, leading out to the precipitous part, are exceedingly hard of access; for wooded places are before these gates, extending a very great distance.

[96677] 14.3. And since the inhabitants there have no other water at all, those who built the city constructed two walls from it down to the river, so that they might in safety draw the water of the river. Mermeroes, then, eager and straining to assault the wall there with all his might, did the following.

[96978] 14.4. First he ordered the Sabeiri to make a great number of rams, of a kind that men could carry on their shoulders, since he could in no way bring the customary machines up to the circuit-wall of Archaeopolis, lying as it did at the foot of the mountain; and he had heard what had been made by the Sabeiri, the allies of the Romans, against the wall of Petra not long before, and, following the contrivances, he pursued the benefit from experience. And they did what was ordered.

[97461] 14.5. And they at once constructed many rams, in the way I have just said the Sabeiri made for the Romans. Then he sent the so-called Dolomitae against the precipitous parts of the city, ordering them to harass the enemy there with all their might.

[97711] 14.6. Now these Dolomitae are barbarians settled in the midst of the Persians, yet they have never become subjects of the king of the Persians.

[97856] 14.7. For, established in mountains steep and altogether impassable, being independent, they have continued from of old down to the present time; but, serving for hire, they always campaign with the Persians when these go against their enemies.

[98102] 14.8. And they are all foot-soldiers, each man carrying a sword and a shield and three javelins in his hands.

[98213] 14.9. And they know how to run exceedingly well both among the crags and over the heights of the mountains, as on a level plain.

[98343] 14.10. And on this account Mermeroes set them to assault the wall there, while he himself with all the rest of the army went against the lower gates, bringing up the rams and the elephants.

[98534] 14.11. The Persians, then, with the Sabeiri, shooting frequently at the wall and covering the air there with their arrows, came not far from compelling the Romans there to abandon the battlements.

[98732] 14.12. And the Dolomitae, hurling their javelins from the crags outside the circuit-wall, distressed the enemy opposite them much more.

[98869] 14.13. And everywhere the affairs of the Romans had become grievous and full of dangers, as they suffered the extremes of evils.

[98999] 14.14. Then indeed Odonachus and Babas, whether displaying their valour or wishing to make trial of the soldiers, or whether some divine power moved them, left a few of the soldiers, whom they ordered to defend from the battlements against those assaulting the wall, and, calling together the most, made a brief exhortation and spoke as follows: "The present danger, fellow-soldiers, and the necessity that has encompassed us, you see.

[99436] 14.15. And we must by no means yield to these evils. For those who have come into despair of safety—this alone could save them: not to be eager for safety; since for the man fond of life destruction for the most part is wont to follow.

[99673] 14.16. And this too you will need to consider amid the present dangers: that if we defend against the enemy from these battlements, the matter of our safety will not lie on a sure footing, even if we carry through the contest most eagerly.

[99914] 14.17. For a battle waged from separated positions allows no man to play the hero, but turns for the most part to the dominion of fortune.

[100054] 14.18. If, however, the engagement should become a close one, eagerness will for the most part prevail, and victory will go with valour.

[100192] 14.19. Apart from this, those fighting from the circuit-wall, even if they succeed in the engagement, would not greatly profit from their success, since for the moment it befalls them to beat off the enemy, but on the morrow the danger again comes to its height, and, gradually worsted, as is likely,

[100494] 14.20. they are destroyed together with their fortifications. But those who have conquered their adversaries hand to hand will thenceforth have their safety in security. Considering these things, let us go against the enemy with all eagerness, bringing the help from above to our aid, and become of good hope from the very despair that has now fallen upon us.

[100855] 14.21. For those who have no hope of safety at all in themselves the divine is always especially wont to save."

[100968] 14.22. Having exhorted them this much, Odonachus and Babas opened the gates and led out the army at a run, a few being left behind there for the following reason.

[101132] 14.23. One of the Lazi the day before—a notable man in this nation, but settled in Archaeopolis—was negotiating with Mermeroes concerning the betrayal of his fatherland.

[101303] 14.24. And Mermeroes ordered him to do the Persians no other service except that, when they should set about the assault on the wall, he should secretly burn the houses where the grain and the rest of the provisions were stored.

[101533] 14.25. And he gave these orders, reckoning that one of two things would happen: either the Romans, busy and wasting time about this fire, would let them mount the circuit-wall at will, or the Persians, wishing to beat off those defending the wall, would make light of these houses;

[101816] 14.26. and, the grain and the other provisions being burned in this way, they would with no toil of siege take Archaeopolis in a short time.

[101958] 14.27. With such a purpose, then, Mermeroes gave these orders to this Lazus; and he agreed to fulfil the command, whenever he should see the assault on the wall at its height, by kindling fire as secretly as possible in these dwellings.

[102196] 14.28. And the Romans, seeing the flame suddenly rising, a few brought aid there, and with much toil quenched the fire, which had done some damage, while all the rest, as has been said, went against the enemy.

[102407] 14.29. Falling upon them suddenly and confounding them by the unexpectedness, they killed many, who neither defended themselves nor dared to lift their hands against them.

[102580] 14.30. For the Persians, having no hope at all that the enemy, being very few, would come out against them, stood separated from one another in disorder, as they had been drawn up for the assault on the wall.

[102790] 14.31. And those who carried the rams on their shoulders were unarmed and unprepared for battle, as was likely, while the rest, holding their strung bows in their hands, had no device by which to defend against the enemy who pressed upon them in close array.

[103050] 14.32. Thus the Romans, cutting them down, dispatched them as they wheeled about. And it happened then too that one of the elephants, having been struck, as some say, or thrown into confusion of its own accord, wheeled about in no order and reared, and threw its riders and broke the array of the rest.

[103354] 14.33. And from this the barbarians drew back, and the Romans, more fearlessly, kept dispatching those ever in their path.

[103478] 14.34. And one might here justly wonder, if the Romans, who knew well in what way they had to beat off the attack of the enemy by means of the elephants, did none of the things they should, plainly thrown into confusion by the present circumstances, while this befell them of its own accord. And what this is, I shall now make plain.

[103813] 14.35. When Chosroes and the Median army were assaulting the circuit-wall of Edessa, one of the elephants, a great throng of the most warlike of the Persians having mounted upon it, coming near the circuit-wall, was likely, after a little, to overpower the defenders from the tower there—who were struck frequently from above—and to take the city.

[104162] 14.36. For such a machine seemed to be a city-taker. But the Romans, hanging a pig from the tower, escaped this danger.

[104283] 14.37. For the pig, being, as was natural, suspended, emitted a certain squealing from there, at which the elephant, distressed, reared and, drawing back little by little, retreated backward. So, then, that happened thus.

[104506] 14.38. But now fortune was filling out what had been let slip by the negligence of the Romans. But since I have made mention of Edessa, I shall not pass over in silence the portent which happened there before this war.

[104726] 14.39. For when Chosroes was about to break the so-called Endless Peace, a certain woman in the city was bearing a babe in other respects fairly of human form, but having two heads. And this became plain by the events that followed.

[104960] 14.40. For Edessa and almost all the East, and toward the north wind the greater part of the Roman empire, became a thing fought over by two kings. These things, then, happened thus. But I return to the point from which I digressed.

[105194] 14.41. When the confusion thus fell upon the Median army, all of them who had been drawn up in the rear, seeing the confusion of those in front, but in no way learning what had happened, were terrified and turned to flight with much disorder.

[105438] 14.42. And the Dolomitae too, suffering this same thing (for, fighting from the heights, they saw all that was being done), fled a shameful flight, and the rout became brilliant.

[105618] 14.43. And four thousand of the barbarians fell there, among whom three of the commanders happened to be, and the Romans took four of the Persian standards, which they at once sent to the emperor in Byzantium.

[105829] 14.44. And they say that of their horses no fewer than twenty thousand perished, not shot or struck by the enemy, but having gone a long road, and met with much toil on it, and, when they came into Lazica, having by no means come to satiety of fodder, and so, hard pressed by famine and great weakness, having perished.

[106150] 14.45. Failing of this attempt, Mermeroes withdrew with all his army to Mocheresis, since, failing also of Archaeopolis, the Persians for the most part held the mastery of the rest of Lazica.

[106343] 14.46. And Mocheresis is distant from Archaeopolis a journey of one day, having many and populous villages—and this is especially the best of the land of Colchis; since here both wine and the other fruits grow good, although the rest of Lazica is not so.

[106599] 14.47. A river flows past this country, Rheon by name, by which the Colchians had built a fort of old, the greater part of which they themselves afterwards razed to the ground, since, lying in an exceedingly low-lying plain, it seemed to them easy of approach.

[106861] 14.48. And the fort was then named Cotiaeon in the tongue of the Greeks, but now the Lazi call it Cotais, corrupting the harmony of the name through their ignorance of the language. These things Arrian thus recorded.

[107079] 14.49. But others say that the place was in olden times a city and was called Coetaeum; and that from there Aeetes set out, and that on account of this the poets call him the Coetaean and the land of Colchis the Coetaean land.

[107307] 14.50. Mermeroes, eager now to rebuild this, since he had no preparation for the work, and at the same time winter was already pressing, having made of wood, as quickly as possible, such parts of the fort as had fallen, remained there.

[107544] 14.51. And very close to Cotais is an exceedingly strong fort, Uthimereos by name, in which the Lazi accurately held a garrison.

[107674] 14.52. And there shared with them in the guard of the fort also a few Roman soldiers.

[107761] 14.53. Mermeroes, then, with all his army sat there, holding the fairest parts of the land of Colchis, and proving an obstacle to his opponents in bringing any provisions into the fort of Uthimereos, or going to the country called Suania and Scymnia,

[108013] 14.54. although it was subject to them. For, the enemy being in Mocheresis, it befell the Lazi and Romans to be cut off from the road to the regions there. The armies, then, were thus carried on in Lazica.

[108220] 15.1. And in Byzantium the envoy of Chosroes, Isdigousnas, conferring with the emperor Justinian about the peace, wasted much length of time.

[108363] 15.2. And after much disputing they at last came to an agreement, on condition that there should be a five-year truce in the dominions of each king, and that, going to and fro to one another from both sides and treating without fear in this time, they should settle the differences concerning Lazica and the Saracens.

[108682] 15.3. And it was agreed that the Persians should receive from the Romans, on behalf of the truce of these five years, twenty centenaria of gold, and on behalf of the eighteen months which happened to elapse in the interval after the former truce until this one, while each side was sending envoys to the other, another six centenaria;

[109018] 15.4. for on this condition the Persians said they too had consented that the discussions about the treaty should take place.

[109145] 15.5. And these twenty centenaria Isdigousnas thought fit to carry off at once, but the emperor wished to give four each year, for this reason: that he might have a pledge against Chosroes' transgressing the agreement.

[109365] 15.6. Afterwards, however, the Romans gave the Persians at once all the gold agreed upon, in order not to seem to be paying them tribute every year.

[109515] 15.7. For men are wont for the most part to be ashamed of the shameful names, not of the things.

[109613] 15.8. And there was a certain man among the Persians, Berzabus by name, exceptionally notable and most dear to the king Chosroes.

[109744] 15.9. Whom once Valerian, when he had fallen in an engagement in Armenia, took as a spear-captive, and at once sent to the emperor in Byzantium.

[109890] 15.10. And much time was spent while he was kept under guard there. And it was Chosroes' wish to give a great amount of money for him, in order that he might see Berzabus returned to the homes of the Persians.

[110101] 15.11. But now, Isdigousnas having asked for him, the emperor Justinian released the man; for this envoy promised the emperor that he would persuade Chosroes to remove the Persian army from Lazica.

[110300] 15.12. And this truce was made between the Romans and the Persians while the emperor Justinian was holding the imperial power for the twenty-fifth year.

[110454] 15.13. And at this treaty most of the Romans were fairly vexed. And whether they made their complaint with justice, or without reason, as is the way with subjects, I cannot say.
