[110831] 17.20. But some say that he escaped no other disease than that madness which held him after he killed his own mother. But I return to my former narrative.

[110987] 17.21. From the Taurian region of Armenia and the country of Celesene the river Euphrates, flowing on the right, encompasses much land, and other rivers mingling with it, and the Arsinus itself, which is borne in great volume from the so-called Persarmenians—having become great, as is likely—goes to those called of old the White Syrians, now the lesser Armenians, of whom the first city, Melitene, is worthy of much account.

[111415] 17.22. From there it flows past Samosata and Hierapolis and all the places there as far as the land of the Assyrians, where both rivers, mingling with one another, are distinguished under the name of the Tigris.

[111628] 17.23. The country, then, which is outside the Euphrates river from Samosata, was of old called Commagene, but now is named after the river. And that within it, which is the middle between it and the Tigris, is called Mesopotamia, as is likely; a portion of it, however, is called not by this name only, but also by certain others.

[111961] 17.24. For the part as far as the city of Amida is by some called Armenia, and Edessa with the places about it is called Osroene after Osroes, a man who reigned there in former times, when the people there were in treaty with the Persians.

[112202] 17.25. The Persians, then, when they had taken from the Romans the city of Nisibis and certain other places of Mesopotamia, whenever they were going to make an expedition against the Romans, holding in little account the country outside the Euphrates river, since it is for the most part waterless and empty of men, and gathering there with no trouble, since they were in their own country and very near the inhabited part of the enemy's land, from there they always made their invasions.

[112692] 17.26. When the mirranes, defeated in battle and having lost most of his men, came with the rest of the army to the country of the Persians, he received a bitter punishment from King Cabades.

[112885] 17.27. For he took from him the ornament which he was wont to bind upon the hair of his head, made of gold and pearls. This is a great dignity among the Persians, second only to the honour of the king.

[113088] 17.28. For there it is not lawful to use either golden ring or belt or brooch or anything else, except for one deemed worthy by the king.

[113227] 17.29. And for the rest Cabades took counsel in what way he himself might make an expedition against the Romans. For since the mirranes had failed as has been said, he had no confidence in any other.

[113428] 17.30. And as he was at a great loss, Alamoundaras, King of the Saracens, came to him and said: "Not in all things, O master, ought one to trust to fortune, nor to think that one must succeed in all wars. For this is not even likely, nor otherwise human, but this thought is most inexpedient to those who hold it.

[113743] 17.31. For those who have hoped that all good things will be theirs, having failed at some time, should it so chance, hope, having reckoned things otherwise than is fitting, has grieved beyond what was proper.

[113954] 17.32. Therefore men, not having always confidence in fortune, do not enter openly into the danger of war, even though they boast that they wholly surpass the enemy, but are eager to outwit their adversaries by deceit and certain devices.

[114194] 17.33. For to those who have danger on equal terms the matters of victory do not proceed in security. Now then, O King of Kings, neither be so grieved at the misfortunes of the mirranes, nor wish again to make trial of fortune.

[114423] 17.34. For of Mesopotamia and the country called Osroene, as being very near your borders, the cities are most strong of all, and they now have such a multitude of soldiers as never before, so that for us, going there, the matters of the contest will not be in safety; but in the country which is outside the Euphrates river, and in Syria which adjoins it, there is neither a strong city nor an army worth mentioning.

[114842] 17.35. For these things I have often heard from the Saracens sent there on reconnaissance.

[114934] 17.36. There, they say, is the city of Antioch, first of all the cities in the East of the Romans in wealth and size and population; which is unguarded and empty of soldiers.

[115110] 17.37. For its people care for nothing else than festivals and luxury and their perpetual rivalry with one another in the theatres.

[115243] 17.38. So that if we go against them unexpectedly, it is not at all unlikely that we shall take the city in a raid, and, having met no army of the enemy, then return to the country of the Persians, the soldiers in Mesopotamia not yet having learned what has befallen.

[115512] 17.39. And let no anxiety about the want of water or any other of the necessaries come upon you; for I will lead the army wherever it seems best."

[115660] 17.40. Hearing this, Cabades had no power either to gainsay or to disbelieve. For Alamoundaras was most intelligent and well versed in the experience of war, most faithful to the Persians and exceedingly effective, who for the space of fifty years brought the affairs of the Romans to their knees.

[115959] 17.41. For beginning from the borders of Egypt and as far as Mesopotamia plundering the places there, he led and carried off all in succession, burning the buildings in his path and enslaving the people always by many myriads, and killing most of them with no account, but selling the others for great sums.

[116268] 17.42. And no one of all opposed him. For he never made his inroad without consideration, but so suddenly and to his own advantage, that he was already, as a rule, departing with all the booty, while the generals and soldiers were only beginning to learn what had befallen and to gather against him.

[116569] 17.43. And if anywhere they chanced to overtake him, this barbarian, falling upon the pursuers while they were still unprepared and not drawn up, routed and destroyed them with no trouble, and once took alive the pursuing soldiers together with their commanders.

[116833] 17.44. These were Timostratus, the brother of Rufinus, and John the son of Lucas, whom he afterward sold, gaining for himself no mean or ordinary wealth.

[116988] 17.45. And in a word this man became most hard and most terrible an enemy to the Romans, beyond all others. The cause was that Alamoundaras, holding the dignity of king, alone of all the Saracens among the Persians had command, and was able with his whole army always to make his inroad wherever he wished of the Roman realm.

[117315] 17.46. But no commander of the Roman soldiers, whom they call dukes, nor any leader of the Saracens in treaty with the Romans, who are called phylarchs, was able with his followers sufficiently to draw up against Alamoundaras; for in each country they were stationed not a match for the enemy.

[117610] 17.47. Therefore the Emperor Justinian set Arethas the son of Gabalas over as many tribes as possible, who ruled the Saracens in Arabia, investing him with the dignity of king, a thing that had never before happened among the Romans.

[117845] 17.48. Alamoundaras, however, no less, if not more, ruined the affairs of the Romans, since Arethas, in every inroad and contest, either fared most ill or turned traitor most quickly. For we do not yet know anything clearly about him. And in this way it befell that Alamoundaras, with no one opposing him, plundered all the East for a very long time, since he was simply most long-lived.

[118234] 18.1. Pleased then at the time with this man's counsel, Cabades, choosing out fifteen thousand men, set over them Azarethes, a Persian man, exceedingly valiant in war, and ordered Alamoundaras to lead the way of the march for them.

[118467] 18.2. And they, crossing the Euphrates river into Assyria, and having traversed a certain country empty of men, suddenly and unexpectedly invaded the country called of the Commageni.

[118651] 18.3. And this was the first invasion of the Persians from there into the land of the Romans, so far as we know either by report or in any other way, and it struck all the Romans with consternation by its unexpectedness.

[118873] 18.4. When Belisarius learned this, at first he was at a loss, but afterward resolved to bring help quickly. And setting a sufficient garrison in each city, so that Cabades and another army of the enemy, going there, should by no means find the places in Mesopotamia unguarded, he himself with the rest of the army went to meet them, and crossing the Euphrates river they advanced with much haste.

[119272] 18.5. The Roman army came together to about twenty thousand foot and horse, and of them the Isaurians were not fewer than two thousand.

[119409] 18.6. The commanders of the horse were all those who before had carried through the battle at Daras against the Persians and the mirranes, and of the foot one of the bodyguards of the Emperor Justinian, Peter by name.

[119628] 18.7. Over the Isaurians Longinus and Stephanacius were set. There Arethas too came to them with the army of the Saracens.

[119752] 18.8. And when they had come to the city of Chalcis, they encamped and remained there, since they learned that the enemy were at a place called Gabboulon, distant one hundred and ten stades from Chalcis.

[119957] 18.9. When Alamoundaras and Azarethes learned this, dreading the danger, they no longer advanced, but resolved to withdraw homeward at once. They themselves, then, keeping the Euphrates river on the left, rode back, and the Roman army followed behind.

[120210] 18.10. And in the place where the barbarians bivouacked each night, the Romans always remained on the night following.

[120330] 18.11. For Belisarius on purpose by no means allowed the army to travel a longer road, since it was not his wish to come to blows with the enemy, but he thought it sufficient for them that the Persians and Alamoundaras, having invaded the land of the Romans, then having thus withdrawn from it,

[120626] 18.12. should make their way home unsuccessful. Therefore all secretly reviled him, both commanders and soldiers, yet no one reproached him to his face.

[120780] 18.13. Finally the Persians bivouacked on the bank of the Euphrates, which is opposite the city of Callinicum. For from there they were going to march through a country inhabited by no men, and thus to depart from the land of the Romans.

[121019] 18.14. For they no longer purposed to go as before, holding to the bank of the river. The Romans, having passed the night in the city of Sura and rising from there, came upon the enemy already packing up for the departure.

[121243] 18.15. Now the festival of Easter was at hand, due on the day next following, which the Christians revere most of all, and on the day before it, abstaining from food and drink, they are accustomed not only to pass the whole day, but also to go far on into the nights fasting.

[121520] 18.16. Then, therefore, Belisarius, seeing all eager against the enemy, and wishing to draw them off from this resolve (for Hermogenes, just come from the emperor on an embassy, was of the same mind), having called together all who were present, spoke as follows: "Whither are you carried,

[121811] 18.17. men of Romans, or what has befallen you that you take counsel to choose a danger not necessary for yourselves? Men think the one unalloyed victory is to suffer nothing dreadful at the hands of the enemy, which in the present case both fortune and our enemies' fear of us have given us.

[122105] 18.18. Therefore it is better to enjoy the present goods than, having let them pass, to seek them. For the Persians, led by many hopes, made an expedition against the Romans, but now, having fallen short of all, have rushed to flight.

[122341] 18.19. So that if we compel them unwillingly to change their purpose of retreat, and to come to blows with us, having conquered we shall gain nothing more at all.

[122505] 18.20. For why should one turn back him who is already fleeing? But if we fail, perhaps we shall be deprived of the victory we have, not having had it taken away by the enemy, but having ourselves thrown it away; and we shall for the future give over the emperor's land to lie exposed to the enemy without defenders.

[122823] 18.21. And yet it is worth your while to consider this also, that God is wont always to help men in necessary, not in self-chosen, dangers.

[122964] 18.22. And apart from this, for those who have nowhere to turn, it will come about that they play the man even unwilling; but for us many obstacles to the engagement happen to be present;

[123153] 18.23. for many of us have come going on foot, and all of us happen to be fasting. I forbear to say that some are not even now present." So much Belisarius said.

[123316] 18.24. But the army insulted him not in any silence nor in a corner, but coming into his presence with shouting they called him soft and a destroyer of their zeal; and in this some even of the commanders erred together with the soldiers, in this way displaying their daring.

[123592] 18.25. And Belisarius, struck with consternation at their shamelessness, reversing his exhortation, seemed now to be urging them on against the enemy and arranging them into line, and said that he had not known their zeal for fighting before, but now took courage and with the better hope went against the enemy.

[123906] 18.26. And making the phalanx front-ways, he arrayed it as follows. On the left wing, by the river, he set all the foot; and on the right, where the ground was steep, Arethas and all the Saracens with him; and he himself with the horsemen stood at the centre. Thus the Romans were arrayed.

[124197] 18.27. And Azarethes, when he saw the enemy coming together into line, exhorted as follows: "That you, being Persians, would not exchange the virtue of life, if anyone gave the choice of the two, no one would deny.

[124413] 18.28. But I say that not even if you wished would it be in your power to make the choice of these two. For those to whom it is possible, escaping the danger, to live with dishonour, there is nothing unlikely, if they wish, that they should choose pleasantest things in place of the best; but those to whom it is necessary to die, either gloriously at the hands of the enemy, or being shamefully led to punishment by the master, it is great folly not to choose the better in place of the most shameful.

[124917] 18.29. Since, then, these things are so, it befits all of you, I think, having in mind not the enemy only, but also your own master, thus to go into this battle."

[125081] 18.30. So much also Azarethes having exhorted, he set the phalanx facing the enemy, the Persians holding the right, the Saracens the left. And at once both sides came to blows.

[125259] 18.31. And the battle was most fierce. For the arrows, shot thick from both sides for a great extent, made much slaughter on both, and some, getting into the space between, displayed deeds worthy of valour against one another; but rather did many of the Persians die from the arrows.

[125544] 18.32. For their missiles were simply far more frequent, since the Persians are nearly all archers and are taught to make their shots much more quickly than all other men;

[125717] 18.33. but being shot from bows soft and not very tight-strung, falling upon a corselet perhaps or helmet or shield of a Roman man, they were broken off and could in no way harm him whom they struck.

[125918] 18.34. But the missiles of the Romans are ever slower; yet being shot from bows exceedingly hard and terribly tight-strung, and one might add from stronger men, they injure far more easily than the Persians whomever they strike, no armour being able to stand in the way of their rush.

[126204] 18.35. Already, then, two portions of the day had passed, and the battle was still even. But then, those of the Persian army who were the best, taking counsel together, drove against the right wing of the enemy, where Arethas and the Saracens were drawn up.

[126463] 18.36. And these, thus breaking the phalanx, became divided in two, so that they even bore away the repute of having betrayed the Romans' cause to the Persians. For not withstanding the assailants, they at once all rushed to flight.

[126697] 18.37. The Persians, then, having thus broken through the line of their adversaries, came straightway upon the rear of the Roman horse. But the Romans, already wearied both by the road and by the toil of the battle, and being all fasting up to this hour of the day, and being harassed by the enemy on both sides, no longer held out, but most of them, fleeing at full force, made for the islands of the river, which were very near, while some, even remaining where they were, performed marvellous deeds worthy of much account against the enemy.

[127242] 18.38. Among these was Ascan too, who, having killed many of the men of note among the Persians, and being cut to pieces little by little, hardly fell, leaving great repute of himself to the enemy. And with him eight hundred others, having proved brave men, died in this toil, and the Isaurians together with their commanders nearly all, not having even dared to lift their arms against the enemy.

[127641] 18.39. For they were held by much inexperience of this work, since they had just left their farming and entered the danger of war,

[127773] 18.40. unknown to them before. And yet these very men, lately raging for battle through ignorance of war, then reproached Belisarius with cowardice. They were not, however, all Isaurians, but the most were Lycaonians.

[127992] 18.41. Belisarius, remaining there with a few, so long as he saw those under Ascan holding out, himself too with those present defended himself against the enemy;

[128156] 18.42. but when of them some fell, and others rushed to flight where they could, then he too with his followers, fleeing, came to the phalanx of the foot, who with Peter were still fighting, being not many, since most of these too had chanced to flee.

[128409] 18.43. There he let his horse go and bade all his followers do the same, and on foot together with the rest defend themselves against the assailants.

[128560] 18.44. And those of the Persians who followed the fugitives, having made the pursuit for a short space, at once returned, and went against the foot and Belisarius with all the rest. And they, turning their backs to the river, that no encircling might come upon them from the enemy, from the present situation defended themselves against the assailants.

[128914] 18.45. And again the battle became fierce, although not on equal terms of strength. For the foot, and very few of them, fought against the whole Persian horse. Yet the enemy could neither rout them nor otherwise force them.

[129139] 18.46. For ever drawing close to one another into a small space, and most strongly fencing themselves with their shields, they shot at the Persians more conveniently than they themselves were shot at by them.

[129349] 18.47. And often the barbarians, despairing, drove against them, as though to throw into confusion and break up the line, but unsuccessful they rode back again from there.

[129522] 18.48. For their horses, distressed by the clatter of the shields, reared up and were thrown into confusion together with their riders. And thus both sides held on until it was late in the day.

[129717] 18.49. And when night had now come on, the Persians withdrew to their camp, and Belisarius, finding a merchant vessel, with a few crossed to the island of the river, where the rest of the Romans too came, swimming.

[129933] 18.50. On the next day the Romans, many merchant vessels coming to them from the city of Callinicum, were conveyed into it, but the Persians, having stripped the dead, all withdrew homeward. They did not, however, find their own dead fewer than the enemy's.

[130192] 18.51. And Azarethes, when he came with the army to the Persians, although he had had good fortune in the battle, met with an exceedingly ungrateful Cabades, for the following cause.

[130376] 18.52. It is a law among the Persians that, whenever they are about to make an expedition against any of the enemy, the king sits upon the royal throne, and many baskets are there somewhere, and the general who is reckoned likely to lead the army against the adversaries is present, and this army passes before the king's sight man by man, and each of them throws one missile into the baskets; after which these, sealed with the king's seal, are guarded, and whenever this army returns to the Persians, each of the soldiers takes one missile from the baskets.

[130937] 18.53. Then, counting how many of the missiles have not been taken up by the men, those on whom this office is laid announce to the king the number of soldiers who have not returned, and in this way it becomes manifest how many have died in the war.

[131188] 18.54. Such, then, is the law among the Persians from of old. And when Azarethes came into the king's sight, Cabades asked him whether he came having gained for him any Roman place, since he had made the expedition against the Romans with Alamoundaras to subdue Antioch. And Azarethes said that he had taken no place, but that he had conquered the Romans and Belisarius in battle.

[131570] 18.55. Cabades, then, bade the army with Azarethes pass before him, and each took up a missile from the baskets, as was the custom.

[131703] 18.56. And many missiles being left behind, the king reproached Azarethes with the victory and for the rest held him among the most dishonoured. So the matter of the victory came to this for Azarethes.

[131906] 19.1. At that time it came into the mind of the Emperor Justinian to make allies of the Ethiopians and the Homerites against the Persians. But where in the earth these peoples dwell, and how the emperor hoped they would profit the Romans, I now go on to relate.

[132169] 19.2. The borders of Palestine toward the rising sun reach to the sea called Red.

[132252] 19.3. This sea, beginning from the Indians, ends here at the Roman realm. And there is a city called Aila by its shore, where the sea, as I have said, coming to an end, becomes a very narrow strait. And to one sailing in from there, on the right are the mountains of the Egyptians, turned toward the south wind; on the other side a country empty of men reaches for a great extent toward the north wind, and this land becomes visible to the one sailing in on both sides, as far as the island called Iotabe, distant from the city of Aila not less than a thousand stades.

[132822] 19.4. There Hebrews dwelt independent from of old, but under this Justinian's reign they have become subject to the Romans.

[132947] 19.5. From there a great open sea receives one. And those sailing in there no longer see the land on the right, but on the left, night always coming on, they put in to anchor.

[133124] 19.6. For it is impossible to sail in the dark on this sea, since it happens to be full of shoals for a great extent.

[133243] 19.7. There are many anchorages there, made not by men's hands, but by the nature of the places, and from this it is not difficult for those sailing to put in wherever they chance. To one who has crossed the borders of Palestine, this shore is straightway held by the Saracens,

[133522] 19.8. who from of old are settled in the Palm-grove.

[133576] 19.9. The Palm-grove is in the interior, stretching over a great country, where nothing at all else grows except palms alone.

[133703] 19.10. This Palm-grove Abocharabos, the ruler of the Saracens there, gave to the Emperor Justinian, and the emperor set him as phylarch over the Saracens in Palestine.

[133872] 19.11. And he guarded the country unravaged throughout the whole time, since Abocharabos seemed ever terrible both to the barbarians subject to him and no less to the enemy, and was exceedingly effective.

[134078] 19.12. In word, then, the emperor holds the Palm-grove, but to have any share of the places there is in no way possible for him.

[134208] 19.13. For a country wholly empty of men and simply waterless lies in the midst, reaching to a ten days' journey, and the Palm-grove itself is in no way worth anything; rather, Abocharabos gave only the name of a gift, and the emperor, knowing this well, received it. So the matter of the Palm-grove stands thus.

[134522] 19.14. Other Saracens, neighbours of these men, hold the coast, who are called Maddeni, being subject to the Homerites.

[134643] 19.15. These Homerites dwell in the country beyond, by the shore of the sea. And beyond them many other nations are said to be settled, as far as the man-eating Saracens.

[134815] 19.16. After whom are the races of the Indians. But about these let each tell as he pleases.

[134909] 19.17. Opposite the Homerites, very nearly, on the mainland over against them, dwell the Ethiopians, who are called Auxomites, because their royal seat is in the city of Auxomis.

[135089] 19.18. And the sea that is between, when a moderately favouring wind falls upon it, reaches to a passage of five days and nights.

[135220] 19.19. For there they are accustomed to sail even by night, since there is no shoal anywhere there; this sea is by some called Red. For the part outside it, to one sailing out as far as the shore and the city of Aila, is named the Arabian Gulf.

[135466] 19.20. For the country from there as far as the borders of the city of Gaza was called Arabia of old, since in former times the king of the Arabs had his royal seat in the city of Petrae.

[135655] 19.21. The harbour of the Homerites, from which they are wont, setting out, to sail to the Ethiopians, is named Boulicas.

[135778] 19.22. And having crossed this open sea, they always put in at the harbour of the Adulitae. Adulis the city is distant from the harbour a measure of twenty stades (for by so much it is cut off from being on the sea), but from the city of Auxomis a journey of twelve days.

[136051] 19.23. The ships, however, that are among the Indians and on this sea are not made in the same fashion as other ships. For they neither use pitch nor anything else, nor indeed are the planks fastened together to one another with iron going through, but they are bound together with certain cords.

[136349] 19.24. The cause is not what most suppose, that there are certain rocks there that draw the iron to themselves (the proof: for to the ships of the Romans sailing from Aila into this sea, although fitted with much iron, it never befell to suffer any such thing), but that the Indians and Ethiopians have neither iron nor any other of the things suitable for this.

[136713] 19.25. Nor indeed are they able to buy any of these from the Romans, it being expressly forbidden by law to all.

[136827] 19.26. For death is the penalty for one who is caught. So the matter concerning the so-called Red Sea, and the country which is on either side of it, stands thus.

[136991] 19.27. From the city of Auxomis to the borders of the Roman realm at Egypt, where the city called Elephantine is inhabited, is a journey of thirty days for an unencumbered man.

[137169] 19.28. There many other nations are settled, and the Blemyes and the Nobatae, very populous races. But the Blemyes dwell in the middle of this country, and the Nobatae hold the parts about the river Nile. Formerly these were not the limits of the Roman realm, but beyond by about seven other days' journey;

[137477] 19.29. but when the Emperor of the Romans Diocletian, coming there, perceived that the tribute of the places there was of no account, since the land happens to be very narrow there (for rocks not far from the Nile, rising exceedingly high, hold the rest of the country), and that a very great multitude of soldiers had been settled there from of old, with whose expenses the treasury was burdened beyond measure, and at the same time the Nobatae, settled about the city of Oasis, formerly used to lead and carry off all the places there for ever, he persuaded these barbarians to rise from their own homes and settle about the river Nile, agreeing to gift them with great cities and a country much and exceedingly better than that they had inhabited before.

[138236] 19.30. For thus he thought both that they themselves would no longer trouble the places about the Oasis, and that, holding to the land given them as their own, they would, as was likely, repel both the Blemyes and the other barbarians.

[138473] 19.31. And since these things pleased the Nobatae, they made the migration at once, just as Diocletian charged them, and they held both the cities of the Romans and all the country on either side of the river from the city of Elephantine.

[138713] 19.32. Then this emperor appointed that there be given them and the Blemyes every year a fixed sum of gold, on condition that they should no longer plunder the land of the Romans.

[138894] 19.33. Which, although they receive it even to my time, they none the less overrun the places there. So no device can preserve the faith toward the Romans for all barbarians, except the fear of soldiers defending.

[139109] 19.34. And yet, finding also an island in the Nile river very near the city of Elephantine, this emperor built there a most strong fortress, and established certain common temples and altars there for the Romans and for these barbarians, and set priests of each in this fortress, thinking that their friendship would be secure by their sharing in the rites together.

[139477] 19.35. Therefore he named the place Philae. And both these nations, the Blemyes and the Nobatae, worship all the other gods which the Greeks hold, and reverence Isis and Osiris, and not least Priapus.

[139679] 19.36. The Blemyes, however, are accustomed even to sacrifice men to the sun. These temples in Philae these barbarians held even to my time, but the Emperor Justinian resolved to pull them down.

[139875] 19.37. Narses, then, a Persarmenian by race, whom I mentioned before as having deserted to the Romans, being commander of the soldiers there, pulled down the temples at the emperor's bidding, and kept the priests in custody, and sent the images to Byzantium. But I return to my former narrative.

[140172] 20.1. About the time of this war Hellestheaeus, the King of the Ethiopians, being a Christian and exceedingly zealous for this faith, when he learned that of the Homerites on the mainland over against him many were Jews, and many worshipped the ancient faith which men nowadays call Greek, and were using a measureless plotting against the Christians there, gathering a fleet of ships and an army went against them, and conquering in battle killed both the king and many of the Homerites, and having set up another Christian king there, a Homerite by race, named Esimiphaeus, and appointed him to bring tribute to the Ethiopians every year, withdrew homeward.

[140833] 20.2. Of this Ethiopian army many slaves and as many as were suited to wickedness by no means wished to follow the king, but stayed behind there out of desire for the country of the Homerites; for it is exceedingly good.

[141055] 20.3. This people not long after, with certain others, rose against King Esimiphaeus, and shut him up in one of the fortresses there, and set up another king for the Homerites,

[141233] 20.4. Abramus by name. This Abramus was a Christian, but a slave of a Roman man who in the city of the Ethiopians, Adulis, occupied himself with the trade by sea.

[141397] 20.5. When Hellestheaeus learned this, being eager to punish Abramus together with those who had risen with him for their injustice toward Esimiphaeus, he sent against them an army of three thousand men and one of his own kinsmen as commander.

[141642] 20.6. This army, no longer willing to return home but to remain there in a good country, came secretly, without the commander's knowledge, to a parley with Abramus, and when they had come into the engagement with the adversaries, as soon as they were in the work, killing the commander they mingled with the army of the enemy and remained there.

[141989] 20.7. Hellestheaeus, held by much anger, sent another army against them, who, coming to blows with those under Abramus and being far worsted in the battle, at once withdrew homeward. And for the rest the King of the Ethiopians, fearing, no longer made an expedition against Abramus.

[142273] 20.8. And on the death of Hellestheaeus, Abramus agreed to pay tribute to him who received the kingship of the Ethiopians after him, and thus secured his rule. But these things took place at a later time.

[142479] 20.9. At that time the Emperor Justinian, while Hellestheaeus reigned among the Ethiopians and Esimiphaeus among the Homerites, sent an envoy Julian, asking that both, through their oneness of faith, should join with the Romans in their war against the Persians, in order that the Ethiopians, buying the silk from the Indians and selling it to the Romans, should themselves become masters of great wealth, while they would make the Romans gain only this, that they would no longer be compelled to transfer their own money to the enemy (this is the silk from which they are wont to make the garment which the Greeks of old called Median, but now name Seric), and that the Homerites should set up Caïsus the fugitive as phylarch over the Maddeni, and with a great army both of the Homerites themselves and of the Saracens of the Maddeni invade the land of the Persians.

[143348] 20.10. (This Caïsus was of the phylarchic race and exceedingly valiant in war, but having killed one of the kinsmen of Esimiphaeus he had fled to the land which is

[143513] 20.11. altogether empty of men.) Each of them, then, promising to fulfil the request, dismissed the envoy, but neither did what had been agreed.

[143659] 20.12. For it was impossible for the Ethiopians to buy the silk from the Indians, since the Persian merchants, ever being present at the very harbours where the Indian ships first put in, since they dwell in the adjoining country, are wont to buy up all the cargoes; and to the Homerites it seemed difficult, traversing a country empty and reaching a journey of much time, to go against men far more warlike.

[144069] 20.13. And Abramus later too, when he had most securely strengthened his rule, often promised the Emperor Justinian to invade the land of Persia, but only once began the march, and at once withdrew back. So the affairs of the Ethiopians and the Homerites turned out thus for the Romans.

[144357] 21.1. Hermogenes then, as soon as the battle by the Euphrates had taken place, coming to Cabades on an embassy, accomplished nothing concerning the peace for which he came, since he found him still swelling against the Romans; therefore he withdrew unsuccessful.

[144621] 21.2. And Belisarius came to the emperor at Byzantium, summoned, stripped of the office he held, in order that he might make an expedition against the Vandals.

[144782] 21.3. And Sittas, this having been resolved by the Emperor Justinian, came there to guard the East.

[144883] 21.4. And the Persians again invaded Mesopotamia with a great army, the chanaranges and Aspebedes and Mermeroes leading them.

[145010] 21.5. And since no one dared to come to blows with them, settling down before Martyropolis they besieged it, where Bouzes and Bessas chanced to have been stationed for the guard.

[145190] 21.6. This lies in the country called Sophanene, distant from the city of Amida two hundred and forty stades toward the north wind; and it is by the Nymphius river, which divides the land of the Romans and the Persians.

[145411] 21.7. The Persians, then, assaulted the wall, and the besieged at first manfully withstood them, but were reckoned likely not to hold out long.

[145556] 21.8. For the wall was very assailable for the most part and easily to be taken by a siege of the Persians, and they themselves did not have provisions sufficiently, nor indeed engines nor anything else adequate with which to defend themselves.

[145802] 21.9. Sittas and the Roman army came to a place Attachas, distant a hundred stades from Martyropolis, but did not dare to go forward; rather, encamping there, they remained.

[145977] 21.10. And Hermogenes was with them, having again come on an embassy from Byzantium. At this point it befell that the following thing happened.

[146122] 21.11. From of old it has been a custom among both the Romans and the Persians to maintain spies at public expense, who are wont to go secretly among the enemy, in order that, having scanned exactly what is being done, then returning they may report it to their rulers.

[146393] 21.12. Of these many, as is likely, are eager to use goodwill toward their own people, but some even betray their secrets to the adversaries.

[146536] 21.13. At that time, then, a certain spy sent from the Persians to the Romans, coming into the presence of the Emperor Justinian, told many other things being done among the barbarians, and that a race of Massagetae, to the Romans' hurt, was about to go very soon to the country of the Persians, and from there, coming into the land of the Romans, was ready to mingle with the Persian army.

[146928] 21.14. And he, hearing this, having already made trial of the man's truthfulness toward him, gifting him with some ample sums of money, persuaded him to go to the Persian camp which was besieging the Martyropolitans, and to announce to the barbarians there that these Massagetae, persuaded by money by the Emperor of the Romans, were about presently to come against them.

[147301] 21.15. And he did accordingly, and coming to the camp of the barbarians announced to the chanaranges and the others that an army of hostile Huns would before long come to the Romans.

[147485] 21.16. And they, when they heard this, were dismayed and at a loss in their present circumstances.

[147585] 21.17. At this point it befell that Cabades fell grievously sick in body, and calling one of the Persians most intimate with him, Mebodes by name, he conferred with him about Chosroes and the kingship, and said that he feared lest the Persians might be eager to disregard what he had resolved.

[147880] 21.18. And he thought fit to leave the declaration of his purpose in writing, taking courage that the Persians would never dare to disregard it.

[148026] 21.19. So Cabades plainly ordained that Chosroes be made king for the Persians. And Mebodes himself wrote the document, and Cabades at once vanished from among men.

[148192] 21.20. And when all the customary rites had been performed at the burial of the king, Kaoses, trusting in the law, laid claim to the honour, but Mebodes prevented him, saying that no one ought to go to the kingship of his own accord, but by the vote of the men of note among the Persians.

[148482] 21.21. And he entrusted the decision of the matter to the magistrates, suspecting that thence no obstacle would arise for him.

[148610] 21.22. But when all the men of note among the Persians, gathered for this, were seated, Mebodes, reading the document, declared the purpose of Cabades concerning Chosroes, and all, remembering the virtue of Cabades, at once proclaimed Chosroes king of the Persians.

[148877] 21.23. Thus Chosroes obtained the rule. But in Martyropolis Sittas and Hermogenes, fearing for the city—for they had no way to defend it in its peril—sent certain men to the enemy, who, coming into the presence of the generals, spoke as follows:

[149124] 21.24. "You have, unawares to yourselves, become an obstacle, against need, to the King of the Persians and to the goods of peace and to both states. For envoys sent by the emperor are now present, to the end that, going to the King of the Persians, they may dissolve the differences and make a treaty with him; but rise as quickly as possible from the land of the Romans and allow the envoys to do what is going to profit both sides.

[149560] 21.25. For we are ready, concerning these very things, to give as hostages men of repute, so that the thing will not long after be brought to effect in deed." So much the envoys of the Romans said.

[149759] 21.26. And it chanced that a messenger from the palace too came to them, who reported that Cabades had died, and that Chosroes son of Cabades had been set as king for the Persians,

[149941] 21.27. and that for this reason their affairs were in suspense. And from this the generals gladly heard the words of the Romans, having also feared the inroad of the Huns. The Romans, then, at once gave as hostages Martin and one of the bodyguards of Sittas, Senecius by name; and the Persians, breaking up the siege, made their withdrawal straightway.

[150295] 21.28. And the Huns not long after, invading the land of the Romans, since they did not find the Persian army there, having made their raid for a short time, all carried themselves off home.

[150487] 22.1. At once Rufinus and Alexander and Thomas came to act as envoys together with Hermogenes, and all arrived at the Tigris river before the King of the Persians.

[150652] 22.2. And when Chosroes saw them, he released the hostages. And the envoys, taming Chosroes, said many alluring things by no means befitting Roman envoys.

[150808] 22.3. By these Chosroes, becoming tractable, agreed to make with them an endless peace for a hundred and ten centenaria, on condition that the commander of the soldiers in Mesopotamia should no longer be at Daras for the future, but pass the whole time at Constantina, just as had been the custom of old; the fortresses in Lazica, however, he said he would not give back, though he himself thought it just to recover both Pharangium and the fortress of Bolon from the Romans.

[151285] 22.4. Now the centenarium weighs a hundred pounds, from which indeed it has its name. For a hundred the Romans call centum.

[151410] 22.5. He thought fit that this gold be given him, so that the Romans should neither be compelled to dismantle the city of Daras nor share with the Persians the guard-post at the Caspian Gates.

[151604] 22.6. The envoys, however, praised the rest, but said they were not able to hand over the fortresses, unless they first learned the emperor's mind about them.

[151764] 22.7. It was resolved, then, that Rufinus should be sent to Byzantium about these things, and the others, until he himself should return, should remain. And a space of seventy days was agreed upon for Rufinus's return.

[151984] 22.8. And when Rufinus, coming to Byzantium, reported to the emperor what seemed good to Chosroes about the peace, the emperor ordered that the peace be concluded with them accordingly.

[152171] 22.9. But meanwhile a certain rumour, not true, coming to the country of the Persians, announced that the Emperor Justinian, being angry, had killed Rufinus. At this Chosroes, thrown into confusion and now held by much anger, went against the Romans with his whole army. But Rufinus, returning in the meanwhile, met him not far from the city of Nisibis.

[152526] 22.10. Therefore both they came into this city, and, since they were going to ratify the peace, the envoys brought the money there.

[152659] 22.11. But the Emperor Justinian already repented of having conceded the fortresses of Lazica, and wrote to the envoys a letter expressly forbidding them by any means to give these up to the Persians.

[152861] 22.12. Therefore Chosroes no longer thought fit to make the treaty, and then a thought came to Rufinus that he had brought the money into the land of the Persians having taken counsel more quickly than safely.

[153072] 22.13. At once, then, he let his body down to the ground, and lying prone he besought Chosroes both to send the money back with them and not to make the expedition against the Romans at once, but to defer the war to some other time.

[153306] 22.14. And Chosroes bade him rise from there, promising to grant him all these things. The envoys, then, with the money came to Daras, and the Persian army rode back.

[153474] 22.15. And then the fellow-envoys held Rufinus in much suspicion and slandered him to the emperor, inferring that Chosroes had conceded to him everything he wished, persuaded by him.

[153658] 22.16. The emperor, however, did him no harm for these things. And not long after Rufinus himself and Hermogenes were again sent to Chosroes, and at once they came to terms with one another for the treaty, on condition that each side give back as many places as both had taken from one another in this war, and that there should no longer be any command of soldiers at Daras; and to the Iberians it was granted to be in their own judgement either to remain there in Byzantium, or to return to their own fatherland. And there were many both who remained and who returned to their native homes.

[154252] 22.17. Thus, then, they concluded the so-called Endless Peace, the Emperor Justinian now holding the kingship in the sixth year.

[154382] 22.18. And the Romans gave the Pharangium and the fortress of Bolon, together with the money, to the Persians, and the Persians gave the Romans the fortresses of Lazica. The Persians also gave Dagaris back to the Romans, having received in exchange for him another man, not obscure.

[154666] 22.19. This Dagaris at a later time often conquered the Huns who had invaded the land of the Romans, and drove them out in battle. For he was exceedingly valiant in war. Thus, then, both sides ratified the treaty with one another in the manner described.

[154922] 23.1. At once it befell each emperor that a plot arose against him from his subjects; in what manner, I shall presently show. Chosroes son of Cabades was disorderly in mind and a strange lover of new things.

[155131] 23.2. Therefore he himself was ever full of disturbance and tumult, and was the foremost cause of the like in all others.

[155254] 23.3. Vexed, then, at his rule, all who among the Persians were energetic, took counsel to set up another king for themselves from the house of Cabades.

[155408] 23.4. And (for there was among them a great longing for the rule of Zames, whom the law, on account of the maiming of his eye, as I have said, excluded) reckoning, they found it better for themselves to set up Cabades, his son and namesake of his grandfather, in the rule, and Zames, as being guardian of the boy, to administer the affairs of the Persians as he wished.

[155779] 23.5. And coming to Zames they declared their design, and with much eagerness urging him on led him to the deed. And since the man was pleased with the plan, they purposed to set upon Chosroes at the right moment. But the plan, becoming known as far as the king, prevented what was being done.

[156074] 23.6. For Chosroes both killed Zames himself and all his own brothers and those of Zames together with every male offspring, and as many of the men of note among the Persians as had taken counsel against him either to rule or to share in the rule in whatever way. Among these too was Aspebedes, the brother of Chosroes's mother.

[156404] 23.7. Cabades, however, the son of Zames, he had no way himself to kill. For he was still being reared under the chanaranges Adergoudounbades. And he charged the chanaranges himself to make away with this boy whom he had reared. For he neither thought it just to distrust the man, nor was otherwise able to force him.

[156723] 23.8. The chanaranges, then, when he heard the commands of Chosroes, being exceedingly grieved and bewailing the misfortune, conferred with his wife, the nurse of Cabades, all that the king had charged him. And the woman, weeping and taking hold of her husband's knees, besought him by no device to kill Cabades.

[157037] 23.9. Taking counsel, then, among themselves, they reckoned that they should rear the boy hidden as securely as possible, and signify quickly to Chosroes that Cabades had vanished from among men.

[157234] 23.10. And they signified this to the king accordingly, and so hid Cabades that they gave perception of it to no one except their own son Varrames and one of their household servants, who seemed to them most trustworthy of all.

[157463] 23.11. But when, as time went on, Cabades came to manhood, the chanaranges, fearing lest what had been done should be brought to light, gave Cabades money and bade him depart and save himself wherever he could in his flight. At that time, then, the chanaranges escaped the notice of Chosroes and all the rest in having done these things.

[157802] 23.12. At a later time Chosroes invaded the land of Colchis with a great army, as shall be written in my later books.

[157921] 23.13. And there followed him the son of this chanaranges, Varrames, bringing with him others of the household servants, and the one who chanced to share with him the knowledge concerning Cabades; there Varrames told the king everything about Cabades, and produced the servant agreeing with him in all things.

[158232] 23.14. When Chosroes learned this, he was now held by extraordinary anger, and thought it monstrous that he should have suffered such deeds at the hands of a slave; and not having any way to get the man into his power, he devised the following.

[158478] 23.15. When he was about to withdraw homeward from the land of Colchis, he writes to this chanaranges that it had been resolved by him to invade the land of the Romans with his whole army, not, however, by one entrance into the country, but having divided the Persian army in two, so that the invasion against the enemy should be both within and outside the Euphrates river.

[158854] 23.16. With one portion of the army he himself, as was likely, would lead the way into the enemy's land; but to no other of his slaves would he give equal honour with the king in this dignity, except to the chanaranges himself on account of his valour.

[159108] 23.17. He must therefore, on the return march, come quickly into his presence, so that, having conferred with him, he might charge him everything that was going to profit the army; and he should bid those with him go on behind by the road.

[159349] 23.18. When the chanaranges saw this brought to him, overjoyed at the honour of the king toward him, and far removed from his own evils, he at once performed what was enjoined.

[159527] 23.19. But on this road, being by no means able to hold out against the toil (for the man was very old), letting go the rein of his horse he falls and the bone in his leg was broken; therefore it was necessary for him to remain there quietly being tended, and into this place he came into the presence of the king as he arrived.

[159857] 23.20. And Chosroes said to him that it was impossible for him to make the expedition with them while in such state of his foot, but that he must go to one of the fortresses there and receive the care of physicians.

[160074] 23.21. Thus Chosroes sent the man off to his death, and with him there followed behind those who were to make away with him in the fortress—a man invincible in Persia, both being and called a general, who, having made expeditions against twelve barbarian nations, subdued them all to King Cabades.

[160373] 23.22. And on Adergoudounbades being made to vanish from among men, Varrames his son obtained the dignity of chanaranges.

[160496] 23.23. Not long afterward, either Cabades himself, the son of Zames, or someone laying claim to the name of Cabades, came to Byzantium; he was, however, most like in countenance to King Cabades.

[160692] 23.24. And the Emperor Justinian, though in doubt, since he was the grandson of King Cabades, held him in honour with much friendliness. So the affairs concerning the Persians who rose against Chosroes turned out thus.

[160912] 23.25. Later Chosroes made away with Mebodes too, for the following cause. As he was dispatching some matter of importance, he gave Zaberganes, who was present, the order to summon Mebodes; but it chanced that Zaberganes was at variance with Mebodes; and he, coming to him, found him marshalling the soldiers who followed him, and said that the king summoned him as quickly as possible.

[161300] 23.26. And he agreed to follow at once, as soon as he should have disposed of the matters in hand; but Zaberganes, led by his hatred toward him, reported to Chosroes that Mebodes was unwilling to come at present, saying that he had some occupation.

[161550] 23.27. Chosroes, then, held by anger, sending one of his followers, bade Mebodes go to the tripod. What this is, I shall presently show.

[161688] 23.28. An iron tripod stands always before the palace. Whenever, then, one of the Persians learns that the king is angry with him, it is not lawful for this man to flee to a temple anywhere nor to go elsewhere, but, sitting by this tripod, to await the king's vote, no one at all thinking fit to guard him.

[161996] 23.29. There Mebodes sat in a piteous posture for many days, until someone, at Chosroes's bidding, took and killed him. To this came his benefaction to Chosroes.

[162159] 24.1. About the same time at Byzantium a faction fell unexpectedly upon the people, which became, beyond expectation, the greatest, and ended in a great evil for both the people and the senate, in the following manner.

[162379] 24.2. The peoples in each city have from of old been divided into Blues and Greens, but it is not a long time since, for the sake of these names and of the benches on which they stand to watch, they both spend their money and give up their bodies to the most bitter torments, and do not refuse to die a most shameful death;

[162704] 24.3. and they fight against those set opposite them, neither knowing for what cause the danger is to them, and knowing well that, even if they prevail over their enemies in the fight, it will remain for them to be at once led away to prison, and, after being tortured to the utmost, then to perish.

[163005] 24.4. There springs up in them, then, a hatred toward their neighbours that has no cause, and it remains unending for all time, yielding neither to marriage-tie nor to kinship nor to the law of friendship, even if those at variance over these colours be brothers or aught else of the kind.

[163296] 24.5. And they care neither for things divine nor human beyond conquering in these, even if some impiety toward God be committed by anyone whomsoever, or the laws and the constitution be violated by their own people or the enemy; since even when, perhaps, lacking necessaries and being wronged in their most pressing needs and their fatherland injured, they take no heed, provided it shall be well for their part; for so they call their fellow-partisans.

[163752] 24.6. And of this pollution the women too share with them, not only following their husbands, but even, should it so chance, opposing them, although they neither go to the theatres at all nor are led by any other cause; so that I for my part can call this nothing else than a disease of the soul. So these things stand thus for the cities and for each people.

[164113] 24.7. At that time the magistracy which presided over the people in Byzantium was leading away some of the partisans to death. But both factions, taking thought together and making truce with one another, seize those being led off, and at once breaking into the prison release all who had been bound for faction or any other offence.

[164448] 24.8. And the underlings, as many as follow the city magistracy, were killed without account, and of the citizens whatever was pure fled to the mainland opposite, and fire was brought upon the city, as though it had come under enemies.

[164685] 24.9. And the church of Sophia and the bath of Zeuxippus and, of the emperor's court, the part from the entrance-porches as far as the so-called House of Ares, being burned, were destroyed; and besides these both the great colonnades reaching to the forum which is named after Constantine, and many houses of wealthy men and great riches.

[165025] 24.10. And the emperor and his consort and some of the senate, having shut themselves up in the palace, remained quiet. And the peoples gave one another the watchword "Conquer" (Nika), and from this even to this time that disturbance is called by that name.

[165284] 24.11. At that time the prefect of the court was John the Cappadocian, and Tribunian, a Pamphylian by race, was the emperor's assessor; this man the Romans call quaestor.

[165456] 24.12. Of these two, the one, John, was without share of liberal letters and education. For he learned nothing else, going to the schoolmaster, than letters, and these badly and ill; but in strength of nature he became the most powerful of all whom we know.

[165715] 24.13. For he was most able both to know what was needful and to find a solution for difficulties. But having become most wicked of all men, he used the power of his nature to this end, and neither thought of God nor any respect of men came upon him; rather it was his care both to destroy the lives of many men for the sake of gain and to pull down whole cities.

[166080] 24.14. In a short time, then, having got great riches about him, he broke into a certain debauchery that had no limit, plundering up to the hour of his breakfast the substance of the subjects, and for the rest occupied with drinking and the licentious works of the body;

[166352] 24.15. and he could in no way restrain himself, but ate his food up to vomiting, and was ever ready to steal money, but readier to give it out and spend it. Such, then, was John.

[166532] 24.16. Tribunian, however, both used the power of his nature and reached the height of education, second to none of his time, but having become demonically eager for love of money he was able ever to sell justice for gain, and of the laws each day for the most part he abolished some, and wrote others, selling each to those who needed it according to their need.

[166897] 24.17. So long, then, as the people carried on their war against one another over the names of the colours, there was no account of the wrongs these men did to the state; but when, having taken counsel together, as has been said, they entered into faction, then both openly throughout the whole city they insulted them, and going about sought for someone to kill. Therefore the emperor, wishing to win over the people, at once deprived both of their office.

[167356] 24.18. And he set up Phocas, a patrician, as prefect of the court, a most intelligent man and well fitted to care for justice; and he ordered Basilides to hold the office of quaestor, a man known among the patricians for his fairness and otherwise of repute.

[167616] 24.19. But none the less the faction was at its height in their time. On the fifth day from the faction, toward late afternoon, the Emperor Justinian charged Hypatius and Pompeius, nephews of Anastasius who had been emperor, to go home as quickly as possible, either suspecting that some revolutionary measure was being plotted by them against his own person, or because destiny was leading them to this.

[168022] 24.20. And they, fearing—which indeed happened—lest the people should force them to the kingship, said they would not do justly if they should abandon their emperor, who had come into so great a danger.

[168226] 24.21. Hearing this, the Emperor Justinian fell still more into suspicion, and at once bade them depart. So, then, these two men were conveyed home, and, so long as it was still night, there they remained quiet.

[168439] 24.22. On the next day, at sunrise, it became known to the people that both had departed from their stay in the palace. The whole populace, then, ran upon them and proclaimed Hypatius emperor, and led him to the forum, as one who should take up the reins of affairs.

[168707] 24.23. But Maria, the wife of Hypatius, an intelligent woman and having the greatest repute for prudence, clung to her husband and would not let go, and cried out with wailing and exhorted all her intimates, saying that the people were leading him to his death.

[168970] 24.24. But the throng overpowering her, she let go her husband against her will, and the populace, leading him unwilling to the forum of Constantine, called him to the kingship, and (for they had neither diadem nor any other of the things with which it is the law for an emperor to be invested) setting a certain golden chain upon his head, proclaimed him Emperor of the Romans.

[169350] 24.25. And now, when the members of the senate too were assembling, as many as had not chanced to be left behind in the emperor's court, many opinions were spoken, that they should go to fight at the palace;

[169559] 24.26. but Origenes, a man of the senate, coming forward spoke as follows: "Our present affairs, men of Romans, cannot be decided otherwise than by war. And war and kingship are agreed to be the greatest of all things among men.

[169789] 24.27. But great deeds will not be set right by shortness of time, but by good counsel of mind and toils of body, which men display over a length of time.

[169945] 24.28. If, then, we go against the enemy, our affairs will stand on a razor's edge, and we shall risk all in a short time, and for what is going to come about we shall either do obeisance to fortune or blame it altogether.

[170169] 24.29. For the swiftest of matters for the most part come round to the mastery of fortune. But if we manage the present more leisurely, not even should we wish shall it be in our power to take Justinian in the palace, but he will be glad if someone allows him to flee as quickly as possible.

[170462] 24.30. For a rule that is despised is wont to fall away, its strength failing it day by day. There are, then, for us other palaces, both the Placilliana and those named after Helena, whence this emperor ought, setting out, to carry on the war and

[170710] 24.31. dispose of all else in the way it shall be better." So much Origenes said. But the others, as a throng is wont to do, took it up more sharply and thought the immediate course expedient, and not least Hypatius (for it was destined that ill befall him) bade them lead the way of the road to the hippodrome. And some say that he came there on purpose, being well-disposed toward the emperor.

[171107] 24.32. Those about the emperor were in counsel whether it would be better for them to remain or, turning to flight by the ships, to depart. And many words were spoken tending to either course.

[171301] 24.33. And Theodora the empress spoke as follows: "As for the matter that a woman ought not to be daring among men, or to play the bold spirit among those who shrink back, the present crisis, I think, by no means allows us to consider whether it must be thought thus or otherwise.

[171583] 24.34. For those whose affairs have come into the greatest danger, nothing else seems best than to dispose what is at hand in the best way.

[171724] 24.35. But I hold that flight, if ever, even now, even if it bring safety, is inexpedient. For to a man who has come to the light it is impossible not also to become a corpse, but for one who has been emperor to be a fugitive is not to be endured.

[171973] 24.36. May I not be without this purple, nor may I live that day on which those who meet me shall not address me as mistress. If, then, you wish to save yourself, O Emperor, there is no difficulty.

[172172] 24.37. For we have much money, and there is the sea, and here are the ships. But consider whether, when you have been saved, it will not befall you to exchange most gladly your safety for death. For a certain ancient saying pleases me, that kingship is a fair

[172433] 24.38. winding-sheet." When the empress had said so much, courage came upon all, and turning to defence they took counsel how they might be able to defend themselves, should anyone come to make war on them.

[172641] 24.39. The soldiers, all of them, both the rest and as many as were stationed about the emperor's court, were neither well-disposed toward the emperor nor wished openly to set to work, but waited to see how the issue would turn out.

[172875] 24.40. And all his hope the emperor had in Belisarius and Mundus, of whom the one, Belisarius, just returned from the Median war, brought with him an attendant force both capable and worthy of account, and had a multitude of bodyguards and shield-bearers who had practised their exercises in contests and the dangers of war.

[173201] 24.41. And Mundus, appointed general of the Illyrians, by some chance happened to have been summoned to Byzantium for some need, bringing barbarian Heruls.

[173358] 24.42. Hypatius, then, when he came to the hippodrome, went up at once to where it is the law for an emperor to take his place, and sat upon the royal throne, whence the emperor was ever wont to view the equestrian and gymnic contest.

[173594] 24.43. And from the palace Mundus went out by a gate, where the "Snail" is named from the spiral form of its descent.

[173713] 24.44. And Belisarius at first went up straight toward Hypatius himself and the royal throne, but when he came to the adjoining building where there is from of old a guard of soldiers, he cried out to the soldiers, bidding them open the door for him as quickly as possible, that he might go against the tyrant.

[174025] 24.45. But it had been resolved by the soldiers to defend neither side until one of them should win a clear victory, and, pretending to hear least of all, they put him off.

[174199] 24.46. Turning back, then, to the emperor, Belisarius insisted that their affairs were ruined for them.

[174304] 24.47. For the soldiers who held the palace guard were rebelling against him. The emperor, then, bade him go to the so-called Chalce and the entrance-porches there.

[174470] 24.48. And he, hardly and not without dangers and great toils, going through ruins and half-burned places, went up into the hippodrome.

[174607] 24.49. And when he had come to the Blue Colonnade, which is on the right of the emperor's throne, he purposed first to go against Hypatius himself, but there being a small postern there which was shut and guarded by Hypatius's soldiers within, he feared lest, while he was toiling in a narrow place, the people, falling upon him, should destroy himself and all his followers, and the more easily and with less toil go against the emperor.

[175047] 24.50. Reckoning, then, that he must go against the people who stood in the hippodrome, immeasurable in multitude and pushed by one another with much disorder, drawing his sword from the scabbard and bidding the rest do likewise, with a run and a shout he went against them.

[175323] 24.51. And the people, standing as in a throng and not in array, when they saw soldiers corseleted and having great repute both for manliness and experience of wars, and smiting with their swords with no sparing, rushed to flight.

[175555] 24.52. And a great shout, as was likely, arising, Mundus, standing nearby and wishing to set to work (for he was a daring and effective man), but at a loss how he should use the present occasion, when he inferred that Belisarius was in the thick of the toil, at once burst into the hippodrome by the entrance which is called the Dead.

[175891] 24.53. Then indeed the partisans of Hypatius, smitten with might from both sides, were destroyed. And when the rout had become brilliant and there was already a great slaughter of the people, Boraïdes and Justus, cousins of the Emperor Justinian, no one daring to raise hands against them, both pulled Hypatius down from the throne and, leading him in, handed him over to the emperor together with Pompeius.

[176300] 24.54. And there died of the people more than thirty thousand on that day. And the emperor bade them be in close custody.

[176423] 24.55. There Pompeius wept and uttered words worthy of pity; for the man was least of all experienced in such matters and such evils; but Hypatius, reproaching him much, said that those who were about to perish unjustly ought not to lament.

[176665] 24.56. For they had at first been forced unwilling by the people to the rule, and had afterward come to the hippodrome with no evil intent against the emperor. And the soldiers, on the next day killing both, threw their bodies into the sea.

[176907] 24.57. And the emperor registered their goods to the treasury, and those of all the others of the senate who had set their purpose with them.

[177050] 24.58. Afterward, however, to all the rest and to the children of Hypatius and Pompeius he restored the dignities which they had formerly enjoyed, and of the money as much as he had not chanced to bestow upon some of his intimates. To this point did the faction in Byzantium come to its end.

[177343] 25.1. Tribunian and John, having been thus deprived of their honour, were at a later time both restored to the same offices.

[177469] 25.2. But Tribunian, surviving many years in his honour, died of disease, having suffered nothing else unpleasant from anyone. For he was wily and otherwise agreeable, and most able by the abundance of his education to overshadow the disease of his love of money.

[177734] 25.3. But John (for to all men alike he was heavy and harsh, both laying on blows to those who fell in his way and plundering all money with no account), holding his office a tenth year, paid the penalty rightly and justly for his lawlessness in his way of life, in the following manner.

[178023] 25.4. The empress Theodora was vexed at him most of all. And he, having offended the woman by his misdeeds, by no means resolved to win her over by flattery or favour, but openly set himself to plot against her, slandering her to the emperor, neither blushing at her fortune nor ashamed at the affection which the emperor had toward her, extraordinary as it was.

[178387] 25.5. The empress, perceiving what was being done, purposed to kill the man, but had no device, since the Emperor Justinian made much account of him.

[178538] 25.6. And John, learning the empress's mind toward him, fell into great fears.

[178618] 25.7. And whenever he went to his chamber to sleep, he suspected that some one of the barbarians would set upon him to destroy him each night, and ever peering out from his bedroom and scanning the entrances he remained sleepless, although he had made companions of many thousands of bodyguards and shield-bearers, a thing that had never before happened to any prefect.

[178989] 25.8. But forgetting at daybreak all his fears, both divine and human, he became again a destruction, both publicly and privately, to all the Romans. And he consorted much with sorcerers, and ever using impious divinations which foretold to him the imperial power, he was conspicuous as one walking on air and lifted aloft by hopes of the kingship.

[179339] 25.9. Yet of his wickedness and lawlessness in his way of life nothing either abated or ceased.

[179436] 25.10. And of God he had no account at all, but even if anywhere he went into a church to pray and pass the night there, he did nothing like the customs of the Christians, but putting on a cloak befitting a priest of the ancient faith which they now have been accustomed to call Greek, he recited certain unholy words which he had practised throughout that whole night, that the mind of the emperor might be still more under his hand and that he himself might be unharmed by all men.

[179921] 25.11. Meanwhile Belisarius, having subdued Italy, came to the emperor at Byzantium summoned, together with his wife Antonina, in order that he might make an expedition against the Persians.

[180113] 25.12. And to all the rest he was honoured and held worthy of much account, as was likely, but John alone was harsh toward him and held to him with much plotting, for no other reason than that he himself drew upon himself the hatred of all, while Belisarius chanced to be of all most well-esteemed; and the hope of the Romans being upon him, he again made an expedition against the Persians, leaving his wife in Byzantium.

[180537] 25.13. But Antonina, the wife of Belisarius (for she was most capable of all men in contriving impossibilities), purposing to gratify the empress, devised the following. John had a daughter Euphemia, having great repute for prudence, but very young, and from this very easily caught; whom the father loved exceedingly, since he had become father of her alone.

[180898] 25.14. This girl Antonina, fawning upon her for many days, was able most persistently to make her friend, and did not disdain to share with her her own secrets.

[181060] 25.15. And once, when she alone was present with her in the chamber, she pretended to bewail her present fortunes, that Belisarius, having made the realm of the Romans wider by a greater measure than it had been before, and having brought two captive kings and so great a sum of wealth to Byzantium, had met with an ungrateful Justinian; and in other respects she slandered the government as being unjust.

[181467] 25.16. And Euphemia, overjoyed at the words (for through fear of the empress she too was vexed at the present government), said, "And yet for this, dearest, you yourselves are to blame, in that, when it is in your power,

[181689] 25.17. you will not use your might." And Antonina, taking up the word, said, "For we are not able, daughter, to undertake revolutionary measures in the camp, unless some of those within join with us in the work; but if your father were willing, most easily, setting ourselves to this deed, we should accomplish whatever should be

[182020] 25.18. God's will." Hearing this, Euphemia eagerly promised that it would be brought to effect, and departing thence at once carried the matter to her father.

[182180] 25.19. And he, pleased at the word (for he supposed that this way led to the oracles and the kingship), at once with no delay agreed, and bade his child arrange that on the morrow he should himself meet Antonina in conference and give pledges.

[182425] 25.20. And Antonina, learning John's mind, and wishing to lead the man as far as possible from the perception of the truth, said that for the present it was inexpedient for him to meet with her, lest some suspicion arising in the meanwhile should be sufficient to hinder what was being done; but that she was about presently to set out for the East to Belisarius.

[182790] 25.21. When, therefore, having departed from Byzantium, she should be in the suburb (which is named Rufinianae and chanced to be the property of Belisarius), there let John come as though to salute and escort her, and let them both make their words about the whole matter and take and give pledges. Saying this, she seemed to John to speak well, and a fixed day was appointed for the deed.

[183181] 25.22. And the empress, hearing the whole account from Antonina, praised what had been planned, and exhorting her still much more urged her on to zeal.

[183334] 25.23. And when the appointed day came, Antonina, having saluted the empress, departed from the city and was at Rufinianae, as though on the morrow to begin the road to the East, where John too came by night to fulfil what had been agreed.

[183575] 25.24. And the empress, slandering to her husband what John was doing for the tyranny, sent Narses the eunuch and Marcellus the commander of the palace guards to Rufinianae with many soldiers, on condition that, having searched out what was being done, if they should find John attempting revolutionary measures, they should at once kill the man and return.

[183934] 25.25. And these were dispatched for this work. But they say that the emperor, perceiving what was being done, sent one of John's intimates to him, forbidding him by any means to meet secretly with Antonina.

[184143] 25.26. But John (for it was destined that ill befall him), holding the emperor's suggestion of no account, about midnight met with Antonina near a certain garden-wall, behind which she had chanced to seat those under Narses and Marcellus, that they might hear what was said.

[184419] 25.27. There John, with unguarded mouth, both avowed the matters of the attempt and confirmed them with the most dread oaths, while Narses and Marcellus suddenly set upon him.

[184596] 25.28. And a tumult, as was likely, arising, the bodyguards of John (for they stood somewhere very near) at once came to him.

[184723] 25.29. And one of them smote Marcellus with his sword, not knowing who he was, and thus John was able to escape with them and came quickly to the city.

[184876] 25.30. And if he had taken courage to go at once to the emperor, I think he would have suffered nothing unpleasant from him; but as it was, having fled to a church, he gave the empress full power to use the plot against him as she pleased.

[185117] 25.31. At that time, then, having become from a prefect a private man, he was removed thence and conveyed to another place, which is situated in the suburb of the city of Cyzicus—the Cyzicenes call the suburb Artace. There, against his will, he was clothed in the garb of a priest, not of a bishop, however, but of one whom they have been accustomed to call presbyter.

[185487] 25.32. But he was by no means willing to perform priestly office, lest it should ever be an obstacle to him in going again to the magistracies; for he by no means wished to let go of his hopes. And his money was at once registered to the treasury.

[185736] 25.33. Of which the emperor remitted to him a great part;

[185795] 25.34. for he was still held by his sparing toward him. There it was in John's power, having become careless of all dangers, and having got great riches about him—both what he himself had chanced to hide and what by the emperor's will had remained with him—to live luxuriously at his pleasure, and, by prudent reckoning, to count the present circumstances happy.

[186159] 25.35. Therefore all the Romans were simply vexed at the man, in that, having become most wicked of all demons, he held a life happier than before beyond his deserts.

[186327] 25.36. But God, I think, did not allow the requital for John to be settled at this, but was preparing for him a greater punishment. And it came about as follows.

[186490] 25.37. There was in Cyzicus a bishop, Eusebius by name, harsh to all who came in his way, no less than John; this man the Cyzicenes, slandering to the emperor, called to trial.

[186668] 25.38. But when they accomplished nothing, Eusebius having got round them with much power, certain young men, taking counsel together, killed him in the forum of Cyzicus.

[186840] 25.39. Now John happened to have become most at variance with Eusebius, and from this the suspicion of the plot came to him.

[186966] 25.40. There were sent, then, from the senate men to search out this defilement; who first shut John up in prison, and then, a man who had become so powerful a prefect, and who had been registered among the patricians and had mounted the chair of the consuls—than which there is nothing greater, it seems, at least in the state of the Romans—they set him up naked, like some robber or footpad, and scourging him with many strokes over the back compelled him to tell the things he had done in his life.

[187469] 25.41. And though John was not very clearly convicted as the cause of the murder of Eusebius, yet the justice of God seemed to be exacting from him the penalties of the whole world.

[187652] 25.42. Then, having taken from him all his money, they put him naked into the ship, wearing a single garment, and that an exceedingly coarse one, bought for a few obols, and those who escorted him bade him, wherever the ship should put in, ask bread or obols from those he met.

[187931] 25.43. And thus begging everywhere along the journey, he is conveyed to Antinous in Egypt. And this is the third year they keep him shut up there.

[188079] 25.44. And he, though he had come into such sufferings, did not even let go the hope of the kingship, but resolved to slander certain of the Alexandrians too, as owing gold to the treasury. So this requital for his political deeds overtook John the Cappadocian ten years later.

[188358] 26.1. At that time the emperor again appointed Belisarius General of the East, and sending him to Libya he gained the country, as shall be told in the books to follow.

[188527] 26.2. When this came to Chosroes and the Persians, they were terribly vexed, and they now repented of the peace they had made with the Romans, since they perceived that their power was advancing to greatness.

[188737] 26.3. And Chosroes, sending envoys to Byzantium, said that he rejoiced with the Emperor Justinian, and asked with a pretended laugh to receive his share of the spoils from Libya, since the emperor would never have been able to prevail in the war against the Vandals,

[189005] 26.4. had the Persians not made the treaty with him. Then, therefore, Justinian, having gifted Chosroes with money, dismissed the envoys not long after.

[189159] 26.5. And in the city of Daras the following thing befell. There was a certain John there, enrolled in a regiment of foot. This man, not with all the soldiers in agreement with him, but with a few, setting up a tyranny, seized the city.

[189397] 26.6. And sitting in the palace as in a citadel, he strengthened his tyranny day by day.

[189487] 26.7. And had it not chanced that the Persians were then holding to the peace toward the Romans, irreparable evils would have come from this to the Romans. But as it was, the agreement, anticipating, prevented this, as I have said.

[189720] 26.8. On the fourth day from the tyranny the soldiers, taking counsel together by the purpose of Mamas the priest of the city and Anastasius, one of the notable citizens, went up into the palace at the height of noon, each having hidden a small sword under his cloak.

[189989] 26.9. And first, at the inner door, finding a few of the bodyguards, they at once killed them. Then, going into the men's hall, they laid hands on the tyrant; but some say that the soldiers did not do this first, but that, while they were still hesitating in the inner court and dreading the danger, one of the sausage-sellers who was with them sprang in with his cleaver and, falling unexpectedly upon John, struck him.

[190411] 26.10. And he, not struck with a mortal blow, but fleeing with much tumult, fell suddenly upon these soldiers.

[190523] 26.11. And thus they, laying hands on the man, at once set fire to the palace and burned it, that no hope might be left from there to those attempting revolutionary measures, and leading the man off to prison bound him.

[190744] 26.12. And one of them, fearing lest the soldiers, learning that the tyrant survived, should again cause trouble for the city, killed John, and in this way put an end to the disturbance. So the affairs concerning this tyranny turned out thus.
