[0] 1.1. Thus Belisarius, while affairs were still in suspense, came to Byzantium bringing with him Wittigis and the notable Goths and the children of Ildibadus and all the goods, and there followed him Ildiger and Valerianus and Martinus with Herodian, these alone. 1.2. And the emperor Justinian saw Wittigis with his wife gladly, and marvelled at the throng of the barbarians for the beauty and stature of their bodies. 1.3. And receiving the wealth of Theoderic, which was a thing worth seeing, he set it out in the Palatium as a spectacle for those of the senate in private, taking pride in the magnitude of what had been done; but he neither displayed it to the populace nor granted the triumph to Belisarius, as he had done when he came after conquering Gelimer and the Vandals. 1.4. Yet in all men's tales Belisarius was the theme, having won two victories such as it had never before befallen any man to achieve, and having led two kings captive to Byzantium, and having made the race and the wealth of both Gizeric and Theoderic spoil for the Romans, contrary to expectation—men than whom none more illustrious had ever arisen among the barbarians—and having brought back the wealth from the enemy again into the state, and having recovered for the empire in a short time nearly half the portion of land and sea. 1.5. And it was a delight to the people of Byzantium to see Belisarius going forth from his house into the forum each day, or returning to it, and no one was ever sated with this sight. 1.6. For his going forth resembled a most crowded procession, since a multitude of Vandals and Goths and Moors always followed him. And he was also handsome in body and tall and most comely of countenance of all men. 1.7. And he showed himself so gentle and so easy of approach to those who met him that he resembled a man both very poor and of no repute. 1.8. And the love of his rule that was invincible among the soldiers and the country folk arose because he had become the most generous of all men toward the soldiers; for those who had been unfortunate in an engagement he consoled for their former wounds with great sums of money, and to those who had distinguished themselves he gave as prizes armlets and necklaces to wear; and if a soldier's horse or bow or any other thing whatever had been lost in battle, another was straightway provided in its place by Belisarius. And toward the country folk he used such sparingness and forethought that, while Belisarius was general, it never befell that they suffered any violence, but it came about, contrary to expectation, that all on whom a multitude of his army was quartered grew rich. 1.9. For all their goods were sold to them at their own price. And whenever the crops were at their height, he guarded strictly lest his horse, passing through, should do harm to anyone. 1.10. And of the ripe fruits on the trees no one whatever was at all permitted to touch any. 1.11. And indeed he laid claim to chastity in a prodigious degree; for he touched no woman whatever except his own wedded wife. 1.12. So that, although he had taken captive from among the Vandals and Goths so great a number of women, and women of such beauty of countenance as no one anywhere of men ever saw, he permitted no one of them either to come into his sight or to meet him in any other way. 1.13. And he was, besides all his other qualities, exceedingly quick-witted and most capable of devising the best course in difficulties. 1.14. In the dangers of war he was both stout-hearted with safety and most daring with calculation, and was keen and slow in the attack on the enemy by turns, 1.15. as each was needful. And apart from these things, in terrors he kept his mind hopeful and superior to confusion, while in success he was neither lifted up nor wanton; indeed, no one ever saw Belisarius drunk. 1.16. Now during all the time that he commanded the Roman army both in Libya and in Italy, he continued to conquer and always to win what lay before him. 1.17. But when he came, summoned, to Byzantium, his valour became known to the greatest degree even more than before. 1.18. For being himself pre-eminent in all valour, and surpassing all the generals who had ever been both in the abundance of his wealth and in the power of his shield-bearers and guards, he became, as was natural, terrible to all commanders and soldiers. 1.19. For no one, I think, dared to oppose his command, and they never refused to carry out fully whatever he ordered, both out of respect for his valour and out of fear of his power. 1.20. For he provided seven thousand horsemen from his own household, of whom not one had ever proved a failure, but each one of them claimed to stand first in the line of battle and to challenge the best of the enemy. 1.21. And the elders of the Romans, when, being besieged by the Goths, they watched what was being done in the engagements of the war, marvelled greatly and cried out that a single household was undoing the power of Theoderic. 1.22. So Belisarius, having become powerful both in dignity and in judgement, as has been said, deliberated what would be advantageous to the emperor's affairs and always carried out what had been decided by his own independent judgement. 1.23. But the other commanders, being rather equal among themselves and having in mind to accomplish nothing except their own private gains, began both to plunder the Romans and to give over the subjects to the soldiers, and they neither themselves any longer thought what was needful nor kept the soldiers obedient to their orders. 1.24. Wherefore many errors were committed by them, and the affairs of the Romans were all ruined in a short time. By what manner this came about I now proceed to tell. 1.25. When Ildibadus learned that Belisarius had set out from Ravenna and was going on his journey, he gathered about himself all the barbarians and as many of the Roman soldiers as a revolution pleased. 1.26. And he attended most carefully to his rule, and was eager to recover for the race of the Goths the mastery over the Italians. 1.27. At the beginning, then, not more than a thousand followed him, and they held a single city, Ticinum; but little by little there came over to him all who were in Liguria and Venetia. 1.28. Now there was a certain Alexander in Byzantium set over the public accounts; this office the Romans, using Greek, call logothete. 1.29. This man always charged against the soldiers the loss to the public treasury. And bringing them under such accusations, he himself quickly became of repute from being obscure, and from being poor simply rich, and indeed he exacted great sums of money for the emperor, if anyone ever did; but he became, of all men, most responsible for the soldiers being few and poor and slow toward dangers. 1.30. And the people of Byzantium called him also by the surname Psalidios, because it was easy for him, by cutting off the gold coin in a circle, to make it less by as much as he wished, and yet to preserve the round shape which it had before. 1.31. For psalidion they call the instrument with which one does such things. This Alexander the emperor, when he had summoned Belisarius, 1.32. sent into Italy. And he, when he had come to Ravenna, set forth accounts that had no reason. For the Italians, who had neither touched the emperor's money nor done anything improper toward the treasury, he called to account, bringing against them the charge of their injustice toward Theoderic and the other rulers of the Goths, and compelling them to pay back if they had gained anything by deceiving those men, as he himself alleged. 1.33. And the wounds and dangers of the soldiers he requited, contrary to their expectation, with the pettiness of his accounts. Wherefore the Italians became ill-disposed to the emperor Justinian, and none of the soldiers any longer wished to set himself in the danger of war, but, playing the coward on purpose, they always made the affairs of the enemy advance greatly. 1.34. So the other commanders for these reasons remained quiet, but Vitalius alone (for he happened to have in Venetia both another multitude of an army and a large throng of Erulian barbarians) dared to come to battle with Ildibadus, fearing, as happened, lest, when at a later time he had come to a great measure of power, they should no longer be able to hold him back. 1.35. And when a fierce battle had taken place about the city of Tarbesion, Vitalius, defeated by far, fled, saving some few, but losing the most of his men there. In this battle many of the Eruli fell, and Visandus the leader of the Eruli was killed. 1.36. And Theudimundus the son of Mauricius the son of Mundus, being still a youth, came into danger of death, yet escaped with Vitalius. And the name of Ildibadus from this exploit came both to the emperor and to all men. 1.37. Afterward it befell that Uraïas gave offence to Ildibadus from the following cause. There was to Uraïas a wife who bore off the first prize for wealth and beauty of body among these barbarians, above all. 1.38. She once went down to the bath, clothed in a great quantity of finery and bringing with her an attendance worthy of much account. 1.39. And seeing the wife of Ildibadus there in plain garments, she neither did obeisance to her as one dwelling with a king, but otherwise despised and insulted her. For Ildibadus was still in poverty, since he had by no means come upon the royal wealth. 1.40. And the wife of Ildibadus, being grievously distressed at the strangeness of the insult, came to her husband in tears and saw fit that he should avenge her, who had suffered irreparable things at the hands of the wife of Uraïas. 1.41. Wherefore Ildibadus at first slandered Uraïas to the barbarians, alleging that he was about to desert to the enemy, and a little later killed him by treachery, and from this he fell into the hatred of the Goths. 1.42. For it was by no means their wish that Uraïas should thus, without consideration, be removed from among men. And many now, gathering among themselves, reviled Ildibadus as one who had wrought unholy deeds. None, however, was willing to avenge this murder upon him. 1.43. Now there was among them a certain Velas, a Gepid by race, who had come to the dignity of being one of the king's guards. 1.44. This man, having become betrothed to a woman fair of face, fell into a prodigious love; but while he had been sent against the enemy, in order that he might make with certain others an assault upon them, Ildibadus, whether led by ignorance or by something else, gave the betrothed woman in marriage to another of the barbarians. 1.45. And when Velas, returning from the camp, heard this, being passionate by nature, he did not bear the insult arising from the deed, but resolved straightway to kill Ildibadus, 1.46. thinking that he would thereby gratify all the Goths. And watching him once feasting the best of the Goths, he set about the plot. 1.47. For it is the custom that, while the king is feasting, many others and the guards stand around. So while Ildibadus, putting his hand to the food, was lying prone on the couch, Velas suddenly struck him on the neck with his sword, 1.48. so that, while the food was still held in the man's fingers, the head, falling forward onto the table, struck all present with consternation and brought them into great amazement. 1.49. This vengeance came upon Ildibadus for the murder of Uraïas. And the winter was ending, and the sixth year of this war which Procopius has written was coming to a close.

[11553] 2.1. Now there was a certain Eraric in the army of the Goths, a Rogian by race, but invested with great power among these barbarians. These Rogi are a Gothic nation, and of old they lived independently. 2.2. But when Theoderic at the first joined them in alliance with certain other nations, they were classed as part of the race of the Goths and acted together with them in all things against the enemy. 2.3. By no means, however, mingling with foreign women, they preserved among themselves the name of their nation by an unmixed succession of children. 2.4. This Eraric, when affairs had come into confusion over the murder of Ildibadus, the Rogi suddenly proclaimed king. 2.5. This by no means pleased the Goths, but the most of them fell into great despondency, since the hopes which they had formerly held in Ildibadus had been destroyed—he who had been capable of recovering both the rule and the mastery of Italy for the Goths. 2.6. Eraric, however, did nothing worthy of account; for after living five months he died in the following manner. 2.7. There was a certain Totila, a nephew of Ildibadus, a man who had come to the height of intelligence and was exceedingly energetic and worthy of much account among the Goths. This Totila happened at that time to be the commander of the Goths in Tarbesion. 2.8. And when he learned that Ildibadus had thus been removed from among men, as has been said, he sent to Constantianus at Ravenna and begged that pledges be given him concerning his safety, on condition that he would surrender both himself and the Goths whom he commanded, together with Tarbesion, to the Romans. 2.9. And Constantianus, hearing this gladly, swore to everything just as Totila asked, and a fixed day was agreed upon by both for the deed, on which Totila and the Goths holding the garrison in Tarbesion were to receive into the city some of the friends of Constantianus and to put themselves, together with it, into their hands. 2.10. But the Goths were already distressed at the rule of Eraric, seeing that the man was not adequate to carry on the war against the Romans, and the most of them openly reviled him as having become a hindrance to great deeds for them, because he had removed Ildibadus from their path. 2.11. And at last, taking counsel together, they sent to Totila at Tarbesion, inviting him to the rule. For having already much longing for the rule of Ildibadus, they turned the hope of victory to Totila his kinsman, becoming hopeful in the man because he wished the same things. 2.12. But he openly revealed to those who came to him his agreement with the Romans, and said that, if the Goths should kill Eraric before the appointed day, he would follow them and would accomplish everything in whatever way they wished. 2.13. When the barbarians heard this, they set about a plot for the destruction of Eraric. These things were happening in the camp of the Goths. 2.14. In the meantime the Roman army, enjoying in safety the inactivity of the enemy, neither combined nor purposed to do anything against the barbarians. 2.15. And Eraric, calling together all the Goths, persuaded them to send envoys to the emperor Justinian, to beg that he should make peace with them on the same terms on which he had been willing previously to make a treaty with Wittigis: that the Goths, holding the lands beyond the river Padus, should withdraw from the rest of Italy. 2.16. And when the Goths approved this, selecting some of his most intimate friends, he sent them as envoys, among others one Caballarius by name. 2.17. These were to do with the emperor ostensibly what I have said before, but secretly he instructed them to do nothing else with the emperor than that he himself should receive much money and be enrolled among the patricians on condition that, handing over the whole of Italy, he should lay down the appearance of the rule. 2.18. So when the envoys came to Byzantium, they did these things. In the meantime the Goths killed Eraric by treachery. And when he had died, Totila took over the rule according to their agreement.

[15630] 3.1. When the emperor Justinian learned both what had happened concerning Eraric and that the Goths had established Totila in the rule over them, he ceased not from reproaching and taunting the commanders of the army there. 3.2. Wherefore, establishing garrisons in each city, John the nephew of Vitalian and Bessas and Vitalius and all the others gathered in Ravenna, where Constantianus and Alexander, whom I mentioned before, were staying. 3.3. And when all had gathered, it seemed best to them first to march against Verona, which is in Venetia, and when they should have taken both it and the Goths there, then to go against Totila and Ticinum. 3.4. Now this Roman army had been gathered to twelve thousand, and there were eleven commanders of them, of whom the first were Constantianus and Alexander, who went straight to the city of Verona. 3.5. And coming near it, about sixty stades off, they encamped in the plain there. For everywhere about this place are plains fit for horsemen, extending to the city of Mantua, which is distant a day's journey from the city of Verona. 3.6. Now there was a notable man among the Venetians, Marcianus by name, who dwelt in a fort not far from the city of Verona, and being most well-disposed to the emperor, was eager to hand over the city to the Roman army. 3.7. And, since one of the guards was known to him from boyhood, sending to him some of his friends, he persuaded the man with money to receive the emperor's army into the city. 3.8. And when the guard of the gates agreed to this, Marcianus sent those who had arranged it with him to the commanders of the Roman army, in order that they might report the agreements and burst into the city with them by night. 3.9. And it seemed advantageous to the commanders that one of them should go ahead with a few men; and if the guard should open the gates to them, that they should hold them and receive the army into the city in safety. 3.10. Now of the rest no one was willing to undertake the danger, but Artabazes alone, an Armenian by race and exceedingly good in matters of war, set himself to the task by no means unwillingly. 3.11. This man commanded the Persians whom Belisarius had a little before happened to send to Byzantium from the land of the Persians with Bleschames, having taken the fort of Sisaurana. 3.12. And then, selecting a hundred men from the whole army, at an unseasonable hour of the night he came close to the wall. 3.13. And when the guard opened the gates to them where it had been agreed, some of them, standing there, summoned the army, while others, mounting onto the wall, killed the guards there, falling upon them unexpectedly. 3.14. But when all the Goths perceived the evil, fleeing through other gates they departed. Now a certain rock rises exceedingly high before the wall, from which it is possible to observe everything being done in Verona and to count the people there, and indeed to see the plain for the most part. 3.15. There the Goths, taking refuge, kept quiet the whole night. And the Roman army, having come within forty stades of the city, went no farther forward, since the generals were disputing with one another about the goods in the city. 3.16. And while they were still contending about this booty, the day had now become clearly bright, and the Goths, from the summit, observing exactly both the enemy in the city and how far off the rest of the army stood from Verona, advanced at a run to the city through the very gates from which they had departed before; for those who had entered were not able to hold them. 3.17. The Romans, then, taking counsel together, took refuge about the battlements of the wall, and as the barbarians in great numbers came to close quarters with them, both the others and Artabazes above all, displaying marvellous deeds, most stoutly defended themselves against the assailants. 3.18. And then the commanders of the Roman army, having settled with one another about the goods in Verona, resolved to advance against the city with the whole rest of the army. 3.19. But finding the gates shut against them and the enemy defending themselves most stoutly, they quickly rode back, although they saw the others fighting inside the wall and begging them not to abandon them but to remain there until, taking refuge with them, they might be saved. 3.20. Wherefore Artabazes and his men, overpowered by the multitude of the enemy and despairing of the help from their own people, all leaped down outside from the wall. 3.21. Now those who chanced to fall on level ground were brought unharmed to the Roman camp, among whom was also Artabazes; but all who fell into difficult places perished there. 3.22. And when Artabazes came to the Roman camp, after much reproaching and reviling of all, he went with them, and crossing the Eridanus they came to the city of Faventia, which is in the country of Aemilia and is distant one hundred and twenty stades from Ravenna.

[20577] 4.1. And Totila, learning what had happened in Verona, summoned many of the Goths from Verona, and when they were present, with the whole army, which was five thousand, he went against his opponents. And when the commanders of the Roman army learned this, they deliberated about their present situation. 4.2. And Artabazes coming forward spoke as follows: “Let none of you, men in command, see fit to despise the enemy because we are now inferior in numbers, nor, because we contend against those who have been enslaved by Belisarius, let him go against them with a slack reckoning. 4.3. For many, deceived by an untrue opinion, have brought themselves to ruin, and contempt, arising at the wrong time, has had the power to undo the existing strength of some, since besides their previous misfortune calls these men to good fortune. 4.4. For fortune, coming to the despair of good hopes, turns about to an excess of daring. 4.5. And I have said this among you now not led by suspicion, but from those things by which, having risked myself against them lately, I have learned the daring of these men. 4.6. And let none suppose that, because I was defeated with a few men, I marvel at the power of the enemy. For the valour of men is made manifest both to those who surpass in numbers and to those who are inferior in this, to those at least who will fight against them. 4.7. I think, then, it will be more advantageous for us, watching the crossing of the river by the barbarians, to come to close quarters when they are crossing midway, than when they have already all come together in a mass. 4.8. And let such a victory be deemed inglorious by none. For the issue of affairs is wont to give the name of glory or inglory, and men are accustomed to praise the victors, not searching into the manner of 4.9. the victory.” Artabazes said so much. But the commanders, opposing his opinion, did nothing of what was needful, but remaining where they were, wasted the opportunity. 4.10. And now the army of the Goths had come close, and when they were about to cross the river, Totila, calling all together, exhorted them as follows: “All other battles, men of my kindred, for the most part announcing a battle of a similar kind to the armies, urge them to the contest; but we set ourselves to this contest against the enemy using not an equal fortune, but with much that differs. 4.11. For if they should be defeated, as may chance, they will not long afterward be able to renew the fight against us. For in the forts of all Italy a multitude of soldiers has been left them, and it is in no way unlikely that another army from Byzantium will immediately come to their aid. But if we suffer this same thing, it will be left for the name of the Goths to perish together with the hope. 4.12. For from two hundred thousand it has befallen us to be reduced to five thousand. Having said so much, I think it not out of place to remind you of this also, that when you resolved with Ildibadus to take up arms against the emperor, the multitude that lived with you was no more than a thousand, and the whole country surrounded you up to the city of Ticinum. 4.13. But when you became superior in the engagement, the affairs both of your army and of the country went forward for you. So that if you are now willing to play the man, I have hope, as the war advances, as is likely, of altogether prevailing over our opponents. 4.14. For it always befalls the victors to have become both more numerous and more powerful. Let each of you, then, be eager to come to close quarters with the enemy with all his might, knowing well that for us, who have not now distinguished ourselves, it is impossible to renew the fight against our opponents. 4.15. And it is worth while for us to come to close quarters with the enemy with good hope, being bold in the injustice of these men. 4.16. For their life toward their subjects has been such that the Italians now need no other punishment for the treachery they ventured against the Goths, which they ought not. So that, to put it in a word, all evils have befallen them at the hands of those whom they received. 4.17. And what could be more easily taken than enemies whose dealings with God are evidently not sound? And indeed it would befit us to become hopeful for the contest from the fear that there is of us toward them. 4.18. For we go against no other men than those who lately, having come into the very midst of Verona, gave it up for no reason, although none whatever was pursuing them, and thus shamefully rushed into flight.” 4.19. Having exhorted them thus, Totila ordered three hundred of his followers, crossing the river about twenty stades off, to get behind the camp of the enemy, and when the battle should be at close quarters, going against their rear, to shoot at them and harass them with all their might, in order that those men, being thrown into confusion, might be mindful of no resistance. 4.20. And he himself with the whole rest of the army immediately crossed the river and went straight against his opponents; 4.21. and the Romans straightway came to meet them. And when both, advancing on their way, came nearer one another, a Goth, Valaris by name, great in body and exceedingly terrible in appearance, yet energetic and good in matters of war, riding his horse out ahead of the rest of the army, took his stand in the space between, corselet-clad and having a helmet on his head, and challenged all the Romans, if anyone wished to come to close quarters with him. 4.22. The rest, then, dismayed, remained quiet, but Artabazes set himself to the contest with him alone. 4.23. So both, driving their horses against each other, when they came close, thrust their spears; and Artabazes, anticipating, struck Valaris first in the right side. 4.24. And the barbarian, struck a mortal blow, was about to fall backward to the earth, but his spear, propped behind on the ground against a certain rock, by no means allowed him to fall. 4.25. And Artabazes pressed on still more, thrusting his spear into the man's vitals. For he did not yet suspect that he had been caught by a mortal blow. 4.26. And so it befell that the iron point of Valaris' spear, having become nearly upright, touched the corselet of Artabazes, and advancing little by little passed through the whole corselet, but slipping off the skin about the neck of Artabazes, grazed it. 4.27. And by some chance the iron, going forward, cut an artery which was somewhere there, and there was straightway a flow of much blood. 4.28. But the man had no sensation of pain, but he himself rode his horse back to the Roman army, 4.29. while Valaris fell there dead. And Artabazes too, the blood not ceasing, on the third day afterward was removed from among men, and shook all the hope of the Romans, since then too, having become unfit for fighting in this engagement, he brought their affairs to ruin in no small matters. 4.30. For he, having got out of range of the missiles, attended to his wound, while both armies came to close quarters. 4.31. And when the engagement had become fierce, the three hundred barbarians, going by the road behind the Roman army, suddenly appeared, and the Romans, observing them and supposing that there was a great multitude of those fighting against them, fell into dread and straightway rushed into flight, 4.32. as it became possible for each. And the barbarians killed the Romans, who were fleeing in no order, and took alive many of them and kept them in custody, and they took all the standards, which had never before befallen the Romans. And of the commanders each, as he was able, fled with some few, and they guarded the cities where they chanced to be saved.

[28290] 5.1. Not long afterward Totila sent an army against Justinus and Florentia, over whom he set as commanders the most warlike of the Goths, Bleda and Roderic and Uliaris. These, when they had come to Florentia, sitting down about the wall, set themselves to a siege. 5.2. And Justinus, thrown into confusion since he had brought in none of the provisions, sent to Ravenna to the commanders of the Roman army, 5.3. begging them to help him quickly. And he who was sent, escaping the notice of the enemy by night, came to Ravenna and reported their situation. 5.4. Wherefore a Roman army worthy of account straightway went to Florentia, of which Bessas and Cyprianus and John the nephew of Vitalian were the leaders. When the Goths learned this from their scouts, 5.5. breaking off the siege, they withdrew to a place named Mucellis, distant a day's journey from Florentia. 5.6. And when the Roman army joined Justinus, they left some few of his followers there to guard the city, while they themselves, taking the rest, advanced against the enemy. 5.7. And as they journeyed, it seemed most advantageous to them that one of the commanders, selecting the most notable men of the whole army, should go forward and himself come to close quarters with the enemy unexpectedly by a sudden raid, while the rest of the army, marching at leisure, should come there. And casting lots for this, they awaited the resolve of fortune for this. 5.8. Now the lot fell to John, but the commanders no longer wished to carry out what had been agreed. 5.9. Wherefore John was compelled to go forward with his followers against the enemy. And the barbarians, learning of the assault of their opponents, resolved with much fear to abandon the plain where they happened to be encamped, and ran up with tumult onto a certain great hill which rises there. 5.10. And when John and his men came there, running up themselves against the enemy, they set to work. 5.11. And as the barbarians defended themselves stoutly, there was much shoving, and many on both sides fell, displaying marvellous deeds. 5.12. And when John, with much shouting and tumult, rushed against the enemy facing him, it befell that one of his own guards, struck by a javelin from one of the enemy, fell; and from this the Romans, thrust back, withdrew. 5.13. And now the rest of the Roman army, having come to the plain, made a phalanx and stood. And if they had received John and his men as they fled, going to close quarters with the enemy together with them, they would both have conquered in the engagement and been able to take nearly all of them. 5.14. But by some chance it befell that an untrue report went about in the Roman army, that John had been removed from among men in this action by one of his own guards. 5.15. And when the report came to the commanders, they no longer saw fit to remain there, but all went off in a shameful withdrawal. 5.16. For neither drawn up nor in any companies, but each one as he was able, rushed into flight. And many perished in this flight, but all who were saved fled for many days, though pursued nowhere. 5.17. And afterward, as each chanced, entering certain forts, they reported to those they met nothing else than that John was dead. 5.18. And from this they neither mingled any longer with one another nor purposed for the future to combine against the enemy, but each remained inside his own wall and prepared for a siege, fearing lest the barbarians should come against him. 5.19. And Totila, displaying much friendliness toward the captives, was able to win them over, and for the rest the most of them of their own will marched against the Romans with him. And the winter was ending, and the seventh year of this war which Procopius has written was coming to a close.

[32059] 6.1. Then Totila took the forts of Caesena and Petra. And a little later he came to Tuscany, and making trial of the places there, when no one was willing to come over to him, crossing the river Tiber, he by no means came into the borders of Rome, but coming immediately to the Campanians and Samnites, he won over the strong city of Beneventum with no trouble, and razed its walls to the ground, in order that an army coming from Byzantium might not, setting out from a strong place, be able to give trouble to the Goths. 6.2. After this he resolved to besiege the Neapolitans, since they were by no means willing to receive him into the city, although he said many enticing things. For Conon was on guard there, having a thousand Romans and Isaurians. 6.3. And he himself, encamping with the greater part of the army not far from the wall, kept quiet, but sending a part of the army, he took the fort of Cumae and certain other strongholds, and was able to gird himself there with great wealth. 6.4. And finding there the wives of the senators, he neither insulted them and with much friendliness released them free, and from this he won a great name both for intelligence and for kindness among all the Romans. And since no enemy met him, always sending around small bodies of the army, he accomplished deeds worthy of much account. 6.5. He won over the Bruttii and Lucanians and held Apulia with Calabria, and himself exacted the public taxes, and bore off the revenues of the goods in place of those who possessed the lands, and arranged the rest as having become master of Italy. 6.6. Wherefore the emperor owed great sums of money to the Roman army, which had not received its accustomed allowances at the appointed times. 6.7. And from this the Italians, having fallen both from their possessions and into a great measure of danger again, were in great mourning. And the soldiers showed themselves still more disobedient to their commanders, and remained gladly in the cities. 6.8. Constantianus, then, held Ravenna, and John Rome, and Bessas Spolitium, and Justinus Florentia, and Cyprianus Perusia, and each of the others wherever he chanced at the beginning to have fled and been saved. 6.9. When the emperor learned this and regarded it as a misfortune, he established Maximinus as prefect of the praetorian guards of Italy as quickly as possible, in order that he might both be overseer to the commanders in the war and provide the soldiers with the provisions according to their need. 6.10. And he sent with him a fleet of ships, filling it with Thracian and Armenian soldiers. And Herodian led the Thracians, and Phazas an Iberian, the nephew of Peranius, the Armenians; and there sailed with them also a few Huns. 6.11. So Maximinus, setting out from Byzantium with the whole fleet, came to Epirus in Greece; and there, wasting the opportunity, he sat idle for no reason. 6.12. For he was by no means experienced in deeds of war, and from this was a coward and most given to delay. 6.13. After this the emperor sent also Demetrius as general, who had previously campaigned with Belisarius as commander of a regiment of foot. 6.14. So Demetrius, sailing to Sicily, when he heard that Conon and the Neapolitans were being most bitterly besieged and were altogether in want of provisions, wished to help them quickly, but being unable, since his army was small and not worthy of account, 6.15. devised the following. Gathering as many ships as possible from all Sicily and filling them with grain and the other provisions, he sailed, giving his opponents the impression that there was a very great army in the ships. 6.16. And he hit upon the thought of the enemy. For they supposed that a great army was coming against them, judging from the fact that they heard a great fleet was sailing from Sicily. 6.17. And if at the beginning Demetrius had wished to go straight to Naples, I think he would both have struck the enemy with consternation and saved the city, no one withstanding him. 6.18. But as it was, dreading the danger, he by no means resolved to put in at Naples, but sailing down to the harbour of Rome, he was eager to gather soldiers from there. 6.19. But they, inasmuch as they had been defeated by the barbarians and still had great fear of them, were by no means willing to follow Demetrius against Totila and the Goths. Wherefore he was compelled to go to Naples with only those who had come with him from Byzantium. 6.20. Now there was another Demetrius, a Cephallenian by race, a sailor of old and exactly experienced in the works and dangers of the sea, and having sailed with Belisarius both to Libya and Italy, he had become of repute for this experience, and from this the emperor had established him as governor of Naples. 6.21. And when the barbarians began to besiege the place, being gripped by much insolence, he heaped many insults upon Totila, and the man was seen to be exceedingly foul-mouthed in this struggle. 6.22. And as the evil advanced and Conon was perishing, getting secretly into a certain skiff, he dared to go to the general Demetrius alone. 6.23. And being saved contrary to expectation, and joining Demetrius, he encouraged him greatly and urged him to this enterprise. 6.24. But Totila, hearing the whole account about this fleet, held many fast-sailing warships in readiness, and when the enemy put in at the shore there not far from Naples, coming upon them unexpectedly, he struck them with consternation and turned them all to flight. 6.25. And of them he killed many and took alive very many, while those escaped who at the beginning were able to leap into the skiffs of the ships, among whom was also Demetrius the general. For all the ships with their very cargoes and the very men 6.26. the barbarians took. And there they found also Demetrius the governor of Naples. Cutting off both his tongue and his hands, they did not kill him, but having thus mutilated him, let him go wherever he wished. And thus Demetrius paid this penalty to Totila for his unbridled tongue.

[38098] 7.1. Afterward Maximinus too with all his ships put in at Sicily, and coming to Syracuse remained quiet, dreading matters of war. 7.2. When the commanders of the Roman army learned this, they sent to him with much eagerness, begging him to help quickly, both the others and Conon from Naples, who was being most strongly besieged by the barbarians; for all the provisions had now failed them. 7.3. But he, having wasted all the opportunity in this dread, when he both feared the emperor's threats and, being reproached by the others, gave way, himself none the less remained there, but sent the whole army with Herodian and Demetrius and Phazas to Naples, the winter season now pressing most strongly. 7.4. And when the Roman fleet came near Naples, a hard wind came down, stirring up a prodigious storm. 7.5. And the darkness gave way everywhere, and the surge did not allow the sailors to draw up the oars or to do anything else. And through the noise of the breakers they could no longer hear one another, but confusion brilliantly prevailed and the violence of the wind held sway, which carried them, by no means willing, to the shore where the enemy were encamped. 7.6. So the barbarians, boarding the vessels of their opponents at their will, killed and sank them, no one withstanding them. And they took alive many others and Demetrius the general. 7.7. But Herodian and Phazas with some few were able to escape, since their ships had come not very near the camp of the enemy. Such, then, was the state of things regarding the Roman fleet. 7.8. And Totila, fastening a cord about the neck of Demetrius, dragged him to the wall of Naples and ordered him to exhort the besieged no longer to perish trusting in useless hopes, but to be released from great evils by handing over the city to the Goths as quickly as possible. 7.9. For it was impossible for the emperor to send them any further help, but on this fleet all their power and hope had perished. Demetrius said so much as Totila ordered. 7.10. And the besieged, now prodigiously hard pressed by famine and the rest of their want, when they saw the suffering of Demetrius and heard all his words, despairing of all hope, fell into wailings and helplessness, and the city was full of much tumult and lamentation. 7.11. After this Totila, calling them together to the battlements, spoke as follows: “Having no charge or complaint against you, men of Naples, we have now set ourselves to this siege, but in order that, having freed you from most hateful masters, we may be able to repay you the thanks for those things on account of which you suffered the most grievous things from the enemy in this war. 7.12. For it has befallen you alone of all the Italians to have displayed the greatest goodwill toward the nation of the Goths and to have come most unwillingly under your opponents. 7.13. So that even now, being compelled to besiege you along with them, we are ashamed, as is natural, of your good faith, although we make this siege not for the harm of the Neapolitans. 7.14. Do not, then, being distressed at the terrors of the siege, think you ought to hold the Goths in anger. For those eager to benefit their friends are worthy to bear no charge from them, even if they are not compelled to accomplish their kindness toward them through what is pleasant. 7.15. And let no fear of the enemy enter into you, nor, led by what has happened before, think that they will prevail over us; for the strange events of life, which come contrary to expectation by some chance, are wont in time to fall again. 7.16. And we are so well-disposed to you that we grant Conon and all the soldiers to go unharmed wherever they wish, if you give over the city to us and depart from there, keeping all their own goods; and nothing will hinder us from swearing concerning these things and the safety of the Neapolitans.” 7.17. Totila said so much, and both the Neapolitans and all the soldiers with Conon approved. For great necessity of famine oppressed them. 7.18. Keeping their good faith toward the emperor, however, and still waiting for some help to come to them, they agreed to give over the city within thirty days. 7.19. But Totila, wishing to drive away from them all hope from the emperor, fixed a period of three months, on condition that after this they should do what had been agreed. And he affirmed that before this he would make no assault upon the wall, nor use any other plot against them. 7.20. These things, then, had been thus resolved. But the besieged, not waiting for the appointed day (for they were too hard pressed by the want of necessities), a little later received Totila and the barbarians into the city. And the winter was ending, and the eighth year of this war which Procopius has written was coming to a close.

[42893] 8.1. When Totila had taken Naples, he displayed kindness toward the captured that befitted neither an enemy nor a barbarian man. 8.2. For having taken the Romans sick from famine, in whom even the strength of the body had now retreated, and fearing lest, coming suddenly to fullness of food, as is natural, 8.3. they should be choked, he devised the following. Setting guards at the harbour and the gates, he ordered that no one go anywhere from there. 8.4. And he himself provided victuals to all with a certain provident sparingness, in less measure than their desire, adding each day so much of the measure as not to seem to give any constant perception of what was being put in. 8.5. And thus, having strengthened their power and opened the gates, he let each go wherever he wished. 8.6. And Conon and the soldiers with him, all whom he was by no means pleased to have remain there, embarking on ships, he ordered to sail at their will. And they, thinking the withdrawal to Byzantium would bring them shame, purposed to sail quickly to Rome. 8.7. But as the wind was against them, being by no means able to set out from there, they were at a loss, fearing lest it should befall that Totila should disregard what had been agreed because he had conquered, and they should suffer some great evil at his hands. 8.8. When Totila perceived this, calling them all together he consoled them and, strengthening his pledges to them still more, ordered them now to take courage and to mingle with the Gothic army with no fear, and to buy provisions there and, if they needed anything else, to obtain it as from friends. 8.9. And when the wind was still borne against them and much time was wasted, providing horses and beasts of burden and presenting them with travelling supplies, he ordered them to journey to Rome by the road, sending with them some of the notable men among the Goths as escorts. 8.10. And the wall of Naples he undertook to raze to the ground, in order that the Romans, occupying it again and setting out from a strong place, might not give trouble to the Goths. 8.11. For he wished rather to settle matters with them by battle in the open in the plain than to contend by certain devices and stratagems. He razed a great part of it, however, and left the rest. 8.12. About this time a certain Roman, a Calabrian by race, coming to him, accused one of his guards of having done violence to his daughter, who was a maiden, against her will. 8.13. And he, being eager to punish the man's offence, since he did not deny the charge, shut him up in prison. 8.14. But the most notable of the barbarians about him, fearing for him (for he happened to be an energetic man and good in matters of war), gathering at once and coming into the sight of Totila, begged him to remit the charge against the man. 8.15. And he, gently and with no confusion making himself acquainted with what was said, spoke as follows: “Not yielding to an excess of inhumanity nor overjoyed at the misfortunes of kinsmen, but, as far as possible, fearing lest something base should befall the Goths, I set myself to these words, fellow-soldiers. 8.16. I know this, that the great throng of men change the names of things into their opposites. 8.17. For they are wont to call lawlessness kindness, from which it befalls that all good things are destroyed and confounded, and harsh and simply ill-tempered whoever wishes to maintain the laws exactly, in order that, using these names as a screen for their wantonness, they may be able to err more fearlessly and to display their wickedness. 8.18. But I exhort you not to exchange the safety of yourselves for the offence of one man, nor, doing no wrong yourselves, to share in this pollution. For to err and to prevent the punishment of those who have erred, I think, are equal. 8.19. I wish you, then, to make your decision about the present matter, looking at it thus: that now a choice of two things lies before you, either not to give over this man to justice for his wrongs, or for the race of the Goths both to be saved and to keep the mastery of the war. 8.20. For consider: at the beginning of this war we had a multitude of soldiers brilliant in repute and in experience of dangers, and money, to speak briefly, greater than could be numbered, and a prodigious abundance of horses and arms, and all the forts there are among the Italians. For these too seem not altogether useless provisions to those who set themselves to war. 8.21. But when, being ranged under Theudatus, a man who set justice below his desire to be rich, we by no means rendered God propitious to us by our lawlessness in conduct, you know, doubtless, whither our fortune has gone, by what and by how many men we have been defeated. 8.22. But now God, having sufficiently taken the penalty from us for the things in which we erred, again orders our life according to our wish, and, to put it altogether, leads our affairs better for us than according to our hope, since it has befallen us to have conquered the enemy beyond our existing power. 8.23. To maintain, then, the pretext of victory by doing what is just will be more profitable than, going contrary to it, to seem to have become envious against ourselves. 8.24. For it is not possible, it is not, for him who does wrong and uses violence to be of good repute in the contests, but according to the life of each the fortune of war is dispensed.” Totila said so much. 8.25. And the notable men of the Goths, approving what had been said, no longer begged off the guard, but allowed him to do whatever should seem best to him. And he not long afterward put the man to death, and the goods, all that happened to be his, he gave to the woman who had been violated.

[48620] 9.1. While Totila was doing these things, in the meantime the commanders of the Roman army, together with the soldiers, were plundering the goods of the subjects, and left out nothing whatever of insolence and wantonness, but the commanders, keeping mistresses in the forts, held revel, while the soldiers, showing themselves disobedient to their commanders, fell into every form of impropriety. 9.2. And it befell all the Italians to suffer the most grievous things at the hands of both armies. 9.3. For of their fields they were deprived by the enemy, and of all their furniture by the emperor's army. And besides this they were maltreated and destroyed for no reason, being hard pressed by the want of necessities. 9.4. For the soldiers, being by no means able to defend them when they were maltreated by the enemy, not only knew no shame at all at the present situation, but even made the barbarians objects of longing to them by the wrongs they did. 9.5. And Constantianus, being at a loss because of these things, sent a letter to the emperor Justinian, openly declaring that he was unable to hold out in the war against the Goths. 9.6. And the other commanders too, as if confirming the opinion, signified in this letter their agreement in their shrinking from the contest. For the Italians the affairs went on thus. And Totila sent a letter to the senate of the Romans. 9.7. And the writing made plain the following: “Those who wrong their neighbours either through previous ignorance, perhaps, or through some forgetfulness that comes upon them, for these it is right that those who have suffered terrible things be forgiving. For the cause of the error, having befallen, begs off for them the most of the charges. 9.8. But if anyone wrongs another from deliberate purpose alone, for this man it will be left not even ever to contradict concerning what has been done. 9.9. For he would justly bear the charge not only of the deed but also of the purpose. 9.10. Therefore, since these things are so, reckon now what defence it will befall you to have made for the things wrought against the Goths: whether it has befallen you to be ignorant of the benefactions of Theoderic and Amalasuntha, or whether they have become faint in you through time and forgetfulness. 9.11. And yet neither of these is so. For it was not on trifling matters nor toward your forefathers, in the olden time, that they displayed their kindnesses, but in the most necessary things toward you yourselves, lately and recently, O dear Romans. 9.12. But did you, having learned the valour of the Greeks toward their subjects either by hearsay or by experience, thus resolve to give over to them the affairs of the Goths and the Italians? 9.13. And yet you, I think, entertained them most excellently, but what guests and friends of them you found, you know, doubtless, if you remember anything of the reckonings of Alexander. 9.14. For I forbear to speak of the soldiers and the commanders of the soldiers, of whose friendliness and magnanimity you have had the benefit; on account of which it has come about that affairs are in such a state of fortune. 9.15. And let none of you suppose either that I bring these reproaches against them out of the ambition of a young man, or that I, being a ruler of barbarians, make my speech rather boastful. 9.16. For I do not say that the mastery over these men is the work of our valour, but I affirm that they paid a certain penalty for their injustice toward you. 9.17. And yet how would it not seem most absurd that God should avenge you upon them, but that you should cling to their impropriety and be unwilling to be released from the evils arising from it? Give, then, some pretext for your defence toward the Goths 9.18. and for our forgiveness toward you. And you will give it, if, not waiting for the end of the war, but while some hope—and that an unprofitable one—is still left to you, you choose the better course and set right the things wrought against us, which you ought not.” 9.19. So much the writing made plain. And Totila, putting it into the hands of some of the captives, ordered them to go to Rome and give it to those of the senate. 9.20. And they did so. But John prevented those who saw this letter from making any answer to Totila. Wherefore Totila again, writing many little letters and putting into them the most dreadful oaths, swore expressly that the Goths would never do harm to any of the Romans. 9.21. Who in the world conveyed these little letters to Rome I cannot say. For all of them, fixed at an unseasonable hour of the night in the conspicuous places of the city, became known when day came; and the commanders of the Roman army, being gripped by much suspicion against the priests of the Arians, established them all outside the city straightway. 9.22. And Totila, hearing this, sent a part of the army to Calabria and instructed them to make trial of the fort in Dryus. But when those who held the watch-station there were by no means willing to come over to him, he ordered those sent there to set themselves to a siege, while he himself went with the greater part of the army to the regions about Rome. 9.23. When the emperor learned this, he fell into helplessness and was compelled to send Belisarius against Totila, although the Persians were still pressing most strongly. And the winter was ending, and the ninth year of this war which Procopius has written was coming to a close.

[54070] 10.1. Thus Belisarius went into Italy a second time. And since he had very few soldiers (for he was by no means able to detach from the army in Media those who followed him), going about all Thrace and putting forth money, he gathered young volunteers. 10.2. And there was with him, by the emperor's decision, Vitalius the general of the Illyrians, who had just returned from Italy, where he had happened to leave the Illyrian soldiers. 10.3. Both, then, having gathered four thousand, came to Salones, having in mind to go first to Ravenna, and from there to carry on the war in whatever way might be possible. 10.4. For to land in the regions of Rome they were by no means able, neither escaping the notice of the enemy (since they heard that they were encamped both among the Calabrians and the Campanians) nor forcing them in any way whatever. For they went against them not with an equal power. 10.5. In the meantime those besieged in Dryus, the necessities having altogether failed them, came to a parley with the barbarians who were besieging them, on condition that they should give over the fort by agreement, and a fixed day was agreed upon by both for this. 10.6. But Belisarius, loading provisions sufficient for a year's length into ships, ordered Valentinus to sail with them to Dryus, and to lead away the former guards of the fort as quickly as possible (whom he had learned were wasted by sickness and famine), and to establish some of those who sailed with him at the watch-station in their place; for thus he would more easily, they being fresh and in want of none of the necessities, guard the fort in safety. 10.7. So Valentinus, meeting a favourable wind with this fleet, sailed down to Dryus four days before the appointed day, and finding the harbour unguarded, both mastered it and was able with no trouble to enter the fort. 10.8. For the Goths, bold in what had been agreed and suspecting that no obstacle would arise for them in the interval, had now arranged matters about Dryus carelessly and remained quiet. 10.9. But then, seeing the fleet sailing in unexpectedly, they were afraid and broke off the siege, and getting far off from the place encamped and reported all that had befallen them to Totila. By so much did the fort of Dryus come into danger. 10.10. And some of Valentinus' men, wishing to plunder the regions there, made sorties, and meeting the enemy by some chance near the seashore came to close quarters with them. 10.11. And being defeated by far in the battle, the most of them fled into the water of the sea, where, having lost a hundred and seventy, the rest withdrew to the fort. 10.12. And Valentinus, finding the former guards half-dead, led them away, and establishing others fresh in their place, just as Belisarius instructed him, and leaving them provisions for a year's length, came with the rest of the army to Salones. 10.13. And Belisarius, setting out from there with the whole fleet, put in at Pola. And there, ordering the army, he remained some time. 10.14. And Totila, hearing that he had come there, and wishing to learn what power he was bringing, did as follows. There was a certain Bonus, a nephew of John, commander of the garrison in Genua. 10.15. Using his name, he wrote a letter to Belisarius ostensibly from him, as though urging him to come to them as quickly as possible, since they were in certain grievous dangers. 10.16. And selecting five exceedingly clever men, he put the letter into their hands and instructed them to observe exactly the power of Belisarius, pretending that they had been sent by Bonus. 10.17. So Belisarius saw the men who came into his sight with much friendliness, as was his custom. 10.18. And reading the letter, he ordered them to report to Bonus that he would come with his whole army not long hence. And they, observing everything, just as Totila instructed them, returned to the camp of the Goths and affirmed that the power of Belisarius was by no means worthy of account. 10.19. In the meantime Totila took by treachery the city of Tibur, which had an Isaurian garrison, in the following manner. Some of the inhabitants kept guard at the gates together with the Isaurians. 10.20. These, having become at variance with the Isaurians who kept guard with them from no cause arising on their part, brought in by night the enemy who were encamped somewhere close. 10.21. So the Isaurians, taking counsel together, while the city was being taken, were nearly all able to escape. 10.22. But of the inhabitants the Goths spared none, but killed them all, together with the priest of the city, in a manner which, though I know it, I shall by no means mention, in order that I may not leave memorials of inhumanity to later time; among whom perished also Catellus, a man of repute among the Italians. 10.23. And the barbarians held Tibur, and the Romans could no longer bring provisions into Rome from Tuscany through the Tiber. For the city, lying by the river above Rome about a hundred and twenty stades, became for the future a bulwark against those wishing to sail in there.

[59155] 11.1. So matters about Tibur it befell to happen thus. And Belisarius, coming with the whole fleet to Ravenna, gathered together the Goths present and the Roman soldiers and spoke as follows: “Not now for the first time, men, has it befallen that the works of valour have been melted away by baseness. 11.2. For from above this is simply implanted in human affairs, that the wickedness of the basest has had the power both to check and to destroy the many deeds of good men. This same thing has now too brought to ruin the affairs of the emperor. 11.3. To whom it is so much a care to set right the errors that, setting the mastery over the Persians below these things, he has resolved to send me now to you, in order that I may set right and heal if anything has been wrought wrongly by the commanders either toward his own soldiers or toward the Goths. 11.4. Now that nothing be done amiss by anyone is neither human nor outside the nature of affairs, but to set right the errors befits an emperor abundantly and is worthy of consolation enough to those who are loved. 11.5. For not only will it befall you to be freed from your difficulties, but it will at once be added that you both know and enjoy the goodwill of the emperor toward you; than which what could be more worthy to a man of all possessions? 11.6. Since, then, I am present with you for this very purpose, it behoves each of you to use all his power, 11.7. in order that you may have the benefit of the help arising from this. And whoever of you has kinsmen or friends with Totila the tyrant, let him summon them as quickly as possible, making plain the emperor's purpose. 11.8. For thus there would come to you the good things both from peace and from the great emperor. As for myself, I have come here neither desiring to make war on anyone, nor would I ever willingly be an enemy to the emperor's subjects. 11.9. But if, even now, holding it a trifling matter to choose what is better for themselves, these men go against us, it is of necessity that we too use them, most unwillingly, as enemies.” 11.10. Belisarius said so much. But none of his opponents came over to him, neither Goth nor Roman. After this he sent Thurimuth the spearman and some of his 11.11. followers with Vitalius and the Illyrian soldiers to Aemilia, and ordered them to make trial of the places there. 11.12. So Vitalius, with this army, coming about the city of Bononia and taking some of the forts there by agreement, kept quiet in the city of Bononia. 11.13. But not long afterward all the Illyrians who were campaigning with him, suddenly, neither suffering nor hearing any evil, withdrew secretly from there and were conveyed home. 11.14. And sending envoys to the emperor, they begged him to give them pardon, having come to their homes in this manner for no other reason than that, having campaigned in Italy for a long time and by no means receiving their allowances, the public treasury owed them much money. 11.15. And an army of Huns, having fallen upon the Illyrians, it had befallen their children and wives to be enslaved. 11.16. Learning which, and being in want of the necessities among the Italians, they had withdrawn. With these the emperor, being at first angry, then became forgiving. And Totila, learning of the withdrawal of the Illyrians, sent an army against Bononia, to snatch up Vitalius and his men. 11.17. But Vitalius and Thurimuth, laying ambushes beforehand, destroyed many of the assailants 11.18. and turned the rest to flight. There Nazares, a notable man, an Illyrian by race and commander of the soldiers in Illyricum, displayed marvellous deeds against the enemy above all. And thus Thurimuth came to Belisarius at Ravenna. 11.19. Then Belisarius sent three of his guards, Thurimuth and Ricilas and Sabinianus, with a thousand soldiers to the city of Auximus, to help Magnus and the Romans besieged there. 11.20. And they, escaping the notice of Totila and the camp of the enemy, came by night to Auximus and purposed to make certain sorties against their opponents. 11.21. And on the following day, learning about midday that some of the enemy were somewhere close, they went out to meet them, but resolved to send scouts against them first to observe their power, in order that they might not go against them unconsidered. 11.22. But Ricilas the guard of Belisarius (for he happened at that time to be flushed with wine) would not let others go on the scouting, but he himself alone, riding his horse out, went quickly. 11.23. And falling in with three Goths in a precipitous place, at first he took his stand as if to array himself against them; for he had come to the height of courage. But seeing many pouring against him from all sides, he rushed into flight. 11.24. And as his horse stumbled in the difficult ground, a great shout arose from the enemy, and all hurled their javelins at him. When the Romans perceived this, they came to the aid at a run. 11.25. And Ricilas, covered with many spears, died; but Thurimuth and his men, routing their opponents and taking up the corpse, conveyed it to the city of Auximus—the man having drawn a lot for the close of his life not worthy of his valour. 11.26. After this Sabinianus and Thurimuth, taking counsel with Magnus, found it inexpedient for them to make any further stay there, reckoning that they would never be a match for the enemy, who were many, and that, consuming the provisions of the besieged, they would make the city even more quickly capturable for their opponents. 11.27. And when this had been resolved, they themselves and the thousand prepared for the departure, intending to begin the retreat by night; but immediately one of the soldiers, deserting secretly to the camp of the enemy, made known what was being done. 11.28. Totila, then, selecting two thousand men for their valour, when night came on, guarded the roads thirty stades from Auximus, giving perception to no one. 11.29. And when these saw the enemy passing about midnight, drawing their swords they set to work. 11.30. And they killed two hundred of them, while Sabinianus and Thurimuth with the rest, since it was in the dark, were able both to escape notice and to flee to Ariminum. 11.31. The Goths, however, mastered all the beasts of burden, which carried the soldiers' service-baggage and arms and clothing. 11.32. Now there are two towns by the shore of the Ionian Gulf, Pisaurus and Fanus, lying between the cities of Auximus and Ariminum. The houses of these Wittigis had burnt at the beginning of this war, and had razed the walls to about half, in order that the Romans, occupying them, might not give trouble to the Goths. 11.33. One of these, Pisaurus, Belisarius resolved to occupy; for it seemed to him that the place lay suitably for pastures of horses. So sending some of his friends by night, he secretly measured out exactly the breadth and length of each gate. 11.34. And constructing the gates and binding them about with iron, and then putting them into certain skiffs, he sent them, ordering Sabinianus and Thurimuth quickly to fit them to the walls and remain inside the wall, and, when they were in safety, to rebuild, in whatever way, all that had fallen of the wall, throwing in stones and clay and anything else. They, then, did so. 11.35. But Totila, learning what was being done, came against them with a great army. 11.36. And making trial and spending some time there, when he was not able to take it, he withdrew without accomplishing anything to the camp at Auximus. 11.37. And none of the Romans any longer sallied out against the enemy, but each remained inside the walls. And Belisarius, sending two of his guards to Rome, Artasires a Persian and Barbation a Thracian, to guard the city with Bessas, who happened to be there, instructed them by no means to sally out against the enemy. 11.38. And Totila and the army of the Goths, when they perceived that the power of Belisarius was not adequate to array itself against them, resolved to trouble the strongest of the places. 11.39. Wherefore, encamping in Picenum about both Firmum and Asculum, they set themselves to a siege. And the winter was ending, and the tenth year of this war which Procopius has written was coming to a close.

[67405] 12.1. And Belisarius, being by no means able to defend the besieged, sent John the nephew of Vitalian to Byzantium, binding him with the most dreadful oaths that he would be eager to return as quickly as possible, to beg the emperor to send them a large army and great sums of money, and indeed also arms and horses. 12.2. For the soldiers, being very few, were not themselves willing to fight, saying that the public treasury owed them much money and that they themselves were in want of everything. 12.3. And so it was. And he wrote a letter to the emperor about these things. And the writing made plain the following: “We have come to Italy, most mighty emperor, without men and horses and arms and money. Without which, I think, no one would ever be able to carry on a war, if he does not have them sufficiently. 12.4. For going about Thrace and Illyricum most diligently, we gathered very few soldiers, pitiable and having no arms in their hands and altogether unpractised in battle. 12.5. And we see that those left here are neither self-sufficient and are cowed and enslaved in spirit by often being defeated by the enemy; who indeed did not escape their opponents even idly, but by abandoning their horses and throwing their arms to the ground. 12.6. And for us to provide a revenue of money from Italy is impossible, since it has again been occupied by the enemy. 12.7. Wherefore, having become overdue concerning the soldiers' allowances, we are by no means able to give them orders; for the debt has taken from us our freedom of speech. 12.8. And know this well also, O master, that of those who campaign under you the greater number happen to have deserted to the enemy. 12.9. If, then, it were necessary for Belisarius alone to be sent to Italy, your preparations for the war have been made most excellently; for I am already in the very midst of the Italians. But if you wish to prevail over the enemy in the war, the other things too must be furnished. 12.10. For a general, I think, could not exist without those who serve under him. My guards and shield-bearers, then, most of all, ought to be sent to me, and after them a very great multitude of Huns and other barbarians, to whom money must now be given.” 12.11. So much Belisarius wrote. And John, spending much time in Byzantium, accomplished nothing of what he came for, but married a daughter of Germanus the cousin of the emperor. 12.12. In the meantime Totila took Firmum and Asculum by agreement; and coming to Tuscany, he besieged Spolitium and Asisium. Now Herodian commanded the watch-station in Spolitium, and Sisifridus that in Asisium, a Goth by race but exceedingly well-disposed to the Romans and the emperor's affairs. 12.13. Herodian, then, came to a parley with the enemy, on condition that they should remain quiet for thirty days. 12.14. Of which, if no help should come to them, he would give over both himself and the city, together with the soldiers and the Goths dwelling there. And he gave his son as a hostage on this agreement. 12.15. But when the appointed day was present and no army of the Romans came from anywhere, Herodian and all who held the garrison there put themselves and Spolitium into the hands of Totila and the Goths according to what had been agreed. 12.16. And they say that Herodian gave over both himself and Spolitium to the Goths out of his hatred toward Belisarius; for Belisarius had threatened that he would make him render accounts of his conduct. So matters about Spolitium went on thus. 12.17. And Sisifridus, making a sortie with his followers, both lost the most of those with him and himself died. 12.18. And the people of Asisium, being at a loss before the present situation, straightway gave over the city to the enemy. And Totila, sending immediately to Cyprianus, saw fit that he should give over Perusia to him, threatening him if he should disobey, but promising to present him with great sums of money 12.19. if he should accomplish this. And when nothing proceeded for him through Cyprianus, he persuaded with money one of his guards, Ulifus by name, to make away with the man by treachery. 12.20. So Ulifus, falling in with Cyprianus alone, killed him and fled to Totila. But none the less the soldiers of Cyprianus guarded the city for the emperor; wherefore the Goths resolved to withdraw from there.

[71746] 13.1. After this Totila went against Rome, and when he came near, he set himself to a siege; the farmers, however, he did no harm to throughout all Italy, but ordered them to till the land fearlessly, as was their custom, for ever, paying him the taxes which formerly they had been wont to pay both to the public treasury and to the owners. 13.2. And when some of the Goths came close to the wall of Rome, Artasires and Barbation, taking with them many of their followers, Bessas by no means approving, made a sortie against them. 13.3. And they killed many straightway and turned the rest to flight. Following them and rushing far out into the pursuit, they fell upon ambushes set by the enemy. 13.4. There, losing the most of their men, they themselves with some few barely escaped, and for the future no longer dared to sally out against their opponents, although they pressed close. 13.5. And from this an exact famine oppressed the Romans, who could no longer bring in any of the necessities from the fields, and were shut off from the cargoes coming from the sea. 13.6. For when the Goths took Naples, establishing a fleet there of many vessels, both at the islands called the islands of Aeolus and at all others lying there, they exactly guarded the passage. 13.7. So all the ships that sailed up from Sicily toward the harbour of Rome all fell, with their very crews, under their hands. 13.8. And Totila, sending an army to Aemilia, ordered them to take the city of Placentia either by force or by agreement. 13.9. This is the first city of the country of Aemilia, having a strong fortification. And it lies by the river Eridanus and was the only one of the places there still left subject to the Romans. 13.10. And when this army came near Placentia, they made proposals to the guards there, that they should give over the city to Totila and the Goths by agreement. 13.11. But when nothing proceeded for them, encamping there, they set themselves to a siege, perceiving that those in the city were in want of provisions. 13.12. Then there came to the commanders of the emperor's army in Rome a suspicion of treachery against Cethegus, a patrician man and first of the Roman senate. Wherefore he went off to Centumcellae. 13.13. And Belisarius, fearing both for Rome and for the whole situation, since it was impossible to help from Ravenna, especially with a small army, resolved to set out from there and occupy the regions about Rome, in order that, being near, he might be able to help those who were suffering there. 13.14. And he repented of his having come at first to Ravenna, which, persuaded by Vitalius, he had previously done, not for the advantage of the emperor's affairs, since, shutting himself up there, he had given the enemy the power to manage at their will the turn of the war. 13.15. And it seemed to me either that Belisarius chose the worse course—since it was then fated that evil should come to the Romans—or that he had deliberated the better, but that God became a hindrance, having in mind to help Totila and the Goths, and from this the best of his plans were turned to the entire opposite for Belisarius. 13.16. For for those on whom the wind of fortune blows from a favourable quarter, even when they deliberate the worst, nothing terrible will meet them, the divine power turning these things about to everything advantageous; 13.17. but to a man who is unfortunate, I think, no good counsel is at hand, his knowledge and true opinion being taken from him by his destiny to suffer. 13.18. And if he should ever deliberate anything of what is needful, fortune, blowing against the one who deliberated, straightway turns his good counsel toward the most wicked of issues. 13.19. But whether these things are so or otherwise I cannot say. And Belisarius, establishing Justinus over the guard of Ravenna, himself with some few was conveyed from there through Dalmatia and the places there to Epidamnus, where, awaiting an army from Byzantium, he remained quiet. And writing a letter to the emperor, he reported his present fortunes. 13.20. And the emperor not long afterward sent to him John the nephew of Vitalian and Isaaces the Armenian, the brother of Aratius and Narses, with an army of barbarian and Roman soldiers. 13.21. These, coming to Epidamnus, joined Belisarius. And he sent Narses the eunuch to the rulers of the Eruli, in order that he might persuade many of them to campaign in Italy. 13.22. And many of the Eruli followed him, of whom, among others, Philemuth was commander, and with him they came to the regions of Thrace. For there, wintering, they were to set out at the beginning of spring to Belisarius. 13.23. And there was with them also John, whom they called by the surname Phagas. And on this journey it befell that a certain fortune wrought great good for the Romans unexpectedly. 13.24. For a large throng of Sclaveni barbarians had lately crossed the river Ister, and plundered the regions there, and enslaved a very great number of Romans. 13.25. Against these the Eruli, coming suddenly to close quarters and conquering them contrary to expectation, though they far surpassed them in measure, both killed them and released all the captives to go home. 13.26. And then Narses, finding also a certain man assuming the name of Chilbudius, an illustrious man who had once been a general of the Romans, was easily able to convict the imposture. And what this was I shall now make plain.

[77198] 14.1. There was a certain Chilbudius, of the household of the emperor Justinian, exceedingly energetic in matters of war, and so superior to money that in place of the greatest possession in his property he held the possessing of nothing; 14.2. this Chilbudius the emperor, when he had held the imperial power for four years, proclaiming general of Thrace, established over the guard of the river Ister, ordering him to keep watch that the river should no longer be crossable by the barbarians there, since the Huns and Antae and Sclaveni, having often made the crossing of it, had wrought irreparable deeds against the Romans. 14.3. And Chilbudius became so terrible to the barbarians that for a period of three years, all the time that he was staying there with this office, not only was no one able to cross the Ister against the Romans, but the Romans, often going to the opposite mainland with Chilbudius, both killed and enslaved the barbarians there. 14.4. But three years later Chilbudius crossed the river where he was wont with a small army, and the Sclaveni met him in full force. 14.5. And when a fierce battle had taken place, many of the Romans fell and Chilbudius the general. 14.6. And for the future the river became crossable at all times for the barbarians at their will, and the affairs of the Romans became easy to assail, and the whole Roman empire was by no means able in this matter to become a counterpoise to the valour of one man. 14.7. And some time later the Antae and Sclaveni, having become at variance with one another, came to close quarters, where it befell the Antae to be defeated by their opponents. 14.8. In this battle a Sclavenian man took prisoner one of the enemy, Chilbudius by name, just growing his first beard, and taking him went home. 14.9. This Chilbudius, as time went on, became most well-disposed to his master and energetic against the enemy. 14.10. And often having risked himself ahead of his master, he distinguished himself exceedingly and was able to gird himself with great fame from this. 14.11. About this time the Antae, falling upon the regions of Thrace, plundered and enslaved many of the Romans there. Leading them off, they were conveyed back to their native abodes. 14.12. And fortune brought one of these captives to a kindly and gentle master. But this man was very mischievous and such as to circumvent those he met by deceit. 14.13. And since, wishing to return to the land of the Romans, he was by no means able, he devised the following. Coming into the sight of his master, he praised his kindness and affirmed that on account of this many good things would come to him from God, and that he himself would in no way appear thankless to a most kindly master, but that, if he should be willing to listen to him as he set forth the best advice, he would make him master of great wealth not long hence. 14.14. For there was in the nation of the Sclaveni Chilbudius, the man who had been general of the Romans, in the position of a slave, escaping the notice of all the barbarians as to who he was. 14.15. If, then, it should be his wish to put forth the price of Chilbudius and to convey the man to the land of the Romans, it was not unlikely that he would gird himself with good repute and a very great quantity of wealth from the emperor. 14.16. The Roman, saying this, straightway persuaded his master, and with him came into the midst of the Sclaveni; for these barbarians now treated with one another and mingled fearlessly. Putting forth, then, much money to the master of Chilbudius, they bought the man and went off with him immediately. 14.17. And when they were in their own abodes, the purchaser inquired of the man whether he were Chilbudius himself, the general of the Romans. 14.18. And he did not refuse to tell what was, with the true account, in order, that he himself was an Ante by race, and fighting with his kinsmen against the Sclaveni, who were then enemies to them, had been taken by one of his opponents; but now, since he had come to his native abodes, he would for the future be free himself too, according to the law. 14.19. So he who had put forth the gold for him, falling into speechlessness, was distressed, having failed of a not moderate hope. 14.20. But the Roman, wishing to console the man and to thrust aside the truth, in order that nothing might be difficult for him about his return home, affirmed that this very man was indeed that Chilbudius, but that, being afraid because he was in the midst of barbarians, he was by no means willing to reveal the whole account; if, however, he should come to the land of the Romans, he would not only not hide the true account, but would even, as is natural, pride himself on this name. At first, then, these things were done secretly from the other barbarians. 14.21. But when the report, going about, came to all, nearly all the Antae gathered for this, and saw fit that the matter should be common, thinking that great good would come to them, since they had now become masters of Chilbudius the general of the Romans. 14.22. For these nations, the Sclaveni and the Antae, are not ruled by one man, but live in a democracy from of old, and for this reason both the advantageous and the difficult matters of affairs are always brought to common counsel among them. And in like manner, to speak generally, all other things too are and have been customary among these barbarians from above. 14.23. For they hold that one god, the maker of lightning, is alone lord of all, and they sacrifice to him oxen and all victims; but fate they neither know nor in any other way admit that it has any power among men, but whenever death is already at their feet, either being seized by sickness or setting themselves to war, they promise that, if they escape, they will straightway make a sacrifice to the god in return for their life, and having escaped they sacrifice what they promised, and they think that their safety has been bought by this sacrifice. 14.24. They worship, however, both rivers and nymphs and certain other divinities, and sacrifice to all of these, and make their divinations in these sacrifices. They dwell in wretched huts, scattered far from one another, and each for the most part changes the place of his habitation. 14.25. When they set themselves to battle, the most go against the enemy on foot, holding small shields and javelins in their hands, but they put on no corselet. 14.26. And some have neither tunic nor cloak, but, fitting on only their trousers up to the privy parts, thus set themselves to engagement with their opponents. And there is for both one tongue, altogether barbarous. 14.27. Nor indeed do they differ at all from one another in appearance. For they are all both tall and exceedingly strong, and as to their bodies and hair they are neither very white or fair, nor are they at all turned wholly to the dark, but they are all ruddy. 14.28. And they have a hard and neglected mode of life, like the Massagetae, and like them they are most continually full of filth; yet they are by no means wicked or mischievous, but even in their simplicity they preserve the Hunnic character. 14.29. And indeed there was one name for both the Sclaveni and the Antae from above; for both were anciently called Spori, because, I think, they dwell in the land scattered (sporaden), in scattered tents. 14.30. Wherefore they hold a large land; for they occupy the most of the other bank of the Ister. So much, then, for this people. 14.31. And the Antae, then gathering, as has been said, compelled this man to agree with them that he was Chilbudius himself, the general of the Romans. 14.32. And when he denied it, they threatened to punish him. While these things were being done thus, in the meantime the emperor Justinian, sending certain envoys to these barbarians, saw fit that they should all settle together in an ancient city, Turris by name, which lies beyond the river Ister, Trajan the emperor of the Romans having built it in earlier times, and which had been deserted from of old, the barbarians there having plundered it. 14.33. This and the land about it the emperor Justinian agreed to give them, as belonging at the first to the Romans, and to help settle them with all his might, and to put forth great sums of money to them, on condition that, being for the future in treaty with him, they should be perpetually a hindrance to the Huns when they wished to overrun the Roman empire. 14.34. When the barbarians heard this, they approved and promised to do everything, if he should establish Chilbudius again as general of the Romans and give him to them as a co-settler, affirming that this very man, as they wished, was Chilbudius. 14.35. And the man, lifted up by these hopes, now himself too both wished and affirmed that he was Chilbudius the general of the Romans. And as he was setting out for Byzantium on these terms, Narses caught him on this journey. 14.36. And joining him, when he found the man dissembling (although he uttered the Latin tongue and had already learned much of the marks of Chilbudius and was sufficiently able to play the part), he shut him up in prison and compelled him to tell the whole account, and thus brought him with himself to Byzantium. But I now return to the point from which I digressed.

[86522] 15.1. The emperor, then, was doing these things which have been shown by me. In the meantime Belisarius sent Valentinus and one of his own guards, Phocas by name, exceedingly good in matters of war, with an army to the harbour of the Romans, in order that they might guard the fort in Portus together with the garrison there, whom Innocentius commanded, and, wherever it should be possible for them, making sorties, throw into confusion the camp of the enemy. 15.2. So Valentinus and Phocas, sending secretly to Rome, signified to Bessas that they were about immediately to attack the stockade of their opponents by a sudden raid; it would be needful, then, for him too, selecting the most warlike of the soldiers in Rome, when he perceived the raid, to come to the aid at a run, in order that both might be able to do the barbarians some great harm. 15.3. But this by no means pleased Bessas, although he had three thousand soldiers with him. Wherefore Valentinus and Phocas, with five hundred, attacking the camp of the enemy unexpectedly, killed some few, and a perception of the tumult there quickly came to the besieged. 15.4. But when no one sallied out from the city, they quickly withdrew to the harbour altogether unharmed. 15.5. And sending to Bessas again, they charged that some unbefitting shrinking had fallen upon him, but affirming that a little later they would make another sortie against the enemy, they exhorted him too in due season to attack the barbarians with all his might. 15.6. But he none the less refused to risk himself by sallying out against his opponents. Valentinus and Phocas, however, purposed to fall upon the enemy with a larger army and were now in readiness. 15.7. But a certain soldier, ranged under Innocentius, deserting to Totila, announced that on the following day there would be an assault from Portus against them. 15.8. And he resolved to lay ambushes of warlike men in the places that were suitable for this. There on the following day Valentinus and Phocas, falling upon them with their followers, lost the most and themselves died. And some few, barely escaping, were conveyed to Portus. 15.9. At that time too Vigilius, the high priest of Rome, staying in Sicily, sent as many ships as possible filled with grain, thinking that in some way they would be able to bring the cargoes into Rome for those conveying them. 15.10. So these ships sailed toward the harbour of the Romans, but the enemy, perceiving this and anticipating by a short time, were in the harbour and hid themselves inside the walls, in order that, as soon as the ships should put in there, they might master them with no trouble. 15.11. Observing which, those who held the garrison in Portus all went up onto the battlements and, shaking their garments, wished to signal to those in the ships not to go forward, but to turn aside elsewhere, 15.12. wherever they chanced. But they, not understanding what was being done, but thinking that the Romans in Portus rejoiced and were inviting them to the harbour, the wind being favourable to them, were quickly inside the harbour. 15.13. Now there sailed in the ships many other Romans and a certain bishop, Valentinus by name. And the barbarians, rising from the ambushes, mastered all the vessels, no one defending himself. 15.14. And taking the bishop alive, they led him to Totila, but all the others they killed, and went off dragging the ships with their cargoes. 15.15. Totila inquired of this priest whatever he wished, and bringing it against him that he by no means told the truth, cut off both his hands. 15.16. These things, then, it befell to happen thus. And the winter was ending, and the eleventh year of this war which Procopius has written was coming to a close.

[90269] 16.1. And Vigilius, the high priest of Rome, came to the emperor at Byzantium, summoned from Sicily. For he happened to have been staying in Sicily a long time on this account. 16.2. About this time the Romans besieged in Placentia, all the necessities having now altogether failed them, came, forced by famine, to certain unholy foods. 16.3. And indeed they tasted of one another. And on account of this they gave over both themselves and Placentia to the Goths by agreement. 16.4. These things, then, proceeded thus, and from Rome, besieged by Totila, all the necessities had now failed. 16.5. There was a certain man among the priests of Rome, Pelagius by name, a deacon in rank, who, having stayed a long time in Byzantium, had become a most intimate friend of the emperor Justinian, and being girt with great wealth had happened to come to Rome a little before. 16.6. And in this siege, having put forth the most of his money to those in want of the necessities, being of repute even before among all the Italians, he bore off, as is natural, a greater fame for kindness. 16.7. This Pelagius the Romans, when they were suffering helpless things from famine, persuaded both to go to Totila and to arrange for them a truce of some few days, on condition that, if no help should come to them from Byzantium within this truce, they would give over both themselves and the city to the Goths by agreement. 16.8. On this embassy Pelagius came to Totila. And Totila, greeting him with much reverence and friendliness, spoke first as follows: 16.9. “To all barbarians, for the most part, it is a custom to revere the office of envoys, but to me it has been from above an object of eagerness to honour those who lay claim to any of the virtue which you yourself possess, and otherwise too. 16.10. And honour and insult toward an envoy I think are distinguished not by the gentleness of countenance nor by the loftiness of words of those who receive him, but in this, whether one tells him the truth or uses unsound speech to him. 16.11. For it befalls that man to be especially honoured to whom, openly revealing the true account, they thus send the man away, but for him to be especially insulted who, having heard hollow and fabricated words, goes off and departs. 16.12. Except for three things, then, O Pelagius, of all the rest, whatever you may ask, you will never fail to obtain it from us. 16.13. Concerning which it is better that you, guarding yourself, keep silence, in order that, having become most responsible to yourself for accomplishing nothing of what you came for, you may not lay upon us the blame concerning them. 16.14. For from asking unsuitably about present matters, the accomplishing of nothing is for the most part implanted. And I say, in order that you may make your speeches neither for the sake of any of the Sicilians, nor of the walls of Rome, nor of the slaves who have come over to us. 16.15. For it is not possible either that the Goths use sparing toward any of the Sicilians, or that this wall stand, or that the slaves who have campaigned with us be slaves to their former owners. And in order that I may not seem to put these things forward with an unreasoning purpose, I shall dissolve the suspicion by telling at once the reasons. 16.16. The island was prosperous from of old in revenue of money and in abundance of all crops growing there, so that it not only sufficed those who dwelt in it, but you Romans, importing from there each year, had the carrying of provisions sufficiently. 16.17. Wherefore at the beginning the Romans begged Theoderic not to establish a garrison of many Goths there, in order that nothing might be a hindrance to their freedom or their other prosperity. 16.18. These things being so, the army of the enemy sailed down to Sicily, neither in multitude of men nor in any other thing whatever a match for us. 16.19. But the Sicilians, seeing the fleet, did not report these things to the Goths, did not shut themselves up in the forts, did not resolve to give any offence to their opponents, but throwing open the gates of the cities with all eagerness received the army of the enemy with upturned hands, like, I think, the most untrustworthy of slaves, watching from a long time for the chance to escape from the hand of their owners and to find new and unknown masters for themselves. 16.20. And setting out from there as from a bulwark, the enemy held the rest of Italy with no toil and occupied this Rome, bringing in from Sicily such a quantity of grain as to hold out against a siege for a year's length for all the Romans. 16.21. Such, then, is the case of the Sicilians, for whom there will be no means for the Goths to be forgiving, the magnitude of their offences taking from those who have done wrong all pity. 16.22. Now the enemy, having shut themselves up inside these walls, by no means resolved to come down into the plain and array themselves against us, but by stratagems and circumventions always and every day driving the Goths back, they have become masters of our affairs unexpectedly. 16.23. In order, then, that we may not suffer this same thing afterward too, it is worth while to take forethought. For those who, once tripped up by ignorance, have fallen into the same misfortune again, not foreseeing the calamity already become familiar to them by experience—this seems to be not an opposition of fortune, but, as is natural, 16.24. to come round to the folly of those who have failed. And one might add that the razing of the wall of Rome will above all be advantageous to you. For no longer being shut up with others and shut off from all the necessities, you will not be besieged by those who come against you, but both sides will risk themselves in battle against one another, and you will become the prize of the victors without dangers of your own. 16.25. As for the slaves who have come over to us, we shall say so much, that, if we should learn that we are now putting into your hands those who arrayed themselves with us against your opponents and who received from us the agreement that we would never give them over to their former owners, we shall not even keep good faith toward you. 16.26. For it is not possible, it is not, that he who disregards his agreements toward the most pitiable of all should display his purpose firm toward any other, but, as if of another mark of his nature, he is wont always to carry his faithlessness round to all who deal with him.” 16.27. Totila said so much, and Pelagius answered as follows: “You have made me your earnest object above all, O noble man, and, alleging the name of the embassy, have ranged us in the most dishonoured portion. 16.28. For to insult a man who is both a friend and an envoy, I think, is not he who would strike him on the cheek and use other wantonness toward him, but he who would resolve to send away unsuccessful the man who came to him. 16.29. For men are wont to set themselves to the embassy not in order that they may obtain some honour from those who receive them, but in order that, having accomplished some good for those who sent them, they may return to them. 16.30. So that it will be more advantageous to have been reviled and accomplished something of what they came for than, having heard kindlier words, to return having failed of their hope. To beg off, then, I do not know why I should about these things which you yourself have said. 16.31. For why should one trouble him who, before the defence, has refused the agreement? But this I would not keep silent, that it is plain how great kindness you intend to display toward the Romans who have taken up arms against you, who have resolved to bring an irrevocable hatred against the Sicilians, who have in no way met you. 16.32. But I, for my part, leaving aside my entreaty to you, shall transfer my embassy to God, who is wont to be wrathful against those who despise supplications.”

[98192] 17.1. Having said so much, Pelagius departed. And the Romans, when they saw him returning unsuccessful, fell into much helplessness. And the famine, growing ever more at its height, wrought irreparable things upon them each day. The soldiers, however, had not yet run out of the necessities, but still held out. 17.2. Wherefore the Romans, gathering in a body and coming to the commanders of the emperor's army, Bessas and Conon, in tears and with much wailing spoke as follows: “We see, O generals, that our present fortunes are such that, even if we were able to do you some unholy deed, this charge would bring upon us no blame. 17.3. For the excess of necessity has its defence in itself. But now, being unable to defend ourselves by deed, we have come to you to make plain in word and to bewail our calamity. Hear us gently, not being thrown into confusion by the boldness of our words, but measuring this by the height of our suffering. 17.4. For he who has been compelled to despair of safety is no longer able for the rest to preserve decorum either in deeds or in words. 17.5. Think of us, O generals, neither as Romans nor as kinsmen to you, nor as having become of the same character as the customs of the state, nor as having received the emperor's army into the city of our own will, but as being enemies from the first and having taken up arms against you, then defeated in battle, having become your captive slaves taken in war according to the law of war. 17.6. And furnish to your captives food not sufficient for our need, but having enough to keep us alive, in order that we who survive may in turn serve you in whatever it is fitting for servants to render to their owners. 17.7. But if this is not easy or pleasing to you, but you see fit to release us from your hand, gaining this, that you do not toil over the burials of slaves; and if not even this is left to us, see fit to kill us and do not deprive us of a temperate death nor grudge us the death sweetest of all, but by a single act release the Romans from countless 17.8. difficulties.” When Bessas and his men heard this, they said that to furnish them provisions was impossible, and to kill them unholy, yet that to release them was not without danger. And they affirmed that Belisarius and the army from Byzantium would be present immediately, and consoling them in this way dismissed them. 17.9. But the famine, growing still more at its height and being prolonged, rose to a great measure of evil, suggesting foods strange and beyond the bounds of nature. 17.10. At first, then, Bessas and Conon, who commanded the garrison in Rome (for they happened to have stored within the wall of Rome a great measure of grain), and the soldiers, cutting off from their own need, sold to the wealthy of the Romans for great sums of money; for the price of a measure had become seven gold pieces. 17.11. But those whose household affairs did not go so well as to be able to share in food of such worth, putting down a quarter of the price and filling a measure with bran, ate it, necessity making their food sweetest and most luxurious to them. 17.12. And an ox, which the shield-bearers of Bessas might take in their sorties, they sold for fifty gold pieces. And whoever had a dead horse or anything else of the kind was ranked among the exceedingly fortunate, since he could luxuriate on the flesh of a dead animal. 17.13. But the whole rest of the throng ate only nettles, of which many grow about the wall and everywhere in the ruins of the city. 17.14. And in order that the sharpness of the herb might not bite their lips and the parts about the throat, they boiled them with care and ate them. 17.15. So long, then, as the Romans had gold coin, thus, as has been said, they bought the grain and the bran and got off. But when this had now failed them, those who carried their furniture to the market exchanged all of it for their daily life. 17.16. But at last, when the emperor's soldiers had no grain to sell to the Romans (except indeed that a little was still left to Bessas), and the Romans had nothing to buy with, all looked to the nettles. 17.17. And as this food was not sufficient for them—since they could not even come to fullness of it at all—their flesh for the most part became wasted, and their colour, coming little by little to lividness, made them most like phantoms. 17.18. And many, while still walking and chewing the nettles with their teeth, died suddenly and fell to the ground. 17.19. And now they even ate one another's dung. And many, hard pressed by famine, made away with themselves, since they no longer found dogs or mice or any other dead animal on which to feed. 17.20. There was a certain Roman man there, the father of five children, whom the children, standing around him and laying hold of his garment, asked for food. 17.21. And he, neither wailing nor showing that he was thrown into confusion, but most strongly hiding the whole suffering in his mind, ordered the children to follow him as though to receive food. 17.22. And when he came to the bridge of the Tiber, binding his garment over his face and covering his eyes with it, he leaped from the bridge into the water of the Tiber, the children and all the Romans there watching what was being done. 17.23. And for the rest the commanders of the emperor, taking other money, released the Romans, as many as wished to depart from there. 17.24. And some few being left there, all the others went off, fleeing as each had become able. And the most of them, their strength being already withered away by famine, died as they sailed or went on the road. 17.25. And many, caught by the enemy on the road, perished. To this had fortune gone for the senate and people of Rome.
