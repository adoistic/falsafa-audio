CHAPTER: 28-book-28



[0] 28. Survey the twenty-eighth, where you may behold the Cyclopes' fiery War-rage rolling abundant.

[99] There a strife arose past all soothing; for the two,

[153] Faunus and Aristaeus, drove one common War,

[198] and Aeacus marched beside them, doing deeds worthy

[250] of Zeus his own father, stretching across his back

[302] a bronze shield of cunning workmanship, in whose round

[358] many marvels were studded, the labour of the Lemnian anvil.

[419] And the host had armed itself, of many fashions, for the fray with the Indians

[499] who pressed on in their droves; one, guiding his richly wrought car

[568] swiftly into the conflict with flesh-cutting ivy,

[619] mounted upon leopards; another, with bristling harness,

[676] yoked the mountain-ranging chariot of Erythraean lions

[732] and drove the grim pair; and another, dismayed by none,

[789] hurling at the dark ranks of the Indians,

[832] unshaken, took joy in lashing an unbridled bull,

[882] and one, springing up onto the back of Cybele's bear,

[937] charged at the foe, and whirling his wine-dark thyrsus

[993] put to flight the drivers of the long-shanked elephants;

[1051] another, casting at the host with flesh-cutting ivy,

[1105] held neither sword nor round shield, nor the deadly

[1158] spear of battle, but, shaking the curling leaves of plants,

[1219] slew a bronze-clad man with a slender frond.

[1265] And a thunderous din crashed out, like a pipe's blast;

[1321] the Sileni shouted; the Bacchant women advanced to war,

[1378] flinging fawnskins like breastplates across their bosoms.

[1437] And one of the mountain-roaming Satyrs, as though driving a colt,

[1504] with legs astride sat upon the back of a lioness.

[1555] The Indians raised their answering war-cry, and, mustering its fighters,

[1629] the barbarian pipe of War, host-gathering, blared aloud;

[1687] garlands were on their helmets, breastplate clashed on aegis,

[1750] the thyrsus rioted against the spears, and against the buskins

[1814] stood matched the answering greaves; and of the yoke-fellow bearers

[1883] the lines of oxhide shields leaned one against another,

[1940] and footmen against footmen, and against the high-crested head

[2004] the Pelasgian helmet thrust the Mygdonian helmet.

[2055] And there was a turmoil of champions, each in his own kind: one, lifting himself,

[2138] whirled the airborne leap of the Bacchic dance,

[2187] one, fallen, groaned, another beat time with his sandal,

[2245] one, struck, gasped out his life, another bounded for Lyaeus;

[2308] another, hurling a martial sound from his lips,

[2357] sang the spear of Ares, while one sang the banquet of Dionysus;

[2422] and War crashed in concert with the rite of Bromius,

[2476] the euhan rattles rang out, and, leader of the tumult,

[2532] the trumpet, mustering the people, sounded with the lyre,

[2591] mixed gore with the libation, and stirred slaughter into the dance.

[2660] There by far the first, light on his own foot springing forth,

[2724] Phalineus hurled his javelin against Deriades,

[2772] and struck the unbreakable iron tunic;

[2812] yet the deadly point, for all its straining, did not touch the flesh,

[2883] but, glancing aside, fixed itself in the ground; and in his fury

[2949] valiant Corymbasus, marking the foe rushing close

[3000] upon Deriades, came upon him, and as he sped on

[3049] severed his throat full in the middle, striking with his blade,

[3114] and reaped off his head; and as the skull was cleft

[3167] Phalineus, blood-stained and headless, fell to the ground.

[3227] And about him a clamorous battle rose; the very tip

[3280] of Phlogius' brow Dexiochus shaved away with bronze,

[3334] striking the brow's edge as the helmet split in two;

[3388] but he, in terror, shifting his knee a little from his knee,

[3450] sheltered beneath the long oxhide of his brother,

[3501] as when Ajax received his brother, the archer Teucer

[3555] of Dardanian stock, into his sevenfold shield,

[3603] hiding the kinsman who shared his toil behind a father's buckler.

[3670] At once Corymbasus, drawing his sword from the sheath,

[3726] flung Dexiochus headlong with a stroke across the neck;

[3783] and swiftly, leaping upon the gasping corpse, ran about it

[3843] frenzied Clytius, leader of the footmen; and high-plumed,

[3902] quick and dismayed, he hurled at Deriades;

[3946] but Hera turned the champion's spear aside,

[3991] angered both at Clytius and at India-slaying Dionysus;

[4047] yet the swift leader did not wholly miss; for, piercing

[4104] the monstrous throat of the irresistible beast,

[4153] he slew the straight-standing elephant of Deriades;

[4206] and, labouring in its anguish, the towering beast, ambidextrous,

[4272] shook the whole car with its dark neck;

[4313] and tossing the crooked jaw that juts before its face,

[4369] blood-stained, it strained back the bonds of the yoking harness;

[4435] but the swift-handed driver, bending the well-fastened yoke

[4496] with his sword, cut the team-binding traces

[4541] of the neck-straps; and from the broad stall

[4587] Celaeneus took another, high to view, and yoked it new.

[4644] And Clytius was bold, in the hope of unreproached victory;

[4704] he called out to the slayer of Dexiochus in a raving voice,

[4765] pouring a deadly word upon the insolent throat:

[4814] "Stand, dog, do not flee, Corymbasus, and I will teach you

[4874] what manner of javelin-men attend on Lyaeus.

[4920] You I will carry off as spoil to Phrygia, and the cities of the Indians

[4993] this spear of mine shall ravage, and after India-slaying victory

[5059] I will make Deriades the servant of Dionysus;

[5106] let some unwooed girl loose her maidenhood,

[5151] receiving the shaggy-breasted wedlock of a Satyr,

[5202] an Indian woman defiled beside the Mygdonian Hermus."

[5257] So he spoke, and Corymbasus was wroth, and while Clytius

[5315] uttered his late-battle words, he sheared through his throat;

[5378] and the head flew up aloft at the leap of Fate,

[5427] sprinkling the dust with bloody drops.

[5467] And, leaving the dancing corpse to whirl back again,

[5521] Corymbasus put the Sileni to flight, foremost of the Indians,

[5584] foremost in manhood after Morrheus and the king.

[5634] And striking the spearman Sebes above the rim of the breast

[5695] he drove the bronze spear into his flesh, and as the bloody

[5756] shaft was dragged out he laid him in the heaped dust.

[5811] Then he rushed at Oenomaus; he, fleeing like the winds,

[5868] withdrew to the host of Bromius with terrified foot;

[5922] and marking him, Corymbasus pursued behind, and in his back

[5983] full in the middle drove the spear; rushing in its onset,

[6042] the point sprang out by the navel through the belly's far side;

[6107] and he, pierced about with the bloody iron,

[6152] fell forward, freshly slain, and slid into the dust;

[6206] and over his eyelids the death-bringing darkness covered him.

[6269] Nor did the monstrous champion cease from battle; but four

[6329] well-helmeted fighters were slain by one slayer,

[6379] Tyndarius and Thoon and Autesion and Onites.

[6425] And many a freshly slaughtered corpse there was, not falling

[6487] forward to the ground, nor stretched supine on the floor;

[6546] but, dead, unshaken, it propped itself upon the earth,

[6602] altogether like a champion still fighting, as though brandishing a spear,

[6677] as though stretching the swift bow and drawing the shaft at its mark.

[6748] And a valiant corpse, longing for War even after its doom,

[6808] forced the threads of the Fates, like a light spear

[6861] poised to strike, pierced from the scattered bows

[6912] by their shafts from head to the very feet,

[6957] an upright image of Ares; and the fighters gazed

[7007] with marvelling eyes upon the spearman who was dead,

[7061] still gripping his spear and not casting away his shield,

[7120] a dead javelin-man and a breathless shield-bearer.

[7172] And one, struck by the Athenian's grim iron,

[7218] reaped off his right hand, smiting the arm's end;

[7269] and the hand, tumbling in a dancing leap of slaughter,

[7325] fell freshly severed, entwined with its fellow shoulder,

[7383] streaking the streaming back with tawny dust.

[7430] And now, drawing the long spear of the leaping hand,

[7484] with far-flung shaft he would have had his War recovered,

[7543] and the left hand, spear-wielding, fought in turn its match;

[7605] but the hostile man, coming from the opposite path, forestalled him,

[7675] and with answering blade struck the left hand off at the root;

[7739] and the palm fell to the ground, and, casting at the slayer,

[7801] the far-flung stream of bloody dew sprinkled it

[7850] in crimson drops, and over the floor the wretched hand,

[7907] self-rolling in its leaps, bounded in its frenzy,

[7958] reddened with blood, and its curved fingers upon the ground

[8019] it locked with deft middle and hooked-nail grip,

[8069] as though clasping again the strap of the oxhide shield.

[8127] And it spoke a word, shedding the tears of Ares:

[8177] "Yet another hand I long for, that I may accomplish

[8230] with three palms deeds worthy of Tritogeneia;

[8277] even without hands I will pursue the hostile man;

[8328] this is the remnant of my manhood still left, that one may tell

[8393] the dexterous boast of the Athenians, that they too,

[8447] excelling even with their feet, fight on when their hands are cut away."

[8521] So speaking, he ran upon the champions like the winds,

[8577] making ready, weaponless, a battle for the destroyer.

[8632] And they, beholding him, marvelled each at the other,

[8687] and the fighters ringed the half-finished champion about

[8745] in throngs; but he alone received with unsparing blade

[8801] the alternating stroke of iron answering iron;

[8849] and hardly did he fall to the ground; he was a likeness of Ares,

[8915] guarding for a late-born dweller the memory of a father.

[8973] Not then alone was the company of foot-travellers cut down,

[9034] but among the horsemen too was slaughter; one marched on

[9092] bringing doom to another; driver overtook driver,

[9143] either cleaving with his spear the back of one who fled before,

[9208] or striking on the breast one who came against him near,

[9266] and thrust him from his horse, freshly slain, into the dust.

[9328] And a horse, struck above the flank by an arrow,

[9378] flung headlong to the ground its hurled-off driver,

[9431] just as the swift-flying Pegasus, companion of the wandering breeze,

[9501] borne aloft, shook off Bellerophon;

[9538] another, in his terror, from the slippery back,

[9587] upright, falling through the horse's belly to the ground,

[9646] stuck head-foremost, hanging, and about the earth,

[9698] casting his head, rolled, leaving his feet upon the horse's back.

[9765] And the mighty Cyclopes ringed the fighters about,

[9817] helpers of Zeus; and against the misty host

[9862] Argilipus blazed, lifting a light-bringing brand.

[9913] And he was armed with the earth-born, fire-pointed thunderbolt,

[9978] fighting with torches; and the swarthy Indians trembled,

[10036] astounded at the fire that answered the heavenly bolt;

[10092] and a fiery champion he was; upon the heads of his foes

[10149] the sparks of the earth-born thunderbolt were shot;

[10202] and the Cyclops conquered ash-spears and countless swords,

[10262] shaking hot bolts and the smoking point,

[10304] holding the brand for a bow; and one Indian after another,

[10364] countless, he burned up, the man, with his archer's flame,

[10424] no single Salmoneus, but with a bastard thunderbolt he confounded them;

[10497] not one god-fighter alone did he slay; not for one alone

[10555] did Evadne lament, as her Capaneus withered away.

[10606] And Steropes had armed himself, whirling a mimic flash,

[10663] bringing against the lightnings of heaven an answering gleam,

[10726] holding a quenchable glitter, which the Western flame begot,

[10788] seed of Sicilian fire and of the smouldering hearth;

[10842] and he had a covering like a cloud, and in its inmost depths

[10904] hid and again revealed his flash with a double quiver,

[10960] bearing the likeness of the heavenly light; for the lightning,

[11024] coming, holds its gleam fleeting yet recovered again.

[11079] And Brontes fought, striking out a ringing strain,

[11131] pouring an answering echo with thunderous crashes;

[11183] and with the strange drops of an earth-born snowfall,

[11238] shedding a made, short-lived, fair-weather water

[11288] in mimic streams, he became a bastard Zeus without clouds.

[11348] And, leaving the cunning crash of his counterfeit thunder,

[11408] he armed himself with Sicilian iron for the slaughter of the foe,

[11475] and, brandishing his hammer aloft above his shoulders,

[11531] he battered the heads of his enemies thick and fast with the iron;

[11599] he struck the dark ranks turn and turn about, as ever

[11654] he beats the forge-wrought anvil with Aetna's clangour.

[11711] And, breaking off the crag of a long-based watch-rock,

[11767] with a rocky spear he charged down upon Deriades;

[11818] and hurling a vast stone with unsparing hand

[11864] against the black-skinned king as he armed for battle,

[11920] he struck his shaggy breast with the ravine-point;

[11972] and he, drunk with so great a millstone of a rock,

[12024] was weighed down over all his breast; and Hydaspes warded off

[12087] the slaughter of his stricken son. But the bold one, faint from his wound,

[12163] shook from his tireless hands his rushing spear,

[12213] the bronze, twenty cubits long, and flung his shield to the ground

[12281] with shame-filled palms; and drawing a feeble breath,

[12336] struck on the rim of the breast by the marble point,

[12390] he fell head-foremost from his lofty car

[12432] like a vast, towering fir—and the fir, falling,

[12481] runs about the boundless bosom of the wide plough-land—.

[12539] And the Indians, pouring round him, lifted him into the chariot,

[12605] fearing the misshapen Cyclops, lest with some cast

[12657] he take up yet another high-stretched crag of the hill

[12713] and slay the king with the rugged bolt,

[12754] having a length equal to that of high-crested Polyphemus.

[12813] And in the middle of the grim champion's brow blazed

[12867] the round flash of his single-eyed face;

[12909] and, cowering before the grim Cyclops' glance,

[12957] the dark-skinned Indians were shaken with astonished fear,

[13017] thinking from heaven that the Olympian Moon,

[13063] rising against the face of the earth-born Cyclops,

[13115] flashed at the full, shielding Lyaeus.

[13155] And father Zeus, beholding the Cyclops' mimic battle-rage,

[13215] high in the clouds laughed, because, receiving from the earthly clouds

[13287] a strange rain through an untried bosom,

[13329] the earth was snowed upon then, yet the thirsty air,

[13383] bearing its naked back unwetted, held no streaming dew.

[13440] And Hypsachis armed himself; marching beside his brother,

[13499] shaking with a towering hand a shield as great as a cliff,

[13559] high in the clouds Elatreus held a vast fir-tree,

[13610] cutting the heads of foes with his tree of a spear.

[13663] Euryalus armed himself; and, cleaving in the tumult

[13716] the great host that fled the plain as far as the sea,

[13771] penning the ranks of the Indians into the fishy gulf,

[13826] he conquered the foe across the spear-bearing deep,

[13879] whirling his sword, twenty cubits long and upright, through the water;

[13951] and with his long ox-cleaver, cutting a sea-neighbouring rock,

[14015] he flung it at the foe; and many of the unburied

[14065] learned the nets of a twofold, sea-drenched Fate,

[14116] by the surging War and by the jagged bolt.

[14160] With them another kinsman, excelling, Halimedes,

[14210] a monstrous Cyclops, raged in Bacchic frenzy with his towering limbs,

[14281] and put the foe to flight; and guarding his face

[14331] he held before it the bossed back of his round oxhide shield.

[14394] And, marking him, Phlogius, avenger of the slain Indians,

[14453] bent his bow, and, drawing a winged shaft, was minded

[14508] to strike him full in the middle with the arrow;

[14558] but, learning of the onset aimed straight at him,

[14609] the high-headed Cyclops, swerving sidelong, avoided

[14662] the cast of the rushing arrow; and he, shaking a crag,

[14718] flung the rugged bolt at Phlogius; but he, fleeing,

[14771] stood beside the car of ox-horned Deriades,

[14816] and hardly avoided the air-roaming marble point,

[14866] staying there; and, wroth at Phlogius' escape,

[14914] Halimedes, splitting open his deadly throat,

[14960] slew twelve men with one bellow of his voice,

[15007] pouring forth the man-destroying roar of his raving cry.

[15065] And the Cyclopes' war-shout crashed upon Olympus

[15115] with terrible tongues. And the dancers of War,

[15163] the Dictaean Corybantes, advanced to the tumult.

[15213] Damneus made war, pursuing the hostile tribes . . .

[15266] and on the plain was the war-cry; and to the troubled Bacchants

[15331] Prymneus came, calm, like a stern-wind breeze

[15378] that saves a sailor riding the road with the storms;

[15432] and much-prayed-for he came to the host, as comes

[15483] to the storm-tossed ships the calm Polydeuces,

[15531] lulling the heavy wave of the storm-breeding sea.

[15582] And with lighter feet Ocythous startled the fighters;

[15637] many he slew with a swift doom,

[15670] one mastering with his spear in close fight, one with a bolt

[15732] from afar, another cutting down with his grim blade;

[15786] another still running before, fled like the winds,

[15838] the frenzied one overtook, plying his wind-swift knees,

[15895] in his running altogether like Iphiclus, who, hastening

[15952] the soles of his feet, grazed the surface of the untrodden calm,

[16018] and held his airborne course above the ears of corn,

[16072] treading the topmost unbending path of the asphodel with his feet.

[16140] Such a storm-foot was Ocythous. And in the tumult

[16191] Mimas set up the whirling-foot of rhythmic War,

[16240] and startled the host, whirling his dance-beating sword,

[16298] holding the mirthless armed leap with knowing foot,

[16351] as when, rousing a din in the ears of Cronus,

[16398] Pyrrhichus, beating his Idaean shield with the swords,

[16454] raised the battle-cheering strain of lying Echo,

[16504] stealing away the hidden, again-grown youth of Zeus;

[16558] with such a mimic armed leap of the dance

[16601] bronze-clad Mimas whirled his airy lance;

[16644] and, cutting the heads of foes, the iron harvests of battle,

[16706] with India-slaying axes and the double-striking blade,

[16762] he held up the first-fruits of the enemy, with Bacchus for witness,

[16831] and, in place of the ox-sacrifice and the wonted wine,

[16887] pouring a libation of blood upon Dionysus.

[16931] And, wheeling on his restless foot with knowing tread,

[16987] helmet-flashing Acmon went, the comrade of Ocythous;

[17041] he fought unshaken, like a forge-wrought anvil,

[17090] lifting his Corybantic shield, in the midst of which

[17144] often the infant Zeus slept his sleep upon the mountains;

[17203] and the house of Zeus was a little cave, where that

[17256] holy goat nursed him with her false, milky teat,

[17306] welling up the strange wise milk, while the oxhide,

[17359] with theft-hiding crashes, made the shield-wielding Echo roar,

[17423] struck in the midst of its back by the tumbling iron.

[17478] For which cause Rhea, fashioning a false stone,

[17527] set it as the antidote for the son of Cronus on Cronus' table.

[17591] And bright-eyed Idaeus entered the revel of War,

[17641] a dancer of battle, whirling his nimble step,

[17688] irresistible, driven by the goad of India-slaying strife.

[17747] And Melisseus startled the whole dark rank,

[17792] with unshaken courage; and, keeping faith with his name,

[17850] he mimicked the dread stings of an armed bee;

[17897] and aiming his marble at the javelin-man of the dappled Curete,

[17962] at his match Melisseus, Morrheus missed,

[18004] he missed; for it is not fitting to destroy the Corybantes with a millstone.

[18082] And, coming together into one, the dancers of joyless Ares

[18142] held a common yoke of War; and about the car

[18188] they wreathed Deriades round with oxhide shields, garland-wise,

[18253] clashing their armour, and in the rhythmic tumult

[18304] they ringed a tower about him with shield-bearing dances.

[18363] And the air-roaming echo ran up to the halls of Zeus,

[18418] and the fair-footed Hours feared the din of both.
