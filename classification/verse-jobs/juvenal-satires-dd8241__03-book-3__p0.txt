[0] 7. Satire VII All the hope and reason of learning rests on Caesar alone;

[74] for he alone in this age has looked with favor on the sad Muses,

[140] when poets now famous and well known

[178] were trying to take a little bath-house at Gabii, or ovens at Rome,

[247] and others thought it neither foul nor shameful

[296] to become auctioneers, when hungry Clio,

[338] her Aganippean valleys deserted, migrated into the salerooms.

[401] For if not one farthing is shown to you in the Pierian

[457] shade, you might come to love the name and livelihood of Machaera

[524] and sell instead, as the auctioneer sells to the bystanders

[585] what is put in his charge—a wine-jar, tripods, cabinets, chests,

[651] the Alcithoe of Pacuvius, the Thebes and Tereus of Faustus.

[712] This is better than to say before a judge, "I saw"

[764] what you did not see—let the Asian knights do that,

[817] and the Cappadocians, whom the other Gaul brands on their bare ankle.

[888] Yet no one henceforth will be forced to bear toil

[939] unworthy of his studies, whoever weaves vocal

[986] eloquence into tuneful measures, and has bitten the laurel.

[1047] Set to it, young men: the indulgence of our leader watches over you,

[1117] and spurs you on, and seeks out matter for itself.

[1169] If you think any other support for your affairs

[1218] is to be looked for elsewhere, and so the parchment of the saffron

[1286] tablet is filled up—be quick, ask for some firewood, and the verses

[1355] you compose, Telesinus, give to Venus' husband,

[1404] or shut them away and let the bookworm bore through the shelved volumes;

[1478] break your pen, poor wretch, and blot out the battles you sat up to write,

[1554] you who make sublime poems in a cramped little cell,

[1608] that you may come out worthy of the ivy and a lean bust.

[1666] There is no hope beyond. The rich miser has now learned

[1723] only to admire, only to praise the eloquent,

[1769] as boys do Juno's bird. But the time of life slips away

[1826] that can endure the sea and the helmet and the mattock.

[1883] Then weariness creeps over the spirit, then eloquent

[1937] and naked old age hates itself and its own Terpsichore.

[1994] Now hear the tricks, so that the man may give you nothing—

[2054] the man you cultivate, having quit the temple of the Muses and Apollo.

[2126] He himself writes verses, and yields only to Homer

[2178] on account of his thousand years; and if, fired by the sweetness

[2244] of fame, you give a reading, he lends you a dingy hall;

[2301] this house, far off and iron-barred, is set to serve you,

[2360] a house whose door mimics anxious city-gates.

[2407] He knows how to post freedmen seated in the farthest part

[2466] of the row, and to arrange the loud voices of his clients:

[2526] none of these great men will give what the benches cost,

[2584] and the tiers that hang from a hired beam,

[2628] and the orchestra set out with chairs to be carried back.

[2687] Yet we keep at this, and draw furrows in the thin

[2738] dust, and turn the shore with a barren plow.

[2784] For if you try to break away, the noose of the ambitious

[2842] itch for writing holds you, and grows old in your sick heart.

[2905] But the poet of distinction, who has no common vein,

[2959] who is not wont to spin out anything trite, nor

[3008] to strike a vulgar song from common coin—

[3051] such a one, whom I cannot point out and only feel,

[3103] is made by a mind free from anxiety, impatient of all

[3158] bitterness, eager for the woods, and fit to drink

[3209] the springs of the Aonides. For poverty cannot sing

[3262] in the Pierian grotto or touch the thyrsus, when it is

[3318] cast down and bare of the coin which night and day

[3370] the body needs: Horace is well fed when he cries "Euhoe!"

[3429] What room is there for genius, unless when your hearts,

[3486] vexed by song alone and swept away by the lords of Cirrha and Nysa,

[3555] admit no two cares at once?

[3584] It is the work of a great mind, not one struck dumb by the thought

[3652] of buying a blanket, to gaze on chariots and horses and the faces

[3719] of gods, and on the sort of Fury that confounds the Rutulian.

[3782] For if Virgil had lacked a slave-boy and a tolerable

[3836] lodging, all the snakes would have fallen from the Fury's hair,

[3901] the muffled trumpet would have groaned nothing deep: do we ask that

[3970] Rubrenus Lappa be no less than the ancient buskin,

[4022] he who pawns his bowls and his cloak to write his Atreus?

[4081] Unhappy Numitor has nothing to send a friend:

[4128] he has something to give Quintilla, nor did he lack

[4181] the means to buy a lion to be fattened on much flesh,

[4236] already tamed; no doubt the beast costs less to keep—

[4291] a poet's guts, of course, take more.

[4329] Let Lucan lie content with fame in his marble

[4376] gardens; but for Serranus and slender Saleius,

[4424] what is glory, however great, if it is glory only?

[4476] Men run to the sweet voice and song of the beloved

[4528] Thebaid, when Statius has gladdened the city

[4574] and named the day: with such sweetness he affects

[4625] the captive souls, and with such delight he is heard

[4679] by the crowd; but when he has shattered the benches with his verse,

[4748] he goes hungry, unless he sells his virgin Agave to Paris.

[4808] That man bestows on many the honor of military rank,

[4862] and rings poets' fingers with the gold of a six-month tribuneship:

[4930] what the nobles do not give, an actor will. Do you court

[4988] the Camerini and a Barea, the great halls of the nobility?

[5048] Pelopea makes prefects, Philomela tribunes.

[5093] Yet you need not envy the poet whom the stage feeds:

[5147] who will be your Maecenas now, who Proculeius

[5194] or Fabius? Who another Cotta, who a second Lentulus?

[5248] Then the reward matched the talent, then it profited many

[5307] to grow pale and to drink no wine the whole of December.

[5365] Moreover, is your labor more fruitful, you writers

[5417] of history? More time perishes here, and more oil.

[5469] For the thousandth page, knowing no limit, rises

[5519] for each of you, and the costly heap of papyrus grows;

[5575] the vast number of facts demands it, and the law of the work.

[5638] Yet what harvest comes from it? What fruit of the broken ground?

[5704] Who will give a historian as much as he would a clerk reading the gazette?

[5780] "But they are a lazy breed, fond of the couch and the shade."

[5843] Tell me, then, what civic duties profit the pleaders,

[5898] and the briefs that go along in a great bundle.

[5947] They sound off grandly themselves, but chiefly when a creditor

[6011] is listening, or if someone keener than he has touched their side—

[6079] a man who comes about a doubtful debt with a great ledger.

[6139] Then the empty bellows breathe out vast lies,

[6186] and the front of the toga is spat upon: if you care to grasp

[6248] the real harvest, set on this side the estates of a hundred pleaders,

[6319] on the other put alone the wealth of Lacerta, the Red charioteer.

[6386] The chiefs have taken their seats; you rise, a pale Ajax,

[6445] to plead for doubtful liberty before a cowherd

[6493] for judge. Burst your strained liver, poor wretch, so that, worn out,

[6564] the green palms, the glory of your staircase, may be set up for you.

[6634] And what is the price of your voice? A dry little ham, and a jar

[6700] of sprats, or some old onions, the monthly ration of the Moors,

[6765] or wine carried down the Tiber—five flagons.

[6811] If you have pleaded four times, if one gold piece has come your way,

[6881] from that the agents' shares fall due by agreement.

[6934] To Aemilius will be given as much as the law allows, though we

[6998] pleaded better; for his bronze chariot stands, with tall

[7056] four-horse teams, in his hall, and he himself, seated

[7111] on a fierce war-horse, threatens with a curved spear from afar,

[7176] and rehearses battles with his one-eyed statue.

[7225] So Pedo goes bankrupt, Matho fails; this is the end

[7278] of Tongilius, who is wont to bathe with a great rhinoceros-horn flask

[7349] and to plague the baths with his muddy throng,

[7397] and presses the young Maedians through the forum with his long pole-borne litter,

[7480] about to buy boys, silver, murrhine, villas;

[7526] for his ship-borne purple, with its Tyrian thread, stands surety.

[7593] And yet this pays them: purple sells

[7631] the pleader, the amethyst-dyed cloaks sell him; it suits him

[7693] to live with the noise and the show of a greater fortune.

[7752] But spendthrift Rome keeps no limit to expense.

[7801] Do we trust to eloquence? No one would now give Cicero

[7857] two hundred coins, unless a great ring flashed.

[7906] The man who goes to law looks first at this: whether you have

[7969] eight slaves, ten companions, a sedan behind you, men in togas

[8033] before your feet. And so Paulus pleaded in a hired

[8085] sardonyx, and so charged more than Gallus,

[8129] than Basilus. Eloquence is rare in a threadbare cloak.

[8185] When is Basilus allowed to bring forward a weeping mother?

[8245] Who could bear Basilus, though he speak well? Let Gaul take you,

[8311] or rather Africa, the nursemaid of pleaders,

[8357] if you have resolved to set a price on your tongue.

[8410] Do you teach declamation? O iron heart of Vettius,

[8462] when his numerous class destroys cruel tyrants!

[8511] For whatever he has just read sitting down, the same he will deliver

[8581] standing, and will chant the same in the same verses;

[8636] the cabbage served up again kills the wretched masters.

[8693] What the color, what the kind of case, where the chief

[8749] question lies, what arrows may come from the opposing side—

[8810] all wish to know, no one to pay the fee.

[8852] "The fee, you say?" "Why, what do I know?" The teacher's fault,

[8917] of course, is blamed, that in the left side of his breast

[8976] nothing throbs for the Arcadian youth, who every sixth

[9032] day fills my wretched head with his dreadful Hannibal,

[9088] whatever it is he is debating: whether to march on Rome

[9145] from Cannae, or, after the storms and lightning, cautiously

[9206] wheel back his cohorts, soaked by the tempest.

[9254] Bargain for any sum you please, and take it down at once: what would I give

[9331] to have the father hear him as often as I do? These six others,

[9396] or more, all clamor with one mouth, the sophists,

[9447] and plead real cases, the abductor abandoned;

[9494] the poisonings fall silent, the wicked and ungrateful husband,

[9558] and the mortars that now cure the old and blind.

[9608] So he will give himself his own discharge, if he heeds

[9664] our counsel, and will enter on a different path of life,

[9722] the man who has come down from the rhetorician's shade to the fight,

[9792] lest the little sum perish for which a cheap ticket of grain

[9854] is bought; for this is the choicest of wages. Try

[9905] how much Chrysogonus, how much Pollio charges to teach

[9961] the children of the rich: you will tear up Theodorus' treatise.

[10026] Baths cost six hundred thousand, and more the colonnade in which

[10092] the master is carried whenever it rains—is he to wait for fair weather,

[10165] or splatter his beasts with fresh mud?

[10205] Here rather, for here the hoof of the clean mule shines.

[10263] In another part let a dining-hall, propped on tall

[10315] Numidian columns, rise and catch the cold sun.

[10363] Whatever the house, there will come one to set out the courses

[10427] skillfully, one to season the relishes.

[10468] Among such expenses, two thousand sesterces for Quintilian

[10528] would be plenty; nothing will cost a father less

[10578] than his son. "Whence then has Quintilian

[10621] so many estates?" Pass over the examples of new

[10670] fortunes: lucky, handsome, and keen,

[10708] lucky, wise, noble, and well-born,

[10744] the lucky man stitches the crescent to his black shoe;

[10800] lucky too the greatest orator and javelin-thrower,

[10852] and, even if he has caught a chill, he sings well. For it makes a difference

[10930] what stars receive you, just as you begin to utter your first

[10993] cries, still red from your mother.

[11029] If Fortune wills, you will become a consul out of a rhetor;

[11090] if this same Fortune wills, a rhetor out of a consul.

[11145] For what was Ventidius? What Tullius? Anything but

[11197] a star and the marvelous power of hidden fate?

[11245] Fate gives kingdoms to slaves, a triumph to captives.

[11300] Yet that lucky man is rarer even than a white crow.

[11353] Many have repented of the empty and barren chair,

[11404] as the end of Thrasymachus proves, and of Secundus

[11456] Carrinas; and you, Athens, saw him poor,

[11498] having dared to bestow on him nothing but cold hemlock.

[11555] May the gods grant our forefathers' shades light, weightless earth

[11623] and breathing crocuses and an everlasting spring in the urn,

[11685] who decreed that a teacher should hold the place

[11735] of a revered parent. Achilles, now grown great, in dread of the rod

[11804] would sing in his native hills, and even then the tail

[11860] of his lyre-master could draw a laugh from no one;

[11912] but his own pupils flog Rufus and the rest, each man his own—

[11975] Rufus, whom he so often called the Allobrogian Cicero.

[12031] Who pays into the lap of Celadus or learned Palaemon

[12085] as much as the grammarian's toil has earned? And yet out of this,

[12152] whatever it is—and it is less than the rhetor's fee—

[12206] the boy's escort, the boorish guardian, takes a first bite,

[12267] and the steward breaks off something for himself. Give way, Palaemon,

[12338] and bear with some shrinkage from it, just as

[12385] the peddler of a winter mat and snowy blanket does,

[12438] provided only that nothing be lost of what, from the midnight hour,

[12507] you sat for, when no workman, when no one would sit

[12560] who teaches to draw out wool with the slanting iron;

[12614] provided only that nothing be lost of having smelled as many lamps

[12682] as there stood boys, while Flaccus grew quite discolored

[12740] and the soot clung to a blackened Maro.

[12781] Yet the fee is rare that does not need the tribunal's

[12836] cognizance. But you, impose your savage laws,

[12883] that the rule of words hold good for the teacher,

[12934] that he read the histories, know all the authors

[12984] as well as his own nails and fingers, so that, asked by chance

[13048] while going to the warm baths or the baths of Phoebus, he may tell

[13116] the nurse of Anchises, the name and country of the stepmother

[13179] of Anchemolus, may say how many years Acestes lived,

[13233] how many jars of Sicilian wine he gave the Phrygians;

[13288] require that he mold tender characters as with the thumb,

[13347] as one shapes a face in wax; require that he be

[13396] even the father of the company himself, lest they play foul games,

[13464] lest they do shameful things by turns; it is no light task

[13524] to watch the hands and the trembling eyes of so many boys.

[13584] "This," he says, "is your charge; but when the year has come round,

[13653] receive the gold the people demand for a victor."

[13704] 8. Satire VIII What do pedigrees avail? What use is it, Ponticus, to be reckoned

[13786] by long descent, to show the painted faces

[13830] of ancestors, the Aemiliani standing in their chariots,

[13887] and the Curii now but halves, a Corvinus reduced to a shoulder,

[13952] and a Galba lacking ears and nose?

[13988] What profit to flaunt on the spreading family-tree

[14040] a Corvinus, and thereafter to touch with many a branch

[14096] the smoke-stained masters of the horse along with a dictator,

[14159] if you live basely before the Lepidi? To what end so many

[14218] images of warriors, if the dice are played all night

[14272] before the Numantini, if you begin to sleep at the rising

[14331] of the morning star, when the generals were moving their standards and camp?

[14409] Why should a Fabius, born in the household of Hercules, rejoice

[14474] in the title "Allobrogicus" and the Great Altar, if he is greedy, if

[14544] vain, and softer than any Euganean lamb,

[14586] if, his tender loin smoothed with Catanian pumice,

[14638] he disgraces his rough ancestors, and—a buyer of poison—

[14696] dishonors his wretched line with an image that must be broken?

[14760] Though ancient waxen busts adorn your halls on every side,

[14820] nobility is the one and only virtue.

[14858] Be a Paulus or a Cossus or a Drusus in your morals;

[14911] set these before the images of your ancestors,

[14959] let them go before the very rods when you are consul.

[15014] You owe me first the goods of the soul. Do you earn to be held holy

[15083] and steadfast in justice in deed and word?

[15127] I recognize a noble: hail, Gaetulicus, or you,

[15175] Silanus, of whatever other blood, a rare

[15217] and excellent citizen, you fall to your rejoicing country—

[15277] one is moved to cry out what the people cry when Osiris

[15334] is found. For who would call him noble who is

[15381] unworthy of his line and distinguished only

[15426] by an illustrious name? We call some man's dwarf an Atlas,

[15486] an Ethiopian a Swan, a crooked, deformed girl

[15533] a Europa; lazy curs, smooth with old mange,

[15578] and licking the rim of a dry lamp,

[15614] will be named Panther, Tiger, Lion, or whatever else there is

[15677] that roars more fiercely on the earth; so you will beware

[15736] and fear lest you be a "Creticus" or "Camerinus" in that fashion.

[15803] Whom have I warned by this? With you is my discourse, Rubellius

[15868] Blandus. You swell with the high pedigree of the Drusi, as though

[15935] you had yourself done something to make you noble,

[15987] something to make her who shines with the blood of Iulus conceive you,

[16059] not the woman hired to weave beneath the windy embankment.

[16119] "You are low," you say, "the very dregs of our rabble,

[16175] none of whom could point to his father's birthplace;

[16229] but I am a son of Cecrops." Long life to you, and may you

[16288] bear long the joys of this origin: yet from the lowest commons

[16352] you will find an eloquent Roman; this man is wont to defend the cases

[16423] of an unlettered noble; from the toga-clad plebs will come

[16483] one to untie the knots of the law and the riddles of statutes;

[16547] from here a young man, busy in arms, makes for the Euphrates

[16609] and the eagles that guard the conquered Batavian. But you

[16668] are nothing but a son of Cecrops, most like a headless Herm:

[16730] for there is no other difference between you, except that

[16789] his head is of marble, and your image lives.

[16835] Tell me, offspring of the Teucri: who would think dumb

[16891] animals well-bred, unless they are strong? It is for this, surely,

[16959] that we praise the swift horse, for whom many a palm

[17013] burns easily and victory exults in the hoarse circus;

[17068] that one is noble, from whatever pasture he comes, whose

[17126] flight is glorious before the rest and who is first to raise the dust on the course;

[17212] but the offspring of Coryphaeus and Hirpinus are a salable herd,

[17278] if victory has rarely settled on their yoke;

[17324] there is no regard there for ancestors, no favor

[17374] for the shades; they are bidden to change masters for a paltry

[17438] price, and, slow-footed, with worn-down neck, drag the cart,

[17500] grandsons fit to turn the mill.

[17533] So, that we may admire you and not your forebears, give us something of your own,

[17616] something I may carve on your titles besides the honors

[17673] we give and have given to those to whom you owe everything.

[17734] This is enough for the youth whom rumor hands down to us as proud

[17801] and puffed up and full of his kinsman Nero;

[17846] for common sense is generally rare in that

[17890] high fortune. But I would not have you, Ponticus,

[17941] valued by the praise of your forebears in such a way that you yourself do nothing

[18024] for future glory. It is wretched to lean on the fame of others,

[18089] lest, the columns withdrawn, the roof come crashing down.

[18148] The vine-shoot stretched on the ground longs for its widowed elms.

[18216] Be a good soldier, a good guardian, an upright

[18264] arbiter too; if ever you are summoned as witness

[18314] in a doubtful and uncertain case, though Phalaris command you to be

[18383] false and dictate perjury with his bull brought near,

[18438] believe it the greatest sin to prefer life to honor,

[18492] and, for the sake of living, to lose the grounds of living.

[18553] He who deserves death has perished, though he dine on a hundred oysters

[18626] from the Gauran rock and bathe in a whole cauldron of Cosmus' scent.

[18696] When the long-awaited province at last receives you

[18749] as its governor, set a curb and a limit to your anger,

[18805] set one to your greed as well; pity the needy allies:

[18860] you see the bones of their realm, sucked dry of marrow;

[18917] mark what the laws advise, what the senate enjoins,

[18970] what great rewards await the good, with what just thunderbolt

[19033] both Capito and Numitor fell, the senate condemning them,

[19092] pirates of the Cilicians. But what does a sentence avail?

[19151] Look for an auctioneer, Chaerippus, for your rags,

[19203] since Pansa snatches whatever Natta left you;

[19250] and now be silent: it is madness, after losing all, to lose the fare too.

[19325] The groan was not the same in old days, nor was the wound equal,

[19391] when our allies' losses were fresh and they but lately conquered.

[19458] Every house was then full, and a great heap

[19503] of coins stood there, a Spartan cloak, Coan purples,

[19557] and along with the paintings of Parrhasius and the statues of Myron,

[19627] the ivory of Phidias was alive, nor was the much

[19677] labor of Polyclitus lacking anywhere, and rare were the tables without a Mentor.

[19759] From there Dolabella, from there Antonius, from there

[19814] sacrilegious Verres carried back in their tall ships

[19868] hidden spoils, and more triumphs over peace than over war.

[19928] Now from the allies a few yoke of oxen, a small herd of mares,

[19992] and the father of the flock will be snatched from a captured little field,

[20068] then the household gods themselves, if there is any notable statue,

[20137] if any single god in his little shrine; for these indeed are now

[20203] their greatest things, these are now their utmost. You might perhaps

[20273] despise the unwarlike Rhodians and oiled Corinth;

[20324] and despise them with reason: what will the resined youths

[20384] and the smooth legs of a whole nation do to you?

[20434] But shun rough Spain, the Gallic clime,

[20475] and the Illyrian coast; spare too those harvesters

[20527] who fill the city that has leisure for the circus and the stage.

[20593] And what reward will you bring from there for so dire a crime,

[20657] now that Marius has lately stripped the impoverished Africans?

[20721] Above all take care that no great wrong be done

[20770] to the brave and the wretched: though you take away all the gold

[20836] and silver there is anywhere, you will leave them shield and sword.

[20905] What I have just set down is no mere maxim: it is truth—

[20963] believe that I recite to you a leaf of the Sibyl.

[21014] If your band of companions is holy, if no long-haired page

[21074] sells your tribunal, if there is no fault in your wife,

[21131] nor any Harpy ready to go through the assizes and all the towns

[21196] with hooked talons to snatch your money,

[21238] then you may count your descent from Picus, and if lofty

[21296] names delight you, set among your forebears the whole

[21351] Titanian war and Prometheus himself,

[21389] take your great-grandsire from whatever book you please.

[21447] But if ambition and lust drag you headlong,

[21492] if you break your rods in the blood of the allies, if you delight

[21559] in blunted axes and a weary lictor,

[21596] then the very nobility of your own ancestors begins

[21649] to stand against you, and to hold a bright torch before your shameful deeds.

[21727] Every fault of the soul carries the more conspicuous

[21781] guilt the greater the man who sins is held to be.

[21832] To what end do you tell me of your wont to seal false tablets

[21895] in the temples your grandfather built, and before your father's

[21960] triumphal statue? To what end, if a nocturnal adulterer,

[22018] you veil your muffled temples in a Santonic hood?

[22069] Past the ashes and bones of his ancestors, in a flying

[22125] carriage, fat Lateranus is whirled, and he himself,

[22178] the consul-muleteer, locks the wheel with his own brake.

[22236] By night, indeed, but the Moon sees, but the stars as witnesses

[22301] strain their eyes. When his term of office is ended,

[22355] Lateranus will take up his whip in broad daylight,

[22407] and will not blush to meet a friend

[22444] now old, and will salute him first with his wand, and will untie

[22510] the hay-bundles and pour out the barley for his weary beasts.

[22573] Meanwhile, while he slaughters the woolly victims and the russet bullock

[22647] in Numa's fashion, he swears before Jupiter's altars

[22701] by Epona alone and the faces painted on the rank stalls.

[22759] But when he chooses to revisit the all-night taverns,

[22814] a Syrophoenician, greasy with constant balsam, runs to meet him,

[22880] a Syrophoenician dwelling at the Idumaean gate,

[22929] and with a host's affection greets him as lord and king,

[22987] and Cyane too, girt up, with her wine-jar for sale.

[23040] The defender of the fault will say to me: "We did

[23091] these things too, as young men." Granted; you stopped, of course, nor

[23162] beyond that did you nurse the error: let your shameful daring be brief.

[23235] Some faults should be cut off with the first beard;

[23288] grant pardon to boys: but Lateranus goes to those

[23339] bath-house cups and inscribed napkins

[23378] ripe for the war of defending the rivers of Armenia and Syria,

[23442] and the Rhine and the Danube; this age is fit to keep

[23497] Nero secure. Send to Ostia, Caesar,

[23534] send—but seek your legate in a great cookshop;

[23582] you will find him lying with some cutthroat,

[23628] mixed in with sailors and thieves and runaways,

[23677] among hangmen and coffin-makers

[29885] thicket of hair, no gloss at all on your whole skin, such as

[30241] body; you may detect its joys too; the face takes

[30292] both habits from within; so you seem to have turned

[30345] your purpose and to be going against your former life.
