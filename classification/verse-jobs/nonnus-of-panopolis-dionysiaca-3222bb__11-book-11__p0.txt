[0] 11. The eleventh, watch, and you will mark lovely Ampelos borne off by the man-slaying, snatching bull.

[105] The contest was dissolved; and the lovely youth, glorying

[164] in the sport-loving victory, leapt up, companion of his coeval Bacchus,

[237] shifting his wandering sole around the rolling-footed circle,

[300] leaning his all-white right hand on Dionysus;

[347] and seeing him exulting, prancing on his feet with the double victory,

[419] Iobacchus soothed him with a friendly word:

[464] "Hasten again, dear boy, after the victory of swift feet

[522] and after the contest on foot, to have yet a third other contest,

[589] and swim, unreachable, with your coeval Bacchus as he swims.

[651] Ampelos, having beaten me wrestling on the sands,

[702] be lighter than Dionysus also in the streams,

[749] and leaving the Satyrs still at play with their leapings,

[808] hasten again, alone, to a third other contest;

[856] may you win on land and in the waters, and after victory

[914] I will crown your lovely locks with double clusters,

[968] Lyaeus twice defeated, yet unvanquished.

[1010] This lovely stream suited you, it suited your beauty

[1064] alone, the beauty of your limbs, that Ampelos might be double,

[1128] cleaving the gold-gleaming waters with golden palm;

[1181] and as you stretch with naked limbs for victory,

[1231] the whole Pactolian water will adorn your beauty.

[1282] Grant the river an Olympian honour equal to your own, since Phaethon too

[1356] casts his rosy rays upon Ocean;

[1389] give Pactolus too your radiance, that Ampelos

[1436] may appear rising like the Morning Star; for this stream

[1494] flashes in both ways, with the reddening metal,

[1543] as you do with your limbs; and let the deep-rich stream

[1600] receive a youth of like colour and like form,

[1647] mingling beauty with beauty, that I may cry to the Satyrs:

[1707] how did rose come to rose? whence is one radiance mingled

[1766] both in the reddening skin and the flashing stream?

[1819] Would that here too, boy, there were the stream of Eridanus,

[1881] where the tears of the Heliades flow rich, that I might wash

[1943] your limbs in both, in gold and in amber.

[1986] But since I dwell very far from the Hesperian river,

[2040] I will come to Alybe, the neighbouring city, where nearby

[2099] the Geudis whitens with the draught of its wealth-bearing waters,

[2166] that you, washed from the river Pactolus,

[2209] Ampelos, I may brighten in the silvery streams too.

[2262] Let fair-flowing Hermus be a care to other Satyrs;

[2314] for it does not bear a stream of gold; but you alone

[2368] were a golden boy, may you have golden water too."

[2420] So speaking, he was borne through the water; and from the ground

[2486] Ampelos was lifted up and went along with Lyaeus;

[2537] and sweet was the course to them both, end from end,

[2591] as they swam in circles in the rich river.

[2635] And the god, holding a watery contest of swiftness,

[2688] ran unsteady in the water, casting his bare

[2733] breast on the streams, and shaking his feet and rowing his hands

[2799] he scored the unshaken back of the wealthy calm,

[2849] now keeping his course in company with the coeval boy,

[2905] now darting past, but guarded, only so far as he would leave

[2967] Ampelos running near the path, his companion, beside neighbouring Bacchus;

[3043] at other times wheeling his palms, as though tiring in the wave,

[3109] the swift-kneed god willingly gave the victory to the water-traveller.

[3181] And after the river's flood Ampelos went to the midst of the thicket,

[3252] holding his neck proud with the river victory.

[3300] And he girt his locks with a serpent cluster,

[3347] bearing a fearful mimicry of serpent-haired Lyaeus;

[3400] and often, seeing the spangled tunic of Bromius,

[3450] he fastened on his limbs a counterfeit, embroidered raiment,

[3512] and bound his light foot with a purple buskin,

[3560] wearing a dappled robe on his skin; and in a mountain-stalled car

[3627] watching Iobacchus driving leopards

[3664] he proudly displayed the sports of the crag-loving beasts;

[3724] now mounting the mane of a mountain bear

[3766] he reined back the grim mane of the hurrying beast,

[3819] now he lashed the shaggy neck of a lion,

[3861] at other times, mounted high upon spangled backs,

[3912] unshaken, he delighted to drive an unbridled tiger.

[3965] And seeing him, Dionysus, with a gentle threat,

[4014] spoke, consoling him with a friendly, prophetic word,

[4069] pouring a pitying voice from reproachful lips:

[4117] "Where are you borne, dear boy? Why does the wood please you so much?

[4188] Stay with me, hunting with hunting Dionysus;

[4234] when I touch the feast, feast together with Lyaeus,

[4287] reveling, when I rouse the revel for the Satyrs.

[4337] The leopard does not trouble me, nor the jaw of the wild bear;

[4401] do not tremble at the greedy mouth of the mountain-ranging lioness;

[4470] only fear the horns of the implacable bull."

[4516] He spoke, pitying bold Ampelos; and the youth

[4563] heard the word with his ears, but his mind played within him.

[4626] There appeared a great sign to loving Dionysus

[4674] announcing Ampelos short-lived; for from a crag

[4723] bearing a freshly grown fawn upon its back

[4920] headlong, self-tumbling; and as the mountain-ranging fawn

[22817] and the flood was his path; but ever coming near him

[22871] the boy, sparing the rush of his hurrying hand,

[22920] watched the rosy-skinned fingers of their swimming hands;

[22979] and Calamus, leading the way, reined back his rush,

[23032] and yielded to the youth; and he ran on rowing his hands,

[23091] the storm-like boy, stretching his neck above the swell;

[23149] and now Carpus would have mounted from the surge onto the land,

[23214] and after the victory on land would have gained the river victory,

[23282] but Aetes, blowing against him, dashed him down,

[23332] and the implacable one slew the sweet boy; for as the youth's

[23395] throat opened, numberless water rushed in.

[23439] And Calamus, fleeing the storms of the jealous wind,

[23493] sank, away from the youth, onto the neighbouring shores;

[23551] and perceiving his friend not present and not hearing,

[23607] groaning lovingly he bellowed with a mournful voice:

[23661] "Naiads, speak, what Aetes snatched away Carpus?

[23711] Yes, I beseech you, grant me a last favour, come to another

[23772] spring, and flee the death-bearing water of my father,

[23828] nor drink the stream that slew Carpus.

[23868] Surely my begetter did not slay the youth; but in envy

[23924] Aetes, after Phoebus, destroyed Carpus for Calamus too,

[23981] and perhaps, longing for him, struck him with a jealous storm,

[24045] bringing a contrary breeze upon the youth after the discus.

[24106] Not yet has my star risen, bathed in the streams,

[24157] not yet has my Morning Star shone; but with Carpus

[24209] sinking in the streams, why should I still look on the light?

[24272] Naiads, speak, who quenched the light of the Loves?

[24325] Do you still linger, boy? Why does the water please you so much?

[24391] Surely you have not found a better friend in the waters, with whom staying

[24467] you have cast the longings of wretched Calamus to the winds?

[24529] If one love-stricken Naiad-Nymph snatched you,

[24577] tell me, and I will arm against them all; but if the wedding hymn

[24644] of marriage with our sister of the Loves delights you,

[24700] tell me, and even in the streams I will light your bridal chamber.

[24768] Carpus, do you float past me, forgetting the accustomed bank?

[24831] I grew weary calling you, and you do not hear me crying out.

[24893] If Notus or bold Eurus blew, let him wander himself,

[24947] pitiless, danceless, wanton, an enemy of the Loves;

[25000] if Boreas overpowered you, I will go to Oreithyia.

[25052] But if a wave covered you and did not reverence your form,

[25112] and my father took you with the unsparing draught of the wave,

[25176] let him receive his own son too in the man-slaying waters,

[25236] and let him hide Calamus near perished Carpus.

[25284] But falling headlong, where wandering Carpus died,

[25336] I will quench my hot love, drinking the Acherousian water."

[25397] He spoke, gushing forth the stream of his eyes; and about the corpse

[25467] he cut his dark lock with mournful iron,

[25509] which he had grown, which he had tended, and held out the mourning hair

[25582] to his father Maeander, and spoke his last word:

[25632] "Receive, after my locks, my body too; for I cannot

[25685] look upon the light for one dawn apart from Carpus;

[25738] Carpus and Calamus have one life, and both have won

[25791] a like goad of Love upon the earth; and let one watery

[25847] death be for us both, even in the streams.

[25891] Make, Naiads, on the high river-bank

[25929] a common cenotaph for us both, and about the tomb

[25980] let a word be inscribed in mournful letters:

[26026] 'I am the grave of Carpus and Calamus, whom of old, both

[26084] longing for each other, the implacable water slew.'

[26137] And for love-stricken Calamus, though he is a brother,

[26193] dying, tear off one small cluster of his hair,

[26241] but for perished Carpus shear all his locks."

[26288] He spoke, and self-tumbling slipped into the stream,

[26342] drinking the child-slaying water of his refusing father.

[26400] And Calamus gave to reeds the shape named for him,

[26452] of like growth, and Carpus grew the fruit of the field.

[26509] Such things consoling, with a friendly word

[26554] did impetuous Eros soothe, lightening the sweet sting for Dionysus.

[26623] And his heart was lashed all the more with mournful grief

[26682] through the untimely doom of the youth.—And the unsteady

[26740] daughters of Lycabas, the storm-footed father,

[26788] the rosy-faced Hours, went to the house of the Sun;

[26841] of whom one, sending around her snow-shaded face

[26891] a thin gleam of dark-clouded radiance,

[26931] joined her cold feet to a hailstorm sandal,

[26976] and binding her locks upon her wet head

[27017] fastened a rain-bearing veil upon her brow,

[27062] and held a green wreath on her head, and with a snowy

[27117] white girdle covered her frosted breast;

[27159] and another spat out the mortal-delighting breeze

[27210] of the swallow-winds, breathing, and of her Zephyr-loving head

[27274] she girt the springtime hair with a dewy bond,

[27322] laughing flowery, and quivering through her robe

[27372] the long-shadowed fragrance of the morning, opening rose,

[27431] she wove a double revel for Adonis and Cytherea;

[27481] another Hour, together with her sisters, came as the harvest-Hour,

[27549] and the ear of grain, bristling round about with topmost clusters,

[27617] she lifted in her right hand, and the blade of the sharp-cutting sickle,

[27691] the messenger of the harvest, and the girl's body was girt

[27751] with white linen, and as she whirled in the dance

[27802] through her thin raiment the mysteries of her thighs appeared,

[27866] and as her face was loosed, the moist sweats

[27912] were dampened on her cheeks by the hotter Phaethon;

[27965] and another, the leader of the well-ploughed dance,

[28018] bound an olive shoot on her hairless temple

[28063] drenched with the waters of the seven-channeled river Nile,

[28124] and tending the thin, withering hair of her temple

[28176] she had a parched body, since, being autumnal,

[28224] she had shorn her tree-like hair to the leaf-shedding winds;

[28286] for not yet, with the plaited clusters of golden tendrils,

[28346] did vine-clusters flow upon the neck of the nymph,

[28398] nor, made drunk beside the wine-loving wine-press,

[28450] did the moisture of the dew of purple Maronian wine intoxicate her,

[28519] nor did the wandering ivy run up in a whirl;

[28565] but then the appointed time came, for the sake of which the Hours themselves

[28643] ran together in company to the house of the Sun.
