CHAPTER: 01-protrepticus



[109179] 8. It is time now, then — the rest being already gone through in order — to come to the prophetic Scriptures. For the oracles too, holding out to us most plainly the starting-points toward piety, lay the foundation of the truth. And the divine Scriptures and sober ways of life are short roads of salvation; bare of cosmetic art and of outward fine speech and chatter and flattery, they raise up the man choked by wickedness, despising the slipperiness of worldly life, with one and the same voice healing many things, turning us away from harmful deception, and openly exhorting us to manifest salvation. At once, then, let the prophetess Sibyl first sing for us the song of salvation: "Behold, this one is clear to all, free from error: come, pursue not darkness and gloom forever. Behold, the sweet-eyed light of the sun shines surpassingly. Know, laying up wisdom in your breasts: there is one God, who sends rains and winds and earthquakes, lightnings, famines, plagues and grievous sorrows, and snows and the rest. Why should I recount them one by one? He rules heaven, he holds sway over earth, himself from the beginning." Very inspiredly, likening deception to darkness and the knowledge of God to the sun and light, and setting both side by side in comparison, she teaches the choice. For falsehood is not dispelled by the bare setting beside it of the truth, but, being driven out by the use of the truth, it is put to flight. Jeremiah the all-wise prophet, or rather the Holy Spirit in Jeremiah, shows God: "I am a God at hand," he says, "and not a God afar off. If a man does anything in secret, shall I not see him? Do I not fill the heavens and the earth? says the Lord." And again, through Isaiah: "Who has measured," he says, "the heaven with a span and all the earth with the hollow of his hand?" Behold the greatness of God, and be amazed. Him let us worship, of whom the prophet says: "At your presence the mountains shall melt, as wax melts at the presence of fire." This, he says, is the God whose throne is heaven and footstool the earth; who, if he should open the heaven, trembling shall seize you. Do you wish to hear too what this prophet says about the idols? "They shall be made a public example before the sun, and their carcasses shall be food for the birds of heaven and the beasts of the earth, and they shall rot under the sun and the moon, which they themselves loved and to which they were enslaved, and their city shall be burned." And he says that the elements too and the world shall be destroyed together with them: "The earth," he says, "shall grow old, and the heaven shall pass away, but the word of the Lord abides forever." And what when again God wishes to show himself through Moses? "See, see that I am, and there is no other God beside me. I will slay and I will make alive; I will smite and I will heal, and there is none who can deliver out of my hands." But do you wish to hear too another oracle-singer? You have the whole prophetic choir, the fellow-revellers of Moses. What does the Holy Spirit say to them through Hosea? I will not shrink to tell it: "Behold, I am he that establishes thunder and creates wind, whose hands have founded the host of heaven." And again, through Isaiah — and this saying too I will recall to you — "I am, I am," he says, "the Lord, who speaks righteousness and announces truth. Gather yourselves and come; take counsel together, you that are saved from the nations. They have no knowledge, those who lift up the carved wood of theirs, and pray to gods who cannot save them." Then, going on: "I," he says, "am God, and there is none righteous beside me, and there is no saviour beside me. Turn to me and be saved, you from the ends of the earth. I am God, and there is none other; by myself I swear." And against the idolaters he is indignant, saying: "To whom have you likened the Lord? Or to what likeness have you likened him? Has a carpenter made an image, or has a goldsmith, having melted gold, gilded it over?" — and what follows. Are you, then, still idolaters? But even now beware the threats; for the carved images and the works of hands shall howl — or rather those who trust in them, for the matter is insensible. Again he says: "The Lord shall shake the inhabited cities, and shall lay hold of the whole world in his hand like a nest." Why should I declare to you the mysteries of wisdom, and sayings from a Hebrew child made wise? "The Lord created me as the beginning of his ways for his works"; and the Lord gives wisdom, and from his presence come knowledge and understanding. "How long, O sluggard, will you lie down? When will you be raised from sleep? But if you are unsluggish, your harvest shall come to you like a fountain" — the fatherly Word, the good lamp, the Lord who brings the light, faith and salvation to all. "For the Lord who made the earth in his strength," as Jeremiah says, "set up the world in his wisdom." For us, when we had fallen away to the idols, wisdom, which is his Word, sets us upright again toward the truth. And this is the first resurrection from our falling. Whence, turning men away from all idolatry, the inspired Moses cried out most nobly: "Hear, O Israel; the Lord your God, the Lord is one, and you shall worship the Lord your God and serve him alone." Now then, understand, O men, according to that blessed psalmist David: "Lay hold of instruction, lest at any time the Lord be angry, and you perish from the righteous way, when his wrath is quickly kindled. Blessed are all they that trust in him." And already, taking exceeding pity on us, the Lord gives the saving strain, like a marching rhythm: "Sons of men, how long will you be heavy of heart? Why do you love vanity and seek after a lie?" What, then, is the vanity and what the lie? The holy apostle of the Lord, charging the Greeks, will explain to you: "Because, knowing God, they glorified him not as God, neither were thankful, but became vain in their reasonings, and changed the glory of God into the likeness of an image of corruptible man, and served the creature rather than the Creator." And surely this God it is "who in the beginning made the heaven and the earth"; but you do not understand God, and you worship heaven; and how are you not impious? Hear again the prophet saying: "The sun shall fail and the heaven shall be darkened; but the Almighty shall shine forever, and the powers of the heavens shall be shaken, and the heavens shall be rolled together like a scroll, stretched out and contracted" — for these are the prophetic voices — "and the earth shall flee from the face of the Lord."

[115793] 9. And I could bring to you ten thousand Scriptures, of which "not one tittle shall pass away" without being fulfilled; for the mouth of the Lord, the Holy Spirit, has spoken these things. "No longer, then," he says, "my son, despise the instruction of the Lord, nor faint when you are rebuked by him." O the exceeding love toward man! Not as a teacher to disciples, nor as a master to servants, nor as God to men, but "as a gentle father he admonishes his sons." Then Moses confesses that he is afraid and trembling, hearing about the Word; but you, hearing the divine Word, do you not fear? Are you not in anguish? Are you not at once cautious and eager to learn thoroughly — that is, eager for salvation — fearing wrath, loving grace, emulating hope, that you may escape the judgement? Come, come, O my young company; for unless you become again as little children and are born anew, as the Scripture says, you shall not receive the Father who truly is, nor shall you ever enter into the kingdom of the heavens. For how is the stranger permitted to enter? But when, I think, he is enrolled and made a citizen and receives the Father, then he shall be among the things of the Father, then he shall be deemed worthy to inherit, then he shall share in the Father's kingdom with the genuine, the beloved Son. For this is the Church of the firstborn, composed of many good children; these are the firstborn enrolled in heaven, who keep festival with so many myriads of angels. And we are the firstborn children, the nurslings of God, the genuine friends of the Firstborn, the first of all other men to have understood God, the first to have been torn away from sins, the first to have been separated from the devil. But now some are godless in proportion as God is loving to man; for he wishes us, who were slaves, to become sons, but they have disdained even to become sons. O the great folly! You are ashamed of the Lord. He promises freedom, but you run away into slavery. He gives salvation as a gift, but you are carried down to death. He bestows eternal life, but you await punishment, and prefer the fire which the Lord has prepared for the devil and his angels. For this cause the blessed apostle says: "I testify in the Lord that you no longer walk as the nations walk, in the vanity of their mind, being darkened in their understanding and alienated from the life of God, through the ignorance that is in them, through the hardness of their heart; who, being past feeling, have given themselves over to wantonness, to work all uncleanness and greed." When such a witness convicts the folly of men and calls upon God, what else remains for the unbelieving but judgement and condemnation? And the Lord does not weary of exhorting, of striking fear, of urging, of rousing, of admonishing; indeed he wakes men from sleep, and raises up out of their darkness those who have gone astray: "Awake," he says, "you that sleep, and arise from the dead, and Christ the Lord shall give you light" — the sun of the resurrection, "begotten before the morning star," who has bestowed life by his own rays. Let no one, then, despise the Word, lest he unawares despise himself. For the Scripture somewhere says: "Today, if you will hear his voice, harden not your hearts, as in the provocation, in the day of temptation in the wilderness, where your fathers tempted me in a testing." And if you wish to learn what the testing is, the Holy Spirit will explain it to you: "And I saw my works," he says, "forty years; wherefore I was grieved with that generation and said, They do always err in heart; but they have not known my ways; so I swore in my wrath, If they shall enter into my rest." See the threat; see the exhortation; see the honour. Why, then, do we still change grace into wrath, and not, with open ears, receive the Word, and lodge God in pure souls? For great is the grace of his promise, "if today we hear his voice"; and that "today" is increased day by day, as long as it is called "today." And until the consummation both the "today" and the learning abide; and then the true "today," the unfailing day of God, is stretched out coextensive with the ages. Let us, then, ever hearken to the voice of the divine Word; for the "today" is an image of the eternal age, and the day is a symbol of light, and the Word is light to men, through whom we are illumined toward God. Reasonably, then, to those who believe and obey, grace shall abound exceedingly; but to those who disobey and wander in heart, and have not known the ways of the Lord, which John commanded to make straight and prepare, with these God is grieved and threatens. And indeed the end of the threat, as the elders among the Hebrews who wandered say enigmatically, the threat — for they are said not to enter into the rest because of unbelief, until, following the successor of Moses, they learned at last by experience that they could not otherwise be saved than by believing as Jesus did. And the Lord, being loving to man, calls all men to the knowledge of the truth, he who sends the Comforter. What, then, is the knowledge? Piety; and "piety is profitable unto all things," according to Paul, "having promise of the life that now is and of that which is to come." At what price would you have confessed, O men, that you would buy eternal salvation, if it were for sale? Not even if one were to measure out the whole Pactolus, the fabled stream of gold, would he count out a worthy price for salvation. Do not, then, grow weary; it is possible for you, if you will, to buy out the precious salvation with your own treasure, with love and faith, which is a price worthy of life. This price God gladly receives. "For we have hoped in the living God, who is the saviour of all men, especially of the faithful." But the rest, grown fast to the world like seaweed on sea-girt rocks, make light of immortality, like the old man of Ithaca, longing not for the truth and the heavenly fatherland, and besides for the light that truly is, but for the smoke.

[121816] Piety, then, making man like to God so far as is possible, inscribes God as a fitting teacher, who alone is able worthily to make man like to God. This teaching the apostle, knowing it to be truly divine, "you, O Timothy," he says, "from a babe have known the sacred writings, which are able to make you wise unto salvation through faith in Christ." For truly sacred are the writings that make holy and make divine; out of which writings and sacred syllables the same apostle accordingly calls the composed Scriptures, the books, "inspired of God," being profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness, that the man of God may be perfect, furnished completely unto every good work. No one would be so astonished at the exhortations of the other saints as at the Lord himself, the lover of man; for he has no other work but this alone, to save man. He cries, at any rate, pressing on to salvation: "The kingdom of the heavens is at hand"; he turns men about as they draw near by fear. In this way too the apostle of the Lord, exhorting the Macedonians, becomes an interpreter of the divine voice: "The Lord is at hand," he says; "take heed lest we be found empty." But you are so fearless — or rather faithless — obeying neither the Lord himself nor Paul, and that though he was bound for Christ's sake. "Taste and see that the Lord is good." Faith will lead you in, experience will teach you, the Scripture will train you, saying: "Come, O children, hearken to me; I will teach you the fear of the Lord." Then, as to those who already believe, it briefly adds: "Who is the man that desires life, that loves to see good days?" It is we, we shall say, the worshippers of the Good, the emulators of good things. Hear, then, you that are far off; hear, you that are near: the Word is hidden from none; it is a common light, it shines upon all men; there is no Cimmerian in the Word. Let us hasten to salvation, to the new birth; let us hasten to be gathered, the many, into one love, according to the union of the single substance. Working good in like manner, let us pursue unity, seeking out the good Monad. And the union out of many, having received divine harmony out of the many-voiced and scattered, becomes one symphony, following one leader of the chorus and teacher, the Word, resting upon the very truth, saying "Abba, Father." This true voice God welcomes, receiving it first from his own children as firstfruits.

[124278] 10. "But," you say, "it is not reasonable to overthrow a custom handed down to us from our fathers." Then why do we not use our first food, milk, to which the nurses no doubt accustomed us from birth? Why do we increase or diminish the paternal estate, and not keep it equal, as we received it? Why do we no longer dribble into our fathers' bosoms, or do the other things which, when we were infants and were reared by our mothers, made us a laughing-stock? Why did we correct ourselves, even if we did not happen upon good tutors? Then, on voyages, deviations, though harmful and dangerous, yet somehow fall out pleasantly; but in life shall we not forsake the custom that is wicked and passion-ridden and godless, even though our fathers be angry, and turn aside toward the truth and seek after the Father who truly is, thrusting away custom like a deadly drug? For this very thing is the fairest of the tasks before us, to show you that it was from madness and from this thrice-wretched custom that piety came to be hated. For so great a good — than which nothing greater has yet been given by God to the begetting of men — would never have been hated or forbidden, unless you, swept along by custom, then, stopping your ears against us, like stiff-necked horses balking, biting on the bit, fled from our words, longing to shake us off, your charioteers of life, and, carried by your folly toward the cliffs of destruction, supposed the holy Word of God to be accursed. There follows for you, then, according to Sophocles, the wage of your choice: "mind gone, ears useless, empty thoughts." And you do not know that this, above all, is true: that the good and pious shall obtain a good requital, having honoured the Good, while the wicked, on the contrary, shall obtain the fitting punishment; and upon the ruler of wickedness chastisement is hung. The prophet Zechariah threatens him: "The Lord who chose Jerusalem rebuke you. Is not this a brand plucked out of the fire?" What craving, then, still lies in men for a voluntary death? Why have they fled to this death-bringing brand, with which they shall be burned up, when it is possible to live well according to God, not according to custom? For God bestows life, but a wicked custom, after the departure from this world, brings empty repentance together with punishment. "And the foolish learns by suffering" that superstition destroys and piety saves. Let one of you look at those who serve the idols, with filthy hair, outraged in dingy and torn raiment, wholly untried in baths, savage with the points of their nails; many, too, deprived of their privy parts, showing in fact that the precincts of the idols are tombs or prison-houses. These seem to me to be mourning, not worshipping, the gods, having suffered things worthy of pity rather than of piety. And, seeing these things, are you still blind, and will you not look up to the Lord of all and Master of the universe? Will you not flee, escaping from these prison-houses here, to the mercy from heaven? For God, out of his great love toward man, holds fast to man, as the mother-bird flies down after the nestling that falls out of the nest; and if some creeping beast should gape at the nestling, "the mother flutters round, bewailing her dear young." God the Father both seeks his creature and heals the fall and drives away the beast and takes up the nestling again, urging it to fly back to the nest. Then, dogs that have lost their way, hunting by scent, have tracked out their master; and horses, having thrown their rider, have obeyed their master at a single whistle. "The ox knows," it says, "his owner, and the ass his master's crib; but Israel has not known me." What, then, the Lord? He does not bear malice; he still pities, he still demands repentance. And I wish to ask you, whether it does not seem to you absurd that you men, having been made the creature of God and having received your soul from him and being wholly his, should serve another master, and besides should worship instead of the king the tyrant, and instead of the good the wicked. For who, in the name of truth, being of sound mind, forsakes the good and consorts with wickedness? Who is there that, fleeing from God, lives with demons? Who, being able to be a son of God, takes pleasure in being a slave? Or who, being able to be a citizen of heaven, pursues the gloom, when he might till paradise and walk about heaven and partake of the living and pure fountain, treading the air in the track of that shining cloud, like Elijah, beholding the saving rain? But these, wallowing like worms about pools and mire, the streams of pleasure, feed upon profitless and senseless luxuries, swinish men. "For swine," it says, "delight in mire rather than in pure water," and "rave over filth," according to Democritus. Let us not, then, let us not be enslaved nor become swinish, but, as true children of the light, let us look up and gaze toward the light, lest the Lord prove us bastards, as the sun does the eagles. Let us repent, then, and pass over from unlearning to knowledge, from folly to understanding, from incontinence to self-control, from injustice to righteousness, from godlessness to God. Fair is the risk of deserting to God. And there are many other goods, too, for the lovers of righteousness to enjoy, who pursue eternal salvation — yes, and those which God himself hints at, speaking through Isaiah: "There is an inheritance for those who serve the Lord." Fair indeed and lovely is the inheritance — not gold, not silver, not raiment, the things of the earth, where the moth and the robber lurk, with eyes set upon the low-lying wealth — but that treasure of salvation, to which we must hasten, becoming lovers of the Word. From here our good works set out with us, and fly together with us on the wing of truth. This inheritance the eternal covenant of God puts into our hands, supplying the eternal gift; and this our loving Father, the true Father, does not cease to exhort, to admonish, to instruct, to love; for neither does he cease to save, but counsels the best: "Become just," says the Lord; "you that thirst, go to the water; and as many of you as have no money, come and buy and drink without money." To the bath, to salvation, to the illumination he calls, all but crying out and saying: "Earth I give you and sea, O child, and heaven, and all the living creatures in them I bestow upon you. Only, O child, thirst for the Father; God shall be shown to you without price; the truth is not bought and sold. He gives you the winged things and the swimming things and the things upon the earth; these the Father has fashioned for your thankful enjoyment." With money the bastard, who is the child of destruction, shall buy — he who has chosen to serve mammon; but to you he entrusts what is yours, to you the genuine, I say, who love the Father, for whose sake he still works, and to whom alone he promises, saying: "And the land shall not be sold for confirmation"; for it is not confirmed to corruption; "for all the land is mine," and it is yours too, if you receive God. Whence the Scripture reasonably proclaims good tidings to those who have believed: "But the saints of the Lord shall inherit the glory of God and his power." What glory, O blessed one, tell me? "That which eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it entered into the heart of man; and they shall rejoice over the kingdom of their Lord forever, Amen." You have, O men, the divine promise of grace; you have heard, too, the other, the threat of punishment, through which the Lord saves, training man by fear and by grace. Why do we delay? Why do we not turn aside from punishment? Why do we not receive the gift? Why do we not choose the better — God instead of the wicked one — and prefer wisdom to idolatry, and take life in exchange for death? "Behold, I have set before your face," he says, "death and life." The Lord tests you, to choose life; he counsels you as a father to obey God. "For if you hear me," he says, "and are willing, you shall eat the good things of the land" — the grace of obedience; "but if you do not hear me nor are willing, the sword and fire shall devour you" — the judgement of disobedience. For "the mouth of the Lord has spoken these things"; the law of truth is the Word of the Lord. Do you wish me to become a good counsellor to you? Then do you hear, and I will show, if I can. You ought, O men, considering the Good itself, to bring to bear an innate faith, a credible witness from within, from your own home, manifestly choosing the best, and not to labour as if it were something to be tracked down. For if one ought to get drunk, say, then one ought to deliberate; but you get drunk before considering; and if one ought to commit outrage, you make no inquiry, but commit outrage with all speed. Only about whether one ought to be pious do you inquire, and whether one ought to follow this wise God and Christ — this you deem worthy of counsel and consideration, not having even understood what befits God, whatever it may be. Believe us, at least, as you believe drunkenness, that you may be sober; believe, at least, as you do outrage, that you may live. But if you wish also to be persuaded, having beheld the manifest faith of the virtues, come, I will set before you, out of my abundance, the persuasion concerning the Word. But you — since now the ancestral customs no longer keep you from the truth, you who have been pre-instructed — you may now hear how the matter stands hereafter. And let no shame at this name forestall you, which "greatly harms men," turning them aside from salvation. Stripping ourselves, then, openly, let us contend genuinely in the stadium of truth, the holy Word being umpire and the Master of all presiding as president of the games. For no small prize lies before us — immortality. Therefore care no more, not even a little, what certain market-rabble say of you, godless dancers in superstition, pushed by folly and madness into the very pit, makers of idols and worshippers of stones. For these have dared to deify men, recording Alexander the Macedonian as a thirteenth god, whom Babylon convicted as a corpse. I admire, then, the Chian sophist, Theocritus by name; after the death of Alexander, mocking the empty opinions of men which they held about gods, Theocritus said to his fellow-citizens: "Men, take courage, so long as you see the gods dying before men."

[134719] But he who worships the visible gods and the promiscuous rabble of these created things, and makes them his companions, is far more wretched than those very demons. For God is in no way unjust, as the demons are, but, as far as may be, most just; and there is nothing more like to him than whoever of us becomes most just. "Go forth, then, to the road, all you handicraft folk, who propitiate the grim-eyed Worker-goddess, the daughter of Zeus, with standing winnowing-fans" — you witless makers and worshippers of stones. Let your Pheidias come, and your Polycleitus, and Praxiteles again, and Apelles, and all who pursue the menial crafts, earthy workers of earth. For then, says a certain prophecy, the affairs here shall fare ill, when men trust in statues. Let the petty craftsmen come again, then — for I will not cease from fair speaking. None of these has fashioned a breathing image, nor moulded soft flesh out of earth. Who melted the marrow, or who fixed the bones? Who stretched the sinews? Who blew up the veins? Who poured the blood into them, or who stretched the skin around? Where could any of them make eyes that see? Who breathed in a soul? Who gave righteousness as a gift? Who promised immortality? The Maker of all things alone, the best-craftsman Father, fashioned such a living statue as man. But your Olympian — image of an image, far out of tune with the truth — is a dumb work of Attic hands. For the image of God is his Word — and the genuine Son of the Mind is the divine Word, the archetypal light of light — and the image of the Word is the true man, the mind in man, who is therefore said to have been made "after the image and likeness of God," likened in his heart's understanding to the divine Word, and in this respect rational. But of the visible, earth-born man the earthy image is the man-shaped statue, and it appears far from the truth, a transient impress. Nothing, then, but a life full of madness has seemed to me to have been, when men gave themselves with so much zeal to matter. And custom, fostered by empty opinion, has given you a taste of slavery and of unreasonable curiosity; and the cause is ignorance of lawless and deceitful pretences, which, introducing into the human race the contrivances of ruinous deaths and of hateful idols, having devised many forms of demons, has stamped upon those who follow it a stain of long death. Take, then, the rational water; wash, you that are defiled; sprinkle yourselves clean from custom with the true drops; you must go up pure into the heavens. You are a man — that which is most common; seek out him who made you. You are a son — that which is most peculiarly your own; recognize the Father. But do you still abide in your sins, fast bound by pleasures? To whom will the Lord say, "Yours is the kingdom of the heavens"? Yours it is, if you will, you who have set your choice toward God; yours, if you will only to believe and to follow the brief proclamation, obeying which the Ninevites, by genuine repentance, exchanged the expected capture for the fair salvation. "How, then, shall I go up to the heavens?" you say. The Lord is the way, narrow indeed, but from heaven; narrow indeed, but sending up to the heavens; narrow, despised upon earth; broad, worshipped in the heavens. Then he who has not heard the Word has ignorance as the excuse of his wandering; but he who is struck on the ears, and yet bears to his soul, from his own judgement, disobedience — the wiser he seems to be, the more his understanding turns to his hurt, since he has used his prudence as accuser, not choosing the best; for man is by nature so framed as to be intimate with God. As, then, we do not force the horse to plough nor the bull to hunt, but lead each animal round to that for which it is by nature framed, so, of course, we call man too, born for the contemplation of heaven, a heavenly plant in truth, to the knowledge of God, having grasped what is proper to him and choice and peculiar to him beyond the other animals, a self-sufficient provision for the ages — piety — counselling him to prepare it. Till the soil, we say, if you are a husbandman, but know God while you till; sail, you who love seafaring, but call upon the heavenly Pilot. Has knowledge laid hold of you while you served as a soldier? Hear the general who signals just things.

[139057] As, then, men weighed down by sleep and drunkenness, come to your senses, and, looking up a little, consider what the worshipped stones mean for you, and the things you waste with empty zeal upon matter; into ignorance you consume both your goods and your life, as you spend your living upon death, having found this only as the end of your vain hope, not able even to pity yourselves, but not even fit to obey those who pity you for your wandering, enslaved by evil custom, dependent on which of your own accord you are carried to destruction to your last breath; "because the light has come into the world, and men loved darkness rather than the light," when it was possible to wipe away the things that stand in the way of salvation — both conceit and wealth and fear — saying that poetic line: "Whither am I carrying all this wealth? And whither do I myself wander?" Will you not, then, casting away these empty fancies, renounce custom itself, saying to vainglory, "False dreams, farewell; you were nothing after all"?

[140082] For what do you think, O men, the Tychon-Hermes is, and that of Andocides, and the Maimed one? Surely it is plain to everyone that they are stones, just as Hermes himself is. And as the halo is not a god, and as the rainbow is not a god, but affections of air and clouds, and as in like manner the day is not a god, nor the year, nor the time made up of these, so neither is the sun nor the moon, by which each of the things named is bounded. Who, then, being of sound mind, would suppose Correction and Punishment and Justice and Nemesis to be gods? For neither are the Erinyes nor the Fates nor Destiny gods, since neither is the state nor glory nor wealth — which painters even show to be blind. But if you deify Modesty and Love and Aphrodite, then let Shame and Impulse and Beauty and Intercourse follow upon them. So Sleep and Death, two twin gods, could no longer reasonably be reckoned by you, these being affections that befall living things naturally; nor will you justly call Doom nor Destiny nor the Fates goddesses. And if Strife and Battle are not gods, neither is Ares nor Enyo. And further, if the lightnings and the thunderbolts and the rains are not gods, how can fire and water be gods? And how the shooting stars and the comets, which come to be through an affection of the air? And he who calls Fortune a god, let him call Action too a god. If, then, none of these is reckoned a god, nor those handmade and insensible fashionings, while a certain providence of a divine power over us is plainly seen, nothing else remains but to confess this — that he alone truly is and subsists, the only truly existing God. But, like men who have drunk mandrake or some other drug, you senseless ones are; and may God grant you one day to come to your senses out of this sleep, and to understand God, and not to imagine gold or stone or tree or action or affection or disease or fear as God. "For thrice ten thousand," in truth, "are upon the bountiful earth" demons — not immortal, nor yet mortal (for they have not even shared in sensation, that they might share in death), but stony and wooden masters of men, outraging and breaking faith with life through custom. "The earth is the Lord's," it says, "and the fullness thereof." Then how dare you, while you revel in what is the Lord's, be ignorant of the Master? "Leave my earth," the Lord will say to you; "touch not the water which I give forth; partake not of the fruits which I till. Render, O man, the price of your nurture to God; recognize your Master; you are God's own creature. But how could his own justly become alien?" For that which is estranged, deprived of intimacy, is deprived of the truth. Are you not, in a manner like Niobe — or rather, to speak more mystically to you, like that Hebrew woman (the men of old called her Lot's wife) — turning to insensibility? We have received that this woman was turned to stone for her love of Sodom; and the Sodomites are the godless and those who turn to impiety, hard-hearted and senseless. Believe that these voices are recited to you from God: do not suppose that stones and timber and birds and serpents are holy, but men not; on the contrary, suppose that men are in truth holy, and that the beasts and stones are what they are. For the wretched and miserable among men believe that God cries out through a raven and a jackdaw, but keeps silence through a man, and they have honoured the raven as a messenger of God, but they hunt down the man of God, who does not croak nor caw, but speaks articulately; alas, who instructs rationally and lovingly — him they undertake to slaughter inhumanly, calling them to righteousness, neither awaiting the grace from above nor turning aside from punishment. For they do not believe God, nor learn his power. But where his love toward man is unspeakable, there his hatred of wickedness is not to be contained. Wrath nourishes the punishment upon sin, but love does good upon repentance. And most pitiable it is to be deprived of the help that comes from God. The blinding of the eyes and the deafening of the hearing is more grievous than the rest of the wicked one's encroachments; for the one is robbed of the heavenly sight, the other deprived of the divine learning. But you, maimed toward the truth, blind in mind and deaf in understanding, are not grieved, are not indignant; you have not desired to see heaven and the maker of heaven, nor sought to hear and learn of the Maker and Father of all things, joining your choice to salvation. For nothing stands in the way of him who hastens toward the knowledge of God — not unlearning, not poverty, not obscurity, not want of possessions. Nor does anyone, having marred the truly true wisdom with bronze, pray to exchange it, nor with iron; for this above all has been well said: "the good man is everywhere saving." For the emulator of the just, as the lover of him who is without need, has little need, having treasured up his blessedness in no other than God himself, where is no moth, no robber, no pirate, but the eternal giver of good things. Rightly, then, have you been likened to those serpents whose ears are stopped against the charmers. "For their wrath," says the Scripture, "is after the likeness of the serpent, like the deaf asp that stops her ears, which will not hear the voice of the charmers." But do you be charmed out of your savagery, and receive the gentle and our Word, and spit out the deadly venom, that it may be granted you, as fully as may be, to put off corruption, as those serpents put off age. Hear me, and do not stop your ears nor block your hearing, but lay to mind the things said. Fair is the medicine of immortality; at last stay your creeping coils. "For the enemies of the Lord shall lick the dust," it says. Lift up your faces from the earth into the upper air; look up to heaven; marvel; cease lying in wait for the heel of the just and hindering the way of truth; become prudent and harmless; perhaps the Lord will grant you the wing of simplicity — for he has chosen to give wings to the earth-born — that, leaving the clefts, you may dwell in the heavens. Only let us repent with the whole heart, that with the whole heart we may be able to contain God. "Hope in him," it says, "all the congregation of the people; pour out before him all your hearts." He speaks to those emptied of wickedness: he pities and fills them with righteousness. Believe, O man, in the man and God; believe, O man, in him who suffered and is worshipped. Believe, you slaves, in the living God who was dead; all men, believe in the only God of all men; believe, and receive as your wage salvation. Seek out God, "and your soul shall live." He who seeks out God is busy about his own salvation; you have found God, you have life. Let us seek, then, that we may also live. The wage of finding is life with God. "Let all that seek you be glad and rejoice in you, and let them say continually, God be magnified." A fair hymn of God is an immortal man, built up in righteousness, in whom the oracles of the truth are engraved. For where else than in a sober soul should righteousness be inscribed? Where love? Where modesty? Where gentleness? Those who have sealed up these divine Scriptures upon the soul ought, I think, to count wisdom a fair starting-place for those who turn to whatever part of life, and to reckon this same wisdom a waveless harbour of salvation; through which the fathers of children are good, those who have run to the Father; and good are the sons to their parents, who have understood the Son; and good are the husbands of wives, who remember the Bridegroom; and good are the masters of servants, who have been redeemed from the uttermost slavery. O happier than the wandering among men are the beasts! They feed upon their ignorance, as you do, but they do not counterfeit the truth; there is among them no race of flatterers; fishes are not superstitious; birds do not commit idolatry; they are astonished at the one heaven alone, since they cannot understand God, having been deemed unworthy of the Word. Then are you not ashamed, having made yourselves more irrational than the irrational, who through so many ages have worn yourselves out in godlessness? You have become children, then youths, then striplings, then men, but never good. At least respect old age, and, being at the setting of life, be sober; and even at the end of life recognize God, that the end of life may take up for you a beginning of salvation. Grow old toward superstition; come young toward piety. God will reckon you guileless children. Let the Athenian, then, follow the laws of Solon, and the Argive those of Phoroneus, and the Spartan those of Lycurgus; but if you enroll yourself as God's, heaven is your fatherland and God your lawgiver. And what are the laws? "You shall not kill; you shall not commit adultery; you shall not corrupt boys; you shall not steal; you shall not bear false witness; you shall love the Lord your God." And there are the complements of these, rational laws and holy words inscribed in the very hearts: "You shall love your neighbour as yourself; and to him that smites you on the cheek offer also the other; and you shall not covet, for by lust alone you have committed adultery." How much better, then, for men than to obtain their lusts, not even at the beginning to wish to lust after what they ought not. But you cannot endure the austerity of salvation; rather, just as we are pleased with the sweet among foods, preferring them for the smoothness of the pleasure, while the bitter, which roughen the sense, heal and make us whole — for the austerity of medicines strengthens those weak in the stomach — so custom delights and tickles, but the one, custom, thrusts into the pit; the other, truth, leads up to heaven, rough indeed at first, but a good nurse of youth. And august is this women's quarter, and sober the council of elders; nor is she hard of access, nor impossible to attain, but she is the nearest indweller of us, as Moses the all-wise hints, dwelling in the three parts that are ours — hands and mouth and heart — a genuine symbol that the truth is fulfilled by all three, by counsel and deed and word. And do not fear this either, lest the many and delightful things imagined take you away from wisdom; of your own free will you will pass over the trifling of custom, just as children too, when they have become men, cast away their playthings. With unsurpassable speed and easily-approached goodwill the divine power, shining upon the earth, has filled the whole with the seed of salvation. For not in so short a time would the Lord have accomplished so great a work without divine care — despised in appearance, worshipped in deed, the purifier and saviour and gentle one, the divine Word, the most manifest, truly God, made equal to the Master of all, because he was his Son, and the Word was in God; neither, when he was first proclaimed beforehand, disbelieved, nor, when he had taken upon him the mask of man and moulded himself in flesh and played out the saving drama of humanity, unknown; for he was a genuine champion and fellow-contestant of the creature, and, swiftly distributed among all men, having risen faster than the sun from the Father's very will, with the greatest ease shone upon us God, presenting both whence he was and who he was by the things he taught and showed — the Word, the bearer of the truce and the reconciler and our Saviour, the life-giving fountain, peaceful, poured out over the whole face of the earth, through whom, so to speak, all things have now become a sea of good things.

[151699] 11. Consider a little, if you will, from above, the divine benefit. The first man played, free, in paradise, for he was a child of God; but when, yielding to pleasure (the serpent is the allegory of pleasure, creeping upon its belly, an earthly wickedness, fed upon matter), he was led aside by lusts, the child, made manly by disobedience and disobeying his Father, was ashamed before God. See what pleasure availed: the man who through simplicity was free was found bound by sins. The Lord wished to loose him again from his bonds, and, bound in flesh (this is a divine mystery), he subdued the serpent and enslaved the tyrant, death; and — most marvellous of all — that man who had been led astray by pleasure, bound by corruption, he showed loosed by hands stretched out. O mystic wonder! The Lord was laid low, but man rose up; and he that fell from paradise receives a greater prize for his obedience — the heavens. Wherefore it seems to me that, since the Word himself has come to us from heaven, we ought no longer to go to human teaching, busying ourselves about Athens and the rest of Greece, and Ionia besides. For if our teacher is he who has filled all things with holy powers — by creation, salvation, beneficence, lawgiving, prophecy, teaching — the teacher now instructs us in all things, and the whole has already become Athens and Greece through the Word. For surely you did not believe a poetic myth that records Minos the Cretan as the familiar of Zeus, and yet will disbelieve us, who have become disciples of God, having taken up the truly true wisdom, which the topmost of the philosophers only hinted at, but the disciples of Christ both grasped and proclaimed. And indeed all Christ, so to speak, is not divided: he is neither barbarian nor Jew nor Greek, neither male nor female, but a new man, remade by the Holy Spirit of God.

[153555] Then the other counsels and precepts are paltry and concern particulars: whether one ought to marry, whether to take part in public life, whether to beget children; but the only universal exhortation, and that for the whole of life, in every season, in every circumstance, straining toward the chief end, life, is piety; in accordance with which alone it is necessary to live, that we may live forever. Philosophy, as the elders say, is a long-lasting counsel, wooing the eternal love of wisdom; but "the commandment of the Lord is far-shining, enlightening the eyes." Receive Christ, receive sight, receive your light, "that you may know well both God and man." "Lovely is the Word that enlightened us," beyond gold and precious stone; "it is sweet beyond honey and the honeycomb." For how is he not lovely who made the mind, buried in darkness, clear, and sharpened the light-bringing eyes of the soul? For just as, the sun not being, for all the other stars it would be night over all, so, had we not known the Word and been illumined by him, we should have differed in nothing from fattened birds, fed in darkness and nourished for death. Let us receive the light, that we may receive God; let us receive the light, and become disciples of the Lord. For this very thing has been promised to the Father: "I will declare your name to my brethren; in the midst of the assembly I will hymn you." Hymn and declare to me your Father, God; your tales shall save, your song shall instruct me. Until now I wandered, seeking God; but since you light my way, Lord, I both find God through you and receive the Father from you; I become your fellow-heir, since you were not ashamed of me as a brother. Let us put away, then, let us put away forgetfulness of the truth; the ignorance and the darkness that stands in our way, like a mist upon the sight, having put it down, let us behold the God who truly is, first hymning to him this voice: "Hail, light." Light from heaven shone upon us, who were buried in darkness and shut up in the shadow of death, purer than the sun, sweeter than the life here. That light is eternal life, and whatever has partaken of it lives; but night fears the light, and, setting through fear, gives way to the day of the Lord; all things have become unsleeping light, and the setting has turned to a rising. This is what the new creation desired; for he who rides upon all things, the sun of righteousness, goes round upon mankind impartially, imitating the Father, who makes his sun rise upon all men and sheds down the dew of truth. He has turned the setting into a rising, and crucified death into life, and, snatching man out of destruction, has hung him up in the ether, transplanting corruption into incorruption and changing earth into heavens — the husbandman of God, "signalling good things, and rousing the peoples to a good work," reminding them of the true livelihood, and granting us the great and truly divine and inalienable inheritance of the Father, making man divine by heavenly teaching, "putting laws into their understanding and writing them upon the heart." What laws does he prescribe? "That all shall know God, from small to great"; and "I will be merciful to them," says God, "and their sins I will remember no more." Let us receive the laws of life; let us be persuaded by God exhorting; let us learn of him, that he may be merciful; let us render, even to him who has no need, a thankful wage of well-being, as a kind of rent to God for our dwelling here. "Gold for bronze, a hundred oxen's worth for nine." For a little faith he gives you so great an earth to till, water to drink and other to sail, air to breathe, fire to serve you, a world to dwell in; from here he has granted you to send out a colony to the heavens. These great and so many works and graces he has let out to you for a little faith. Then those who have believed the cheats accept their amulets and incantations as supposedly saving; but you will not bind upon yourselves the heavenly amulet itself, the saving Word, and, trusting the incantation of God, be released from the passions, which are diseases of the soul, and torn away from sin? For sin is eternal death. Are you utterly toothless and blind, like moles, doing nothing but eating and living in darkness, dribbling away in corruption? But there is — there is — the truth that cried out, "Out of darkness light shall shine." Let the light, then, shine in the hidden part of the man, in the heart, and let the rays of knowledge rise, revealing and making to shine the man hidden within, the disciple of the light, the friend and fellow-heir of Christ — especially when the most precious and most reverend name of a good Father comes to the knowledge of a pious and good child of a good Father, who commands gentle things and enjoins on the child the things that save. He who obeys him has the advantage in every way: he follows God, he obeys the Father, he came to know him through wandering, he loved God, he loved his neighbour, he fulfilled the commandment, he seeks the prize, he demands the promise. And it is ever set before God to save the flock of men. For this cause too the good God sent the good shepherd. And the Word, having unfolded the truth, showed men the height of salvation, that, repenting, they might be saved, or, disobeying, be judged. This is the proclamation of righteousness, good tidings to those who obey, judgement to those who disobey. But while the loud-clamouring trumpet, sounding, gathers soldiers and proclaims war, shall not Christ, having breathed a peaceful strain to the ends of the earth, gather his own peaceful soldiers? He has gathered, O man, the bloodless army by blood and by the Word, and has put into their hands the kingdom of the heavens. The trumpet of Christ is his gospel: he sounded it, and we heard. Let us arm ourselves peacefully, putting on the breastplate of righteousness and taking up the shield of faith and setting about our head the helmet of salvation, and let us sharpen the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God. So the apostle peacefully draws us up in array; these are our invulnerable weapons; with these armed, let us set ourselves against the wicked one; the fiery darts of the wicked one let us quench with the watery points dipped by the Word, requiting kindnesses with thankful blessings and honouring God with the divine Word. "For while you are yet speaking," it says, "he will say, Behold, I am here." O this holy and blessed power, through which God lives as a fellow-citizen with men! Better, then, and best, to become at once the imitator and the servant of the best of beings; for no one can imitate God except by serving him in holiness, nor again serve and reverence him except by imitating him. The heavenly and truly divine love thus comes to men, whenever in the very soul the truly fair, kindled by the divine Word, can shine forth; and, what is greatest, salvation runs side by side with the genuine willing to be saved, choice and life being yoked together, so to speak. Therefore this exhortation of truth alone, like the most faithful of friends, abides with us to the last breath, and becomes a good escort, with the whole and perfect breath of the soul, to those who depart toward heaven. Why, then, do I exhort you? I am eager that you be saved. This Christ wills; in one word he gives you life. And who is he? Learn briefly. The Word of truth, the Word of incorruption, who regenerates man, bringing him back to the truth, the goad of salvation, who drives out corruption, who chases away death, who has built up in men a temple, that in men he may set up God. Purify the temple, and the pleasures and the slacknesses, like a flower of a day, leave to the wind and the fire; the fruits of self-control cultivate prudently, and set yourself up as firstfruits to God, that you may be not only his work but also his grace; and both befit the friend of Christ, both to appear worthy of the kingdom and to be deemed worthy of the kingdom.

[161572] 12. Let us flee, then, custom; let us flee it like a dangerous headland, or the menace of Charybdis, or the fabled Sirens. It chokes man, turns him away from the truth, leads him from life; it is a snare, a pit, a hole, a greedy evil — custom. "Keep your ship away from that smoke and wave." Let us flee, fellow-voyagers, let us flee this wave; it belches fire; it is a wicked island, heaped with bones and corpses, and there sings within it a fair little harlot, pleasure, delighting in vulgar music: "Come hither, much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans; bring your ship to land, that you may hear a more divine voice." She praises you, O sailor, and calls you much-hymned, and the harlot makes the glory of the Greeks her own. Let her feed upon the dead; a heavenly breath helps you. Pass pleasure by; she beguiles. "Let no woman, with her decked-out rear, deceive your mind, prattling coaxing words, prying into your barn." Sail past the song; it works death. Only if you will, you have conquered destruction, and, bound fast to the wood, you shall be loosed from all corruption; the Word of God shall steer you, and the Holy Spirit shall bring you to anchor in the harbours of the heavens. Then you shall behold my God, and be initiated into those holy mysteries, and enjoy the things hidden in the heavens, reserved for me, "which neither ear has heard nor has entered into the heart of any."

[162983] "And truly I seem to see two suns and a double Thebes," said one as he revelled to the idols, drunk with unmingled ignorance; but I would pity him in his drunken folly, and would call him who is thus beside himself to a sober salvation, since the Lord too welcomes the repentance, and not the death, of the sinner. Come, O madman, not leaning on the thyrsus, not wreathed with ivy; cast off the headband, cast off the fawn-skin, be sober; I will show you the Word and the mysteries of the Word, recounting them according to your own image. This is a mountain beloved of God, not laid out for tragedies like Cithaeron, but dedicated to the dramas of truth, a sober mountain, shaded with pure groves. And in it revel not the sisters of thunder-struck Semele, the Maenads, who are initiated into a foul distribution of flesh, but the daughters of God, the fair lambs, declaring the august rites of the Word, gathering a sober chorus. The chorus are the just; the song is a hymn of the King of all; the maidens make music, the angels glorify, the prophets speak, a sound of music is sent forth; at a run they pursue the holy band; those who are called hasten, longing to receive the Father. Come to me too, old man — yes, you — leaving Thebes, and casting away your divination and your Bacchic frenzy, be led by the hand to the truth. Behold, I give you the wood to lean upon; hasten, Teiresias, believe; you shall see. Christ shines more brightly than the sun, through whom the eyes of the blind see again; night shall flee from you, fire shall be afraid, death shall depart; you shall see the heavens, O old man, you who do not see Thebes. O the truly holy mysteries! O the pure light! I am led with torches to behold the heavens and God; I become holy as I am initiated; the Lord is hierophant, and seals the initiate as he leads him to the light, and presents to the Father him who has believed, to be kept through the ages. These are the revels of my mysteries. If you will, be initiated too, and you shall dance with angels about the unbegotten and imperishable and only truly existing God, the Word of God hymning together with us. This Jesus is eternal, one great High Priest of the one God who is at once the same and the Father; he prays for men and exhorts men: "Hearken, you countless tribes" — or rather, all you of men that are rational, both barbarians and Greeks; the whole race of men I call, of whom I am the Maker by the will of the Father. Come to me, to be marshalled under one God and the one Word of God; and do not only excel the irrational animals by reason, but, alone of all mortal things, take from me the harvest of immortality. For I will, I will impart to you this grace too, supplying the whole benefit, incorruption; and I give you the Word as a gift, the knowledge of God; I give you myself, perfect. For this is what I am; this is what God wills; this is symphony, this is the harmony of the Father; this is the Son, this is Christ, this is the Word of God, the arm of the Lord, the power of all things, the will of the Father. O you who were of old images, but not all of them like — I wish to set you right toward the archetype, that you may become like to me also. I will anoint you with the unguent of faith, through which you cast off corruption, and I will show bare the figure of righteousness, through which you ascend to God. "Come unto me, all you that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest; take my yoke upon you, and learn of me, for I am gentle and lowly of heart, and you shall find rest unto your souls; for my yoke is good and my burden is light." Let us hasten, let us run, O statues of the Word, dear to God and like to God; let us hasten, let us run, let us take up his yoke, let us put on incorruption, let us love Christ, the fair charioteer of men; he led the colt under the yoke together with the old beast, and, having harnessed the pair of mankind, he steers the chariot straight toward immortality, hastening to God, to fulfil clearly what he hinted at — first into Jerusalem, but now driving into the heavens, a fairest spectacle to the Father, the eternal, victorious Son. Let us, then, become emulous of fair things and lovers of God, and let us gain the greatest of goods, God and life. Our helper is the Word; let us take courage in him, and let no such longing for silver and gold, nor for glory, ever come upon us, as the longing for the very Word of truth. For neither is it pleasing to God himself, if we count the things of greatest worth as least, and choose rather the manifest outrages of ignorance and unlearning and idleness and idolatry, and the uttermost impiety. For not without reason do the children of the philosophers reckon all that the senseless do to be acts of unholiness and impiety; and, describing ignorance itself as a form of madness, they confess that the many are nothing else than mad. There is no room, then, to doubt, or to be mad; but, holding fast to the truth with all our strength, we must follow God with all our power, being sober, and reckoning all things to be his, as indeed they are; and besides, having learned that we are his fairest possession, we must entrust ourselves to God, loving the Lord God, and reckoning this our work throughout the whole of life. And if the things of friends are common, and man is dear to God — for indeed he is a friend of God, the Word mediating — then all things become man's, because all things are God's, and all things are common to the two friends, God and man. It is time, then, for us to say that the pious man alone is rich and sober and well-born, and in this respect an image of God with the likeness, and to declare and believe that, having become just and holy with prudence through Christ Jesus, he is to this extent already like even to God. For the prophet does not conceal the grace, saying: "I said, You are gods, and all of you sons of the Most High." For us, us he has adopted, and of us alone he wishes to be called Father, not of the disobedient. For thus indeed stand our affairs, who are the followers of Christ: as the counsels, such also the words; as the words, such the deeds; and as the works, such the life. Good throughout is the whole life of men who have come to know Christ. Enough, I think, of words, though out of love toward man I have gone on rather far, pouring out what I had from God, as one exhorting toward the greatest of goods, salvation. For concerning the life that has no pause at all, not even the words are willing ever to cease their hierophany. And for you there is left this final end, the profitable, to choose — either judgement or grace; for I, for my part, deem it not even worthy of doubt which of the two is better, nor is it lawful to compare life with destruction.
