CHAPTER: 10-hymn-10



[0] 10. The Sayings of Saint Romanus the Martyr against the Heathen.

[66] Romanus, brave champion of Christ the God,

[110] move the organ of my tongueless mouth, my patron,

[161] grant a polished song to one most unskilled in speech,

[217] make me sing in harmony the wonders of your praises,

[271] for you yourself know that the dumb can be made to speak.

[330] A savage torturer tore out the quill of your palate

[383] and throat, yet did not enjoin silence

[423] upon the mouth with which you confessed God.

[469] The voice that witnesses to truth cannot be quenched,

[524] not even if it throbs with its passages cut away.

[575] So our halting speech, with a feeble tongue,

[621] stammers and labours in discordant measures,

[667] but if you sprinkle the heart with heavenly dew

[716] and water the breast with spiritual milk,

[759] a hoarse voice will loosen its hindered sounds.

[808] The evangelist wrote that the Messiah himself

[855] gave such precepts to the apostles:

[892] “Do not, when my mystery is to be

[927] proclaimed, seek out your words with forethought;

[978] I will supply to the unprepared what they shall speak.”

[1035] I myself am dumb, but mighty in eloquence

[1078] my tongue, Christ, will speak clearly.

[1118] He himself will explain what tumults the demon,

[1167] with his last breath, while he is being tamed, has stirred up,

[1231] a worse plague in his fury at the very end.

[1276] So a serpent, wounded by the stroke of a dart,

[1324] bites back at the iron, and, fiercer with pain,

[1373] lingers, shaking it, with clenched teeth,

[1416] but the shaft stays fixed the deeper,

[1455] and it feels no vain peril of its bites.

[1497] Galerius, as it chanced, ruled the state of the Roman

[1552] world as commander, as antiquity reports,

[1595] cruel, savage, harsh, implacable.

[1630] He had sent edicts far and wide into all the world,

[1683] that whoever would rather live should deny Christ.

[1735] This the serpent dictates with a royal mouth,

[1782] who, coming forth from the tombs of the dead,

[1829] cries out: “Why before the time, by your swift coming,

[1885] do you dissolve my realms? Spare me, Son of the Highest,

[1943] or bid me at least possess the hearts of swine.”

[1993] The prefect, intent on these affairs,

[2032] Asclepiades, commands his soldiers to go

[2074] and to drag the churchman straight from the sanctuaries,

[2132] a people to be handed over to chains

[2170] unless it should spurn the Nazarene discipline.

[2219] Soon, thinking to burst into the temple himself

[2268] and eager to ravage the holy of holies,

[2309] the profane man, with impious arms, made ready

[2357] to lay the altar’s shrine utterly low

[2396] and to break the very doors into ruin.

[2436] A herald runs ahead, these things being suddenly known,

[2493] Romanus, a hero of keen excellence,

[2530] announces that the enemies are coming in arms,

[2578] forearming the spirits of the fearful with exhortations,

[2636] that they may stand ready and not yield to the whirlwind.

[2695] The Christian flock conspires, leagued

[2735] in one spirit, an undaunted band

[2769] of mothers, men, little ones, and virgins;

[2813] fixed and settled in all is the resolve

[2854] to guard the faith or gladly to die.

[2892] The soldier, repulsed, reports back to the benches

[2944] that Romanus is the leader of the rebellious people,

[2998] that all blaze with stubborn boldness,

[3038] that they obstinately offer their bared throats,

[3088] so that the strong may meet a glorious death.

[3135] Romanus is thereupon ordered to be seized in haste

[3187] and, as the sole instigator and torch of all,

[3234] to plead the cause on behalf of the defiant people,

[3287] he goes without resisting and asks to be bound,

[3336] and of his own accord twists his bent hands behind his back.

[3398] Love of the crown almost forestalls the savage

[3446] art of the lictor, offering of its own will his bared

[3501] ribs to be cut by the cloven claws.

[3538] He bursts in over the high threshold and, while the heralds

[3599] are dumb with amazement, he himself drags the torturer along.

[3662] To him standing before him the tyrant thus begins:

[3714] “Infamous monster, vile, accursed,

[3750] you, agitator of the city and flighty storm

[3795] of the crowd, you stir up their unstable minds,

[3844] that the ignorant mob may not yield to the laws.

[3894] Something popular, under the colour of glory,

[3941] the unlettered throng has believed,

[3978] that they will be consecrated through the ages

[4026] if they wage wars against the gods like giants

[4074] and, conquered, are overwhelmed by the flames of mountains.

[4135] This spectacle you have prepared, lost wretch,

[4183] of bloody slaughter, by the killing of citizens,

[4233] whom, as profane and guilty of the unhallowed age,

[4285] it is necessary, with you for their master, to put to death:

[4347] you are the cause of their death, you the standard-bearer of their woes.

[4421] Unless I err, it is just that what, a wicked author,

[4475] you compelled many to endure, you as their executioner,

[4532] should recoil upon you, and for so great slaughters,

[4586] which are soon to come, you first should pay with destruction,

[4650] and yourself bear what you had urged should be borne.”

[4706] To this that man replies with a free mouth:

[4751] “I embrace it, O prefect, nor do I withdraw,

[4797] that for the faithful people I alone be sacrificed,

[4850] worthy to undergo all things, if you ask me,

[4896] whatever your cruelty shall command.

[4934] It is not lawful for the slaves of idols and demons

[4987] to enter the holy house of our salvation,

[5030] lest the pure place of prayer be polluted;

[5074] I trust in the Holy Spirit that never will it

[5121] be granted you to touch that blessed threshold with your foot,

[5185] unless perhaps, made one of ours, into our flock

[5235] you should deserve to be received, which may God the Father bring to pass.”

[5312] Kindled by this, Asclepiades had ordered

[5354] the body to be disembowelled, to hang aloft

[5399] on the rack, and to be stretched by hooks and bonds.

[5453] But the attendants suggest to the raging man

[5499] that he is noble by an ancient lineage

[5539] and, by his many merits, first among the citizens,

[5591] he bids the death-dealing stake be removed,

[5636] lest a plebeian punishment condemn an illustrious man.

[5692] “Let his back,” he says, “be beaten with frequent blows,

[5750] and let his neck, lashed with lead, swell up.

[5797] Each person is fittingly punished

[5832] and it matters much whether he is base or noble;

[5882] by the rank of the accused the manner is given to the torments.”

[5948] So the martyr, beaten by that hail of blows,

[5994] after he sang a hymn amid the leaden strokes,

[6041] standing upright begins: “Far be it that birth

[6089] of my parents or the law of the senate should make me noble:

[6151] the noble sect of Christ ennobles men.

[6191] If you would reckon what is the first origin

[6237] of our cradle in the weaving of a pedigree,

[6282] we begin from the mouth of God the Father,

[6326] whom each man serves, that man is truly noble:

[6374] rebellious to the Father he is found base.

[6418] A new honour then accrues to the pedigree

[6461] and a vast splendour, as a magistracy comes,

[6507] if the branded wounds of iron and fire

[6547] mark a faithful witness who confesses the name

[6595] and an illustrious death follow the force of pains.

[6648] Beware that you be not perversely willing to be kind,

[6703] nor, relenting, spare me gently;

[6737] press upon my limbs, torturer, that I may be noble,

[6790] enlarged by these, if I may enjoy such successes,

[6841] I will count the lineage of father and mother at a straw.

[6900] These very summits of your dignities,

[6939] what do you think they are? Do they not pass in a rush,

[6996] the fasces, the axes, the chair, the bordered togas,

[7050] the lictor, the tribunal, and the three hundred badges

[7106] with which you swell, and soon you grow flat again?

[7159] When you enter the consulship, as house-slaves are wont,

[7217] (I am ashamed to say it) you feed chickens with grain;

[7273] from an ivory eagle its bearer takes arrogance

[7321] and is proud, puffed up with the bone of a beast

[7371] whose shape is that of a bird.

[7403] Now if beneath the altars, at the feet of little images,

[7461] you lie below, set under a sawn oak,

[7499] what could I think more abject for you?

[7540] I know the toga’d nobles bare their feet

[7582] before the carriage at the rites of the Idaean Mother.

[7638] A blackish stone, to be borne on a chariot,

[7683] set in the face of a woman, shut in silver,

[7728] which, while you lead it forward to the washing,

[7778] rubbing your feet, your shoes removed,

[7818] you go all the way to the brook of Almo.

[7860] What of that foul procession? Surely it shows

[7907] you to be ignoble, when you run as Luperci.

[7952] Whom of the little slaves should I not deem the basest,

[8009] if, naked, running through all the streets,

[8054] he should strike the girls, struck with a sportive lash?

[8112] I pity your rites and your princes,

[8149] and your morals, Rome, supreme head of the age,

[8198] come, let us unfold, if you please, the mysteries,

[8250] prefect, that are yours: now you must needs hear,

[8301] whether you will or not, what filth you worship.

[8351] Nor does that madness, with which you swell, frighten me,

[8410] that, scowling, supine, and stiff,

[8446] you threaten the torments of a harsh death:

[8491] if you strive to move me by any means,

[8531] contend with me by reason, not by fury.

[8572] You bid me, forsaking the rites of the Father and Christ,

[8631] to adore with you a thousand females and males,

[8680] goddesses and gods, and, of a double sex,

[8723] sons, grandsons, great-great-grandsons brought forth,

[8778] and the whole sordid offspring of so many debaucheries.

[8835] Girls wed, are often beguiled by tricks,

[8877] are oppressed by the frauds of their lovers,

[8923] incests burn, the thefts of adulterers grow hot,

[8973] the husband deceives, the wife hates the concubine,

[9026] chains bind the adulterous gods together.

[9069] Show me, I pray, at what altars you command

[9114] that my turf should smoke with a slaughtered sheep?

[9167] Shall I go to Delphi? But the corrupt fame

[9211] of the wrestler-youth forbids it, whom your god

[9260] made effeminate by the licence of the gymnasium.

[9310] Soon the unclean one wept for the boy slain

[9355] by the heavy discus, and consecrated the flowery minion.

[9413] The same god, hired, fed another’s flock,

[9456] then, suffering the thief of the lost herd,

[9501] a slothful herdsman, he even lost his very weapons.

[9554] Or shall I go to Cybele’s grove of pines?

[9597] But the Gallus-boy stands in the way, who for lust,

[9650] through a sad wound and through a cut disgrace,

[9699] safe from the embrace of the unchaste goddess,

[9747] a eunuch to be wept for through the Mother’s many rites.

[9805] But, I suppose, I shall embrace the threshold of great Jove,

[9867] who, if he were summoned as a defendant by your laws,

[9922] entangled in the snares of the menacing Julian law,

[9975] conquered, would pay the stern Scantinian law too,

[10027] with you for judge, fit to go to prison.

[10069] What? Do you reckon that the founder of the golden

[10121] ages should be worshipped? Whom you do not deny

[10170] to have lurked in flight by stealth while he dreaded harm;

[10230] whom, if Jupiter hears living happily,

[10270] he must needs punish the accomplices of his hiding.

[10323] What, amid the altars of discordant deities,

[10369] do you think should be done? The offended valour

[10419] of Mars will be indignant if the Lemnian is worshipped,

[10476] each will feel Juno’s anger who consecrates

[10521] Hercules as a god with an image or a shrine.

[10567] You say the poets freely invent these things,

[10614] but they themselves too are dedicated with you

[10662] to such mysteries, and what they describe, they worship,

[10720] why do you so gladly read over the abomination?

[10769] Why is it applauded in the theatres, with you watching?

[10826] The swan-seducer sins upon the stage,

[10865] the player dances the Thunderer with a bull’s horns:

[10919] you, the spectator of these, sit as chief pontiff

[10970] and laugh yourself, nor wash it away by denying,

[11020] while the fame of so great a god is defiled.

[11066] Why are you, a consecrated man, dissolved in laughter

[11121] when the god feigns himself Alcmena’s husband?

[11169] A stage harlot openly bewails the wounded Adonis

[11219] with lustful passion,

[11242] and the brothel of holy Cypris does not move you?

[11293] What of the fact that the truth lies open in the very images,

[11356] shaped in bronze by the traces of crimes?

[11399] What does the seal mean, always fixed to Jove,

[11447] of the attendant bird? Surely the swift squire,

[11496] a pander, who bore off the minion to the tyrant.

[11546] Ceres holds out a torch with her robe ungirt:

[11593] why, if none of the gods seized her virgin daughter,

[11647] whom the mother, seeking by night, wanders ever-watching?

[11706] We see the Tirynthian spinning the threads:

[11751] why, if he was not the mockery of Neaera?

[11794] What? Shall I detest the monsters of the rustics as gods,

[11853] Fauns, Priapuses, presiders over the pipes,

[11898] swimming nymphs and watery dwellers,

[11936] set beneath a deep lake, after the manner of frogs,

[11989] a right of divinity in cheap seaweed?

[12028] To worship these you call me, good censor?

[12072] Can you reckon any such thing, if you are of sound mind,

[12130] as holy? Does it not move the lungs

[12167] with derision to gaze on these absurdities

[12211] which drunken old women fashion in their dreams?

[12261] Or if anywhere there is any mystic vanity

[12304] to be worshipped by us, begin first yourself:

[12351] adore indiscriminately whatever is sacred on earth,

[12404] the Latin gods and the Egyptian gods,

[12443] to whom Rome makes offering, to whom Canopus prays.

[12496] You pray to Venus; pray to the ape as well.

[12541] You like the consecrated asp of Aesculapius:

[12587] why do the crocodile, the ibis, and the dog displease you?

[12647] Set up religious little altars to leeks,

[12689] venerate the bitter onion, the biting garlic.

[12736] Are the sooty household gods appeased with incense,

[12789] and the consecrated little vegetables spat out?

[12838] Or why is a greater majesty believed to belong to the hearths

[12901] than to what is born in the hoed gardens?

[12944] If there is divinity in pots, there is divinity in leeks too.

[13007] But a fair thing it is, a form carved in bronze.

[13057] What shall I imprecate upon the workshops of Greece,

[13111] which have founded gods for foolish nations?

[13157] The tongs of Myron, the hammer of Polyclitus

[13203] are the nature and origin of your heavenly ones.

[13253] An art effective in sowing errors,

[13289] while it curls about the stiff beard of Jove,

[13336] while, gently bending the flowing hair,

[13377] it files the locks and the clusters of Liber,

[13424] and while it roughens Minerva’s breast with snakes,

[13477] it cast a black dread into the terrified,

[13520] that they should shudder at the bronze as the Thunderer’s bolt,

[13585] tremble at the poison of the hissing Gorgon,

[13631] and think that the youth, after the Indian triumphs,

[13685] can strike with the thyrsus, though he is drunk.

[13735] Then, because they see Diana softly girt,

[13778] they greatly fear the bow of the hunting virgin;

[13828] if perchance the molten mass has fashioned the face

[13881] of a grimmer Hercules with a wrinkled surface,

[13929] he is believed to threaten with his club unless he is worshipped.

[13996] Now what terror seizes the hearts of the fearful

[14046] if the artist has neatly expressed Juno’s anger!

[14096] As though, gazing with averted glances,

[14137] she turned her face from the sacrificing victim,

[14187] the stone with stern brow counterfeits threats.

[14236] I wonder that you did not consecrate Mentor himself,

[14290] and that Phidias himself has no temple and altars,

[14342] the makers of the gods, or the parents of the deities,

[14398] who, if they had stood more slothfully at the forges,

[14453] there would be no molten Jupiter.

[14488] Do you not blush, fool, given over to your village,

[14541] that you have always wasted so many viands

[14585] which, foolish, you offered to such gods,

[14628] whom a ladle, a basin, a tankard, a frying-pan,

[14677] broken and melted vessels, have furnished?

[14721] Yet I pardon these things in the foolish common folk,

[14776] whom party-coloured wool on a post frightens,

[14823] whom a lying mountebank often deceives,

[14864] to whom everything is holy that the rancid

[14908] songs of toothless hags have urged should be feared.

[14962] But you learned and lettered men I wonder at,

[15009] whom the weighed rule of life governs,

[15049] that you know not by what law either things divine

[15101] or things mortal stand, how great a majesty rules

[15152] whatever is created, which created all things.

[15200] God, eternal, a thing beyond all reckoning,

[15245] is not enclosed by thought, nor by sight,

[15288] he surpasses every measure of the human mind

[15334] and cannot be grasped by our gaze,

[15370] and outside and within he fills and overflows.

[15418] Timeless, before the first day,

[15451] he obtains to be and to have always been, the One;

[15503] himself the true light, and author of true light,

[15554] though he was light, he poured out his light:

[15601] from light this radiance born is the Son.

[15644] One is the power of the Father, one too the power of the Son,

[15707] and one, sprung from one light, the splendour begotten

[15763] shone again with the full deity of brightness.

[15811] The simple nature of the one God prevails,

[15855] and whatever is anywhere, one power founded,

[15901] heaven and earth, the force of the sea-deep,

[15947] the orbs that preside over days and nights,

[15992] the winds, storms, lightnings, rains, clouds,

[16039] the northern stars, the evening stars, heat, snows,

[16092] fountains, frosts, and metals, and rivers,

[16136] crags, plains, the valleys of the mountains,

[16182] beasts, birds, creeping and swimming things,

[16228] beasts of burden, cattle, draft-animals, monsters,

[16280] flowers, shrubs, buds, herbs, trees,

[16318] those that are for fragrance and those that flourish for food.

[16382] These God wrought not by labour and art,

[16424] but by a command, which his power put forth,

[16470] he bade them be; things were made that were not,

[16520] by the Word he created the all-formed engine;

[16567] the Father’s power was ever in the Word.

[16609] You have known him: now learn the rite

[16649] and manner of worship, what kind of temple,

[16694] what offerings he has ordained to be dedicated,

[16743] what vows he asks, what priests he wills,

[16786] what nectar he commands to be sacrificed there.

[16835] A temple for himself he founded in the mind of man,

[16888] living, serene, sentient, breathing,

[16926] incapable of being dissolved, nor destructible,

[16975] fair, lovely, eminent in its summit,

[17013] painted over with distinct colours.

[17050] There a priest stands on the consecrated threshold,

[17103] and the maiden Faith guards the first doors,

[17149] her hair entwined with royal bonds;

[17186] she asks that victims be sacrificed to Christ and the Father

[17248] which she knows please him, white-robed, simple:

[17298] modesty of brow, innocence of heart,

[17336] the quiet of peace, chastity of body,

[17375] the fear of God, the rule of knowledge,

[17416] the sober frugality of fastings,

[17450] a hope not cast down, and an ever-bountiful hand.

[17501] From these victims a pleasant vapour rises,

[17546] surpassing the fragrance of balsam, incense, saffron,

[17601] the moist airs of Persian spices.

[17636] Lifted thence, it is borne all the way to heaven

[17686] and, made prosperous, delights the kind God.

[17732] Whoever, hostile, forbids this discipline,

[17776] forbids the approved man to live and to follow the holy,

[17834] forbids the vigour of the mind to strain on high,

[17885] and calls the keen edge of our fire down to the earth,

[17941] nor suffers the force of prudence to be roused.

[17990] O blindness of the heathen, sunk in mire!

[18033] O fleshly hearts of the nations!

[18067] O dense error! O dark race,

[18096] a friend to the earth, given over to the corpse,

[18146] ever gazing on things below, never above!

[18189] Is it not the height of frenzy and the last madness

[18242] to think them gods who are begotten in marriages,

[18293] to seek a spiritual thing in an earthly way,

[18339] to consecrate the elements of the world upon altars,

[18393] to believe that what is created is the creator,

[18442] to make supplication to a stake hewn with an axe,

[18493] to sprinkle inscribed stones with the blood of a boar,

[18549] to entreat altars with bits of beef,

[18587] when you know that those you consecrate were men,

[18638] to lick the carrion-urns of the accused?

[18680] Cease, judge of the age, to command so great a crime

[18734] to men who are strong and free,

[18767] nothing is loftier than the love of truth.

[18811] To those who maintain the name of the eternal God

[18862] there is no cause for fear; death itself lies subject.”

[18919] Long had Asclepiades been seething within,

[18963] while the martyr discoursed, with a treacherous anger,

[19019] fretted with deep gall, while he is long silent

[19068] and conceives bile in his hidden heart;

[19109] at last he vomits out the force of his latent fury:

[19162] “By Jupiter! What is this that I hear from this man?

[19216] He stands among the altars and the images of the gods

[19271] and, what I am compelled to confess, in the midst of the forum,

[19336] while I am silent, and the lost wretch declaims,

[19386] defiling all that is sacred with his impious mouth.

[19439] O the law of our forefathers! O the state of the old custom!

[19501] The inventions of the Pompilian kings, for the public welfare,

[19565] our age carps at,

[19584] what new error has brought in these sophists,

[19631] who argue that the gods are not to be worshipped?

[19682] Now the Christian dogma is born to us

[19721] after a thousand consuls have at last been unrolled

[19774] from the city of Rome, not to recount the Nestors.

[19826] Whatever rises new, once was not.

[19861] Do you wish to know the supreme power of things? Consult Pyrrha.

[19927] Where was that supreme God of yours then,

[19970] when, by the favour of the gods, the boy of Mars,

[20021] Romulus, was founding the seven-hilled citadel?

[20070] That Rome is mighty, founded under good auspices,

[20121] she owes to Jupiter the Stayer and the other gods.

[20173] This is holy from of old, this handed down from our ancestors:

[20237] that we must appease the shrines for the prince’s triumphs,

[20298] that a favourable campaign may second his glory,

[20348] and that, the enemies subdued,

[20380] the leader may curb the quiet world with laws.

[20428] Gird yourself then, whoever you are, most worthless,

[20482] to pray the gods duly with us for the imperial

[20530] life, or, what for a public enemy

[20565] must be suffered, pay the penalty with your blood,

[20617] to have scorned the temples is to have spurned the prince.”

[20678] Then he: “Never for the welfare and the greatest

[20728] and bravest cohorts of the prince

[20763] will I pray otherwise than that they may serve faithfully

[20822] and be reborn to the Father in the waters of Christ,

[20876] and themselves receive the Paraclete from heaven,

[20927] that they may spurn the darkness of idols,

[20971] that they may behold that light of eternal hope,

[21021] flowing not into juicy eyes

[21050] nor flashing through the windows of the body,

[21097] but which shines within for pure minds.

[21138] The thick pupil of the flesh sees the thick,

[21184] and a perishable thing sees what must be dissolved:

[21237] the soul’s fluid is fit for seeing fluid things;

[21287] a fervent nature alone takes the glittering force

[21338] of the most fervent deity.

[21366] I wish that your emperor might know this light,

[21415] and mine—if he is willing to become mine;

[21458] for if he resists the Christian name,

[21497] that emperor of mine will be no such thing:

[21542] believe me, I will never serve one who commands a crime.”

[21601] “Do you stand still, ministers?” the judge cries out,

[21656] “Do you stand still and hold back your avenging hands?

[21712] Do you not split the flesh broken with furrows,

[21761] and search out the soul hidden within,

[21801] whence the profane voice bursts forth against the prince?”

[21861] The soldiers split, with a gaping blade, on both sides

[21917] the side of the hanging man,

[21947] they plough long wounds, trailing, through his limbs,

[22002] they cut the slanting across the straight, the straight across the crosswise,

[22081] and now his breast grows white, the bones laid bare.

[22135] Striving, they pant, they melt with sweat,

[22179] while the hero on whom they rage is at peace.

[22226] Amid these things Romanus of his own will adds to speak:

[22284] “If you wish, O prefect, to know the truth,

[22329] all this, whatever we are mangled with, does not pain me.

[22388] It pains me that error has lodged in your breast,

[22439] that you drag these lost peoples with you.

[22483] They run in throngs from every side to the spectacle,

[22538] the heathen crowd—alas, bodies to be lamented—

[22586] and tremble at the raw example of our lot.

[22630] Hear, all of you: I cry afar and proclaim,

[22674] I send forth my voice the more loftily from the rack:
