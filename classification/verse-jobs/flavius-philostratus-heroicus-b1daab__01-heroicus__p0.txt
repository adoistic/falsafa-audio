CHAPTER: 01-heroicus



[0] 660. Vinedresser Are you an Ionian, stranger, or where are you from?

[70] Phoenician A Phoenician, vinedresser, from the country around Sidon and Tyre.

[149] Vinedresser Then why this Ionian dress?

[190] Phoenician The clothes are native to us now; Ionian luxury has taken hold of the whole of Phoenicia, and a man might be indicted there, I think, for not living softly.

[359] Vinedresser And where are you bound, walking with your head in the air and above everything at your feet?

[466] Phoenician I have need, vinedresser, of a token and an omen for a good voyage; for they tell me they will be launching us out into the Aegean itself, and the sea, I think, is dangerous and not easy to sail. So I am walking the road opposite to the wind, since for a fair voyage we Phoenicians watch for just such a sign.

[788] Vinedresser Clever you are, stranger, in matters of the sea; for it was you, I believe, who marked out the other Bear in the heavens and steer by her. But while your seamanship is praised, your trading is decried—you have the name of being grasping men and sharp dealers.

[1061] Phoenician And are you not grasping yourself, vinedresser, living among these vines and looking out, perhaps, for whoever will buy your fruit, paying you a drachma for the grapes,661. Phoenician and to whom you may sell your new wine or your vintage with its fine bouquet—the very wine, I suppose, you say you keep buried like Maron.

[1396] Vinedresser Stranger from Phoenicia, if there are Cyclopes somewhere on the earth, whom the land is said to feed in idleness, though they plant nothing and sow nothing, then what grows there might go unguarded—though it belongs to Demeter and Dionysus—and nothing would be sold off the land, but things would grow worthless and held in common, as in a market of swine. But where one must sow and plough and plant and do one task after another, devoted to the soil and obedient to the seasons, there one must also sell and buy. For farming too needs money; and without it you will feed neither ploughman nor vinedresser nor herdsman nor goatherd, and you will have no bowl to drink from or to pour libations with—for even the sweetest thing in farming, the gathering of the vintage, must be done for wages, or else the vines will stand wineless and idle, like figures in a painting. This, stranger, I have said about the whole round of a farmer's life; my own way is far more easygoing. For I deal with no merchants, and do not know what a drachma even is; I either buy or sell, an ox for grain or a goat for wine and the like for the like, after a few words spoken and heard.662. Phoenician A golden marketplace you describe, vinedresser, and one for heroes rather than men. But what does this dog want? He keeps circling me, fawning at my feet and offering his ear, soft and gentle.

[2781] Vinedresser He is telling you my character, stranger—that we treat those who come here so kindly and decently that we do not even allow the dog to bark at them, but to welcome them and crouch before them when they arrive.

[3004] Phoenician May I, then, set upon a vine?

[3046] Vinedresser There is no grudging it; we have grapes enough.

[3107] Phoenician What of the figs—may I pick them too?

[3157] Vinedresser That too is free; we have figs to spare. And I would give you nuts and apples and a thousand other good things; I plant them as a kind of side dish to the vines.

[3332] Phoenician Then what should I pay?

[3368] Vinedresser What else, Phoenician, but to eat with pleasure, take provisions, and go away rejoicing?

[3470] Phoenician But surely you are not a philosopher, vinedresser?

[3533] Vinedresser Yes, and along with the noble Protesilaus.

[3589] Phoenician What have you in common with Protesilaus—if you mean the one from Thessaly?

[3677] Vinedresser I mean the husband of Laodamia; for that is what he likes to hear.

[3757] Phoenician And what is he doing here?

[3796] Vinedresser He lives, and we farm together.

[3841] Phoenician Restored to life—or what?

[3879] Vinedresser He does not himself speak of what he has suffered, stranger, except that he died at Troy for Helen's sake and came back to life in Phthia, in love with Laodamia.

[4054] Phoenician And yet he is said to have died after coming back to life, having persuaded his wife to follow him.663. Vinedresser He says this himself; but how he ascended again after that, though I have long wished to learn it, he will not say, hiding, as he claims, some secret of the Fates. And his fellow soldiers, those in this Troy here, still appear in the plain, warlike in their bearing and shaking their crests.

[4474] Phoenician I disbelieve it, by Athena, vinedresser, though I wish it were so. But if you are not busy with your plants, and not channeling water, go through all this for me now—both these things and whatever you know of Protesilaus. For you would do the heroes a favor if I went away a believer.

[4771] Vinedresser The plants no longer drink at midday, stranger; it is autumn now, and the season waters them. So I have leisure to go through everything. For let not such great and divine matters escape men of refinement. And it is better to sit in a fair part of the place.

[5043] Phoenician Lead on, then; I will follow you even beyond the middle of Thrace.

[5122] Vinedresser Let us go into the vineyard, Phoenician; for there too you may find something to gladden you.

[5229] Phoenician Let us go; for there is a sweet breath that comes from the—

[5301] Vinedresser Sweet, do you call it? Divine! For of the wild trees the blossoms are fragrant, but of the cultivated, the fruits. And if ever you meet a cultivated plant with a fragrant flower, pluck rather its leaves, for the scent is in them.

[5544] Phoenician How varied is the season's beauty in your place, how cheerfully the clusters have come out, how all the trees flourish, and how like ambrosia is the fragrance of the spot! These walks you have laid out I think charming—but you seem to me to live in luxury, vinedresser, using so much land idly.

[5851] Vinedresser The walks are sacred, stranger; for the hero exercises in them.

[5928] Phoenician You shall tell me this when we have sat down where you are leading me. But now tell me this: do you farm this land as your own, or has it another master, while you, like the Oeneus of Euripides, tend one who is being tended?664. Vinedresser This one small plot is left to me out of many, and it feeds me not meanly; the other fields the powerful took from me when I was a complete orphan. And even this little plot, already held by Xeinis of the Chersonese, Protesilaus recovered for me by striking him with some apparition of his own, by which his sight was destroyed, so that he went away blind.

[6538] Phoenician A good guardian of your field you have got, then; and you fear, I suppose, no inroad of a wolf, with such a friend keeping watch.

[6680] Vinedresser You speak truly; for he allows no wild beast to come in, neither serpent here nor poisonous spider, nor does any informer attack us over the field. That beast is terribly shameless—it works its ruin in the marketplace.

[6912] Phoenician And your speech, vinedresser—how were you educated? For you do not seem to me to be one of the uneducated.

[7031] Vinedresser In the city, stranger, we first spent our life, employing teachers and studying philosophy. But my affairs went badly, for the farming was in the hands of slaves, and they brought us in nothing, so that I had to borrow on the land and go hungry. And so, coming here, I made Protesilaus my counselor; but he, having a just anger against me because I had left him and lived in the city, kept silent. When I persisted and said I would perish if I were neglected, he said: "Change your clothes." That day I heard this idly, but afterward, weighing it, I understood that he was bidding me change the whole pattern of my life. So I put on a leather jerkin, took up a mattock, and no longer even know the road to the city; and everything in my field flourishes for me. And if a sheep falls sick, or a beehive, or a tree, I use Protesilaus as my physician, being with him and devoted to the soil; and I grow wiser than myself, for he has wisdom too in abundance.

[7999] Phoenician Blessed are you in his company and in your field, if you gather there not only olives and grapes but also wisdom, divine and undefiled.665. Phoenician And perhaps I wrong the wisdom in you by calling you a vinedresser.

[8230] Vinedresser Call me so; for you would gratify Protesilaus by naming me farmer and gardener and the like.

[8336] Phoenician Is it here, then, vinedresser, that you keep company with one another?

[8419] Vinedresser Here, stranger. How did you guess it?

[8470] Phoenician Because this part of the field seems to me the sweetest and most divine. Whether anyone could be brought back to life here, I do not know, but he would live here most sweetly and free from grief, having left the crowd behind. For these trees are exceedingly tall, raised by time, and this water comes varied from springs, and you draw from it, I think, as if drinking now this and now that vintage of fine bouquet. And you plant arbors too, weaving the trees together and fitting them, so that no one could match such a garland even from an undefiled meadow.

[9041] Vinedresser And you have not yet heard the nightingales, stranger—how they sing in the Attic manner in this place, when evening comes and when day begins.

[9197] Phoenician I think I have heard them, and that they do not lament but only sing. But tell me the matters of the heroes; for I would more gladly hear these. You allow me, I suppose, to sit down?

[9392] Vinedresser The hero allows it, being kind and hospitable with these seats here.

[9474] Phoenician See, I rest; for the welcome is sweet to one who is about to hear a more serious tale.

[9573] Vinedresser Ask, stranger, whatever you wish, and you will not say you came in vain. For to Odysseus, when he was wandering far from his ship, Hermes—or one of the wise men sent by Hermes—came into fellowship of speech and earnest converse; this, I think, is what we should take the moly to mean. And you Protesilaus will fill with history through me, and make you happier and wiser. For to know many things is worth much.

[9997] Phoenician But I do not wander, my good man; rather, by God's will, by Athena, I have come here. For I now understand my dream.

[10126] Vinedresser And how does your dream stand? For you will be hinting at something divine.666. Phoenician I have been sailing from Egypt and Phoenicia now some thirty-five days, and when my ship put in here at Elaeus, I dreamed I was reading the verses of Homer in which he tells the catalogue of the Achaeans, and that I was summoning the Achaeans to embark on my ship as though it were large enough for them all together. And when I started up out of the dream—for a kind of shudder had come over me—I took it as meaning the slowness and length of the voyage, since visions of the dead are unprofitable to men in earnest. But wishing to use the dream as an omen—since the wind did not yet allow sailing—I turned aside here from the ship; and walking along, as you saw, I met you first, and we talk of Protesilaus, and shall talk also of the catalogue of the heroes—for so you say you will do. And the cataloguing of them onto the ship would be the gathering of the account about them and then the embarking.

[11134] Vinedresser By God's will you have truly come, stranger, and you interpret the vision soundly. Let us go through the account, then, lest you say I am being coy in keeping you from it.

[11319] Phoenician You understand, surely, what I long to learn. I wish to hear of your very fellowship with Protesilaus, what manner he comes in, and whether he knows anything that agrees with the poets, or that they have misunderstood, about the Trojan matters. By Trojan matters I mean such things as the mustering of the army at Aulis, and each of the heroes one by one—whether they were as handsome as they are sung, and brave, and wise. For the war that was fought over Troy, how could he describe it, since he neither fought it through nor lived, having died first of all the Greeks at the very landing, as they say?

[11936] Vinedresser This is naive of you, stranger. For to souls so divine and blessed the beginning of life is to be purified of the body. They know the gods, whose attendants they are, not then by worshipping images and conjectures, but by holding manifest fellowship with them; and they see the affairs of men, being free of disease and of the body, when indeed they are filled with prophetic wisdom and the oracular power runs riot within them.667. Vinedresser Whom, then, will you name of those who study Homer most rigorously as having read Homer's poems as Protesilaus has read them and sees through them? And yet, stranger, before Priam and Troy there was no recitation of epic, nor were things sung that had not yet been done; for poetry then concerned the oracles and the Heracles son of Alcmene, and was only just being established and not yet come of age. Homer was not yet singing; but some say he set himself to poetry when Troy was taken, others a few generations later, others eight. Yet Protesilaus knows all of Homer, and sings many Trojan events that happened after his own time, and many Greek and Median ones too; the expedition of Xerxes, at any rate, he calls the third destruction of men, after those that came in the time of Deucalion and of Phaethon, since most nations perished in it.

[13241] Phoenician You will fill the horn of Amaltheia, vinedresser, since your companion knows so much. For you will report it soundly and as you heard it.

[13391] Vinedresser By Zeus, yes—or I would wrong a hero who is a lover of wisdom and of truth, if I did not honor truth, which he is accustomed to call the mother of virtue.

[13559] Phoenician I think that at the very beginning of our talk I confessed my own affliction to you; for I say I am disposed to disbelieve marvels. And the reason is this: I have never been with anyone who saw them. One says he heard it from another, one supposes it, and a poet exalts the third. And the things said about the size of the heroes—that they were ten cubits tall—I think charming as mythology,668. Phoenician but false and unconvincing to one who measures them against nature, of which present-day men are the measure.

[14088] Vinedresser When did you begin to think these things unconvincing?

[14156] Phoenician Long ago, vinedresser, while still a youth. As a child I still believed such things, and my nurse used to tell me them charmingly, chanting them and even weeping over some of them; but when I became a young man, I thought I should not accept them without testing.

[14432] Vinedresser And the matter of Protesilaus, and that he appears here—did you ever happen to hear of it?

[14536] Phoenician How could I, vinedresser, when even hearing it from you today I disbelieve?

[14624] Vinedresser Then let the beginning of the account for you be the things you have long disbelieved. You say you disbelieve that men were ten cubits tall. When you have had enough of this, ask then for the account about Protesilaus and as much of the Trojan matters as you wish; for you will disbelieve none of them.

[14940] Phoenician Well said—let us do so.

[14976] Vinedresser Listen, then. I had a grandfather, stranger, who knew many of the things you disbelieve. He used to say that the tomb of Ajax was once destroyed by the sea against which it lies, and that bones appeared in it belonging to a man of eleven cubits; and he said the emperor Hadrian, coming to Troy, wrapped them up and fitted the present tomb around Ajax, and even embraced and kissed some of the bones.

[15389] Phoenician Not without reason, it seems, do I disbelieve such things, vinedresser; for you too say you heard something from your grandfather, and perhaps your mother or nurse, but report nothing of your own—unless you should speak of Protesilaus.

[15637] Vinedresser And indeed, if I were a teller of tales, I would describe the corpse of Orestes, which the Spartans found seven cubits long in Nemea,669. Vinedresser and the one in the bronze Lydian horse, which was buried in Lydia even before Gyges; when the earth split in an earthquake, it was seen as a marvel by the shepherds about Lydia, with whom Gyges then served as a hired man. For within the hollow horse, which had windows on each side, lay a corpse larger than a man would believe. And if these things are open to disbelief because of their age, yet to the things of our own time I do not know what you will say in objection. For Aryades—whom some called an Ethiopian, some an Indian—lying thirty cubits long in the land of the Assyrians, was lately revealed when the bank of the river Orontes split apart; and this Sigeum, less than fifty years ago, displayed on the projection of the headland the body of a giant whom Apollo himself says he slew as it fought against him on Troy's behalf. And I saw, stranger, when I sailed to Sigeum, both the affliction of the earth and the giant, how great he was. Many of the Hellespontians and Ionians sailed there too, and all the islanders and the whole Aeolian people; for two months the great body lay on the great headland, giving one account to one man and another to another, the oracle not yet making it clear.

[17006] Phoenician Could you then say something further, vinedresser, about his size and the joining of his bones, and about those serpents that the painters depict as grown together with the giants, which they sketch beneath Enceladus and those about him?

[17256] Vinedresser Whether those giants were monstrous, stranger, and compounded with beasts, I do not know; but the one at Sigeum measured twenty-two cubits, and lay in a rocky cavern, with his head toward the mainland,670. Vinedresser and his feet ending with the headland; and we saw no sign of dragons about him, nor was there anything in his bones that differed from a man's. And indeed Hymnaeus of Peparethus, being well disposed to me, sent one of his sons here four years ago to ask Protesilaus through me about a similar marvel. For on the island of Cos—which he owns alone—he happened to be digging up vines, and the earth gave back a hollow sound to the diggers, as if empty. So they opened it, and a corpse twelve cubits long lay there, and a serpent had its home in the skull. The young man came to ask us what he ought to do with it, and Protesilaus said, "Let us cover the stranger"—bidding him, I suppose, to bury the corpse and not to strip it bare willingly. He said too that it was a giant of those who had been struck down. And the one that appeared on Lemnos, which Menecrates of Steiria found, was very great; I saw it last year, sailing over from Imbros, for it was a short distance to Lemnos. The bones were no longer in order, for the vertebrae lay apart from one another, displaced, I suppose, by earthquakes, and the ribs were disjointed from the vertebrae; but considering them both together and one by one, the size seemed terrifying and not easy to picture. The skull, at any rate, when we poured wine into it, was not filled even by two Cretan amphoras. There is, toward the south wind, a headland of Imbros, Naulochos, beneath which lies a spring that turns the male animals into eunuchs and so intoxicates the females that they fall asleep. So a fragment of earth, broken off there, drew along with it the body of a very great giant; and if you disbelieve, let us sail there—for it still lies exposed, and the voyage to Naulochos is short.

[19223] Phoenician I would gladly go even beyond the Ocean, vinedresser, if I might find such a marvel anywhere,671. Phoenician but trade does not allow me to wander so far from itself; I must stay bound to the ship, like Odysseus, or else, as they say, both bow and stern will be lost.

[19503] Vinedresser But do not yet hold as credible, stranger, the things I have told you, until you sail to the island of Cos, where the bones of the earthborn are laid up—the first Meropes, they say—and until you see in Phrygia the bones of Hyllus son of Heracles, and, by Zeus, in Thessaly those of the Aloadae, that they were truly nine fathoms tall and just as they are sung. The Neapolitans who dwell in Italy have made a marvel of the bones of Alcyoneus; for they say that many of the giants were struck down there, and that Mount Vesuvius smolders over them. And in Pallene too, which the poets call Phlegra, the earth holds many such bodies of giants who encamped there, and many the rains and earthquakes uncover. No shepherd even dares to approach that place at midday, because of the phantoms that rumble there, which rave in it. To disbelieve such things was perhaps possible even in the case of Heracles; for which reason, having slain Geryon in Erytheia and being said to have met him as a creature of vast size, he dedicated the bones at Olympia, so that his labor might not be disbelieved.

[20603] Phoenician I count you blessed for your store of knowledge, vinedresser. I was ignorant of great things, and foolishly disbelieved. But how stand the matters of Protesilaus? For now, I suppose, is the time to come to those things that are no longer disbelieved.672. Vinedresser Hear of such things, stranger. Protesilaus does not lie in Troy, but here in the Chersonese, and a great mound holds him—that one, surely, on the left. These elms the nymphs planted about the mound, and they laid down this law for these trees: that the branches turned toward Ilium should bloom early but shed their leaves at once and perish before their season—this being the very fate of Protesilaus—while on the other side the trees should live and flourish. And whatever of the trees does not stand about the tomb—like these here in the garden—is strong in all its branches and thrives after its own kind.

[21492] Phoenician I see, vinedresser, and though I might marvel, I do not marvel; for the divine is wise.

[21592] Vinedresser And the sanctuary, in which the Mede committed his outrage in our fathers' time—on account of which they say the salt fish came back to life—take this, stranger, to be it; you see how little of it remains.673. Vinedresser But then, I suppose, it was charming and not small, as one may judge from the foundations. This statue here stands upon a ship—for the shape of the base is a prow—and he is set up as an admiral. Time has worn it down, and, by Zeus, those who anoint it and seal their prayers upon it have altered its appearance. But this is nothing to me; for I am with him myself and look upon himself, and no statue could be sweeter to me than he is.

[22263] Phoenician Will you then describe him to me and share his form?

[22328] Vinedresser Gladly, by Athena, stranger. He was about twenty years old when he sailed to Troy at that age, and he is in bloom with a soft down, and breathes a scent sweeter than the autumn myrtles. He has set a cheerful brow about his eye, for graciousness is dear to him. His glance, in earnest matters, is intense and vehement; but if we catch him at ease—ah, how charming and friendly his eyes appear! He has the right measure of fair hair, just enough to hang over his forehead rather than fall down it, and the line of his nose is square, as in a statue. His voice sounds clearer than trumpets, and that from a small mouth. To meet him naked is most pleasant, for he is well-knit and light, like the running Hermae. His height is perhaps ten cubits; and he would, I think, have shot up beyond even this, had he not died as a youth.674. Phoenician I have seen the young man, vinedresser, and I admire you for your companion. Is he armed, or how?

[23279] Vinedresser He has put on a cloak, stranger, in the Thessalian manner, like this statue. The cloak is sea-purple, of a divine bloom; for the bloom of the purple is beyond words.

[23458] Phoenician And his love for Laodamia, how does it stand with him now?

[23529] Vinedresser He loves, stranger, and is loved, and they are disposed toward one another like ardent newlyweds.

[23640] Phoenician Do you embrace him when he comes, or does he slip from you like smoke, as he does the poets?

[23745] Vinedresser He delights in being embraced, and allows me to kiss him and to take my fill of his neck.

[23848] Phoenician Does he come often, or at long intervals?

[23902] Vinedresser Four or five times a month, I think, I share his company, when he wishes either to plant some of these plants, or to gather the vintage, or to clip the flowers. For he loves garlands, and makes the flowers sweeter when he is among them.

[24152] Phoenician A cheerful hero you describe, and simply a bridegroom.

[24219] Vinedresser And a self-controlled one, stranger. For though fond of laughter from his youth, he does nothing in wantonness. He often takes up the mattock too, if I meet a rock while digging, and helps me with the hard tasks; and if I am ignorant of anything in farming, he corrects me. The trees—I, having half-heard Homer, used to plant tall, setting less of them into the ground than above—and when Protesilaus took me up on it and I used the words of Homer against him, he replied that Homer himself bids the opposite of what you are doing; for in his wisdom he knows deep things to be "long," 675. Vinedresser as somewhere he calls the wells "long," though they are deep. And he said the trees would take to the earth better if they stood by the greater part and were stirred by the lesser. And once, when he came upon me watering some flowers, he said, "My good man, the perfume needs no water"—teaching me, no doubt, not to wash out the flowers' scent.

[25179] Phoenician And the rest of the time, vinedresser, where does he live?

[25250] Vinedresser Sometimes in Hades, he says, sometimes in Phthia, and sometimes again in Troy, where his companions are; and after hunting boars and deer he comes here about midday and sleeps stretched out.

[25454] Phoenician And where does he keep company with Laodamia?

[25512] Vinedresser In Hades, stranger. And he says she fares most honorably among women, counted among those like Alcestis the wife of Admetus and Evadne the wife of Capaneus, and those equal to them in chastity and goodness.

[25732] Phoenician Do they eat together, or is it not lawful?

[25787] Vinedresser I have never yet met him eating, nor have I known him to drink. And yet I pour libations to him each evening from these Thasian vines, which he himself plants, and I set out ripe fruits at midday when summer comes and autumn is at hand; and when the moon is rounding to her circle in the springtime, I pour milk into this cooling-vessel and say, "Look, I offer you the season's stream; drink." Having said this, I withdraw, and the things are eaten and drunk faster than a man can close his eyes.

[26297] Phoenician And about the age at which he died, what does he say?

[26363] Vinedresser He pities, stranger, his own fate and the deity under whom he then was, and thinks it unjust and grudging that it did not allow him even to set foot in Troy; for he says he would have fallen short neither of Diomedes in fighting, nor of Patroclus, nor of the second Ajax;676. Vinedresser for he says he was left behind in war by the sons of Aeacus because of his age—he himself being a youth, while of them Achilles was a young man and Ajax a grown man. And he praises the verses Homer spoke about him, although he does not praise all of Homer—how he called his wife "with cheeks torn on both sides," his house "half-finished," and the ship in which he sailed "fought over," and called him "warlike." But he laments himself, that he accomplished nothing at Troy, but fell in a land on which he did not even set foot. And the scar is impressed upon his thigh, for he says the wound was wiped away together with his body.

[27296] Phoenician And how does he exercise, vinedresser? Since you said he practiced this too.

[27385] Vinedresser He exercises, stranger, in the things of war except archery, and in the gymnastic arts except wrestling; for shooting the bow he counts the mark of cowards, and wrestling of idlers.

[27580] Phoenician And how does he do the pancratium or box?

[27634] Vinedresser He exercises at these, stranger, against shadows, and throws the discus farther than a man could reach. For he drives the discus up beyond the clouds, and hurls it over a hundred cubits—and that, as you see, when it is twice the Olympic weight. And when he runs, you would find no trace, nor would his foot leave any mark on the earth.

[27983] Phoenician And yet great footprints are impressed in the walks, of the hero's ten-cubit size.677. Vinedresser Those footprints, stranger, are of him walking and exercising at something else; but when he runs, the earth is unmarked, for he is borne aloft, as it were riding the waves. He says too that Achilles outran the others at Aulis in the games, when Greece was training for Troy, and was lifted higher than his leap. The things of war he yields, as I said, to Achilles, except for the battle among the Mysians; for there he killed more Mysians than Achilles and carried off the prize of valor, and won also the contest over the shield.

[28626] Phoenician And what might the matter of the shield be, vinedresser? For it has not been told by any poet, nor does it enter into any of the Trojan accounts.

[28784] Vinedresser You will say this of many things, stranger; for the hero tells much about men and about deeds of war that is not yet clear to most. And the reason is this: he says that men, in their amazement at Homer's poems, looked only to Achilles and Odysseus and neglected good and noble men. Some they did not mention at all, while to others they dedicated a trireme of four verses. Achilles, he says, has been worthily hymned, but Odysseus excessively. And as for all that has been left out of Sthenelus and Palamedes and such men, I will go through it for you a little later, that you may not go away ignorant of any of these things. And the Mysian account, in which the shield occurs, we will presently complete; but since in mentioning the pancratium and boxing and the discus we were carried off to the shield, hear the hero's marvels in dealing with athletes who used him as a counselor. You have heard, I suppose, of the Cilician pancratiast whom our fathers called the Jumper, because he was small and far below his opponents.678. Phoenician I know him, judging surely from the statues; for he stands in bronze in many places.

[29922] Vinedresser This man, stranger, had skill in abundance, and spirit too in abundance, and the harmony of his body strengthened him greatly.
