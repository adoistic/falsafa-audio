[110832] 13.30. For then, often having gone two days, as has been said, without food, he thought fit to live on a little water and some wild herbs, and, sleeping, as it might be, a single hour, he would spend the rest of the time

[111054] 13.31. always walking about. And yet, if he had been willing to spend this very time on good deeds, affairs would have advanced to a great degree of prosperity.

[111216] 13.32. But as it was, using the strength of his nature for the ruin of the Romans, he was able to pull down their whole state to the ground. For he made it his work to be perpetually awake and to suffer hardship and toil for no other reason than to contrive day by day ever more pretentious disasters for his subjects.

[111536] 13.33. For he was, as has been said, exceedingly quick to conceive and swift to accomplish unholy deeds, so that even the good gifts of his nature were turned, in his case, to the harm of his subjects.

[111739] 14.1. For there was great untimeliness in affairs, and nothing of the customary things remained—of which, having mentioned a few, I must leave the rest all in silence, lest my account be endless.

[111936] 14.2. First, then, he neither himself had anything suited to the imperial dignity, nor did he think fit to preserve it, but in tongue and bearing and mind he played the barbarian.

[112117] 14.3. And whatever he wished to have written by his order, he did not entrust to the man holding the office of quaestor, as was the custom, to manage; but he himself thought fit to issue most of them—though his tongue was as it was—and a great crowd of bystanders did so too, so that those wronged thereby had no one to charge with it.

[112454] 14.4. And to those called secretaries the dignity of writing the emperor's secrets, for which from of old they had been appointed, was no longer reserved; but he himself wrote, so to speak, everything, and even when it was necessary to give directions to the arbitrators in the city as to how they should proceed in their decisions.

[112788] 14.5. For he allowed no one in the Roman empire to give his vote by his own independent judgment; but, willful with a senseless audacity, he himself regulated the verdicts that were to be, taking a hearsay account from one of the parties, and at once, without examination, set aside what had been decided, led by no law or justice,

[113121] 14.6. but plainly overcome by base love of gain. For the emperor was not ashamed to take bribes, his insatiable greed having stripped him of all shame.

[113274] 14.7. And often what had been approved by the senate and the sovereign ended in some other decision.

[113376] 14.8. For the senate sat as though in a picture, having authority neither over its vote nor over the good, gathered together only for the sake of form and an ancient law; since it was not even permitted to any of those assembled there to utter any sound at all, but the emperor and his consort, for the most part, pretended to take opposite sides in disputed matters, and what had been agreed between them on these matters prevailed.

[113811] 14.9. And if it seemed to anyone that, having broken the law, he was not secure in his victory, this man, paying out yet another piece of gold to this emperor, would at once procure a law that ran contrary to all those laid down before.

[114049] 14.10. And if some other man then sought after this very law that had been abolished, the sovereign by no means disdained to recall it and reinstate it. And nothing stood firm in the security of his power, but the balance of justice wandered, carried about everywhere, wherever the heavier weight of gold could draw it down; and it lay in the public part of the marketplace—and that issuing from the Palace—and there were set up for sale stalls not only of judicial but also of legislative power.

[114547] 14.11. And to those called referendaries it no longer sufficed to refer to the emperor the petitions of suppliants, and only to report to the magistrates, as was the custom, whatever should seem good to him concerning the suppliant; but gathering from all men the unjust plea, they deceived Justinian with tricks and certain misrepresentations—he being by nature subject to those who practice such things.

[114954] 14.12. And going out at once, and shutting up the opponents of those who had dealt with them, they exacted money, with no one defending the victims and with no possibility of refutation, as much as might be enough for them.

[115179] 14.13. And the soldiers who had the guard in the Palace, coming up to the arbitrators in the imperial portico, would bring in lawsuits with a violent hand.

[115336] 14.14. And all, so to speak, abandoning their own posts, then walked at will roads that had before been impassable and untrodden for them; and all affairs were borne along in disorder, not even sharing in any proper name, and the state was like a queen amid playing children.

[115613] 14.15. But the rest I must pass over, as I indicated at the beginning of this account. Yet it shall be told who was the man who first persuaded this emperor to take bribes in giving judgment.

[115806] 14.16. There was a certain Leon, a Cilician by birth, demonically zealous for money. This Leon became the mightiest of all flatterers, and the kind of man to plant his own opinion in the minds of the ignorant.

[116017] 14.17. For he had a certain persuasiveness that worked together with the tyrant's folly for the destruction of men. This man first persuaded Justinian to sell justice for money.

[116196] 14.18. And once this man had resolved to steal in the manner described, he never let up, but the evil, advancing on its way, went to a great height; and whoever was eager to win an unjust suit against one of the respectable men would at once go to Leon, and, agreeing that a certain portion of the disputed property should be deposited both for the tyrant and for himself, would at once depart from the Palace victorious, against right.

[116634] 14.19. And Leon was thus able to surround himself with very great wealth and became master of much land, but to the Romans he was most responsible for the state's coming to its knees.

[116819] 14.20. And for those who had business there was no security—no law, no oath, no writing, no penalty agreed upon, nothing else at all—except to pay money to Leon and the emperor.

[116998] 14.21. Nor indeed did even this remain firm in the resolve of Leon, but he thought fit to take pay from the adversaries too.

[117124] 14.22. For, stealing always on both sides, he never suspected it to be any shame to slight those who had put their trust in him and to go against them.

[117277] 14.23. For nothing seemed to him shameful, provided only that gain was attached, in playing a double game.

[117385] 15.1. So Justinian was such a man. As for Theodora, she had fixed her mind most constantly in the security of inhumanity.

[117508] 15.2. For she never did anything from being persuaded or compelled by another, but carried out what she had resolved, willful with all her power, no one daring to beg off the victim.

[117692] 15.3. For neither length of time, nor satiety of punishment, nor any device of supplication, nor the threat of death—since she was likely to fall from heaven upon the whole race—could persuade her to lay aside any of her wrath.

[117921] 15.4. And in short, no one ever saw Theodora reconciled with one who had offended her, not even when he had vanished from among men; but the son, inheriting the empress' hatred of his dead father like any other part of his inheritance, passed it on to the third generation.

[118196] 15.5. For her spirit was most ready to be stirred to the destruction of men, but unable to be appeased.

[118301] 15.6. She tended her body more than was needful, but less than her own desire.

[118381] 15.7. For very early she would go to the bath, and from there to her breakfast.

[118462] 15.8. And having breakfasted she would rest. But at her midday and evening meals she would set upon every kind of food and drink, and very long sleeps would ever lay hold of her—by day until the first part of night, by night until the rising of the sun.

[118717] 15.9. And though she had fallen into every track of intemperance to such a degree, she thought fit to administer the whole Roman empire within so small a portion of the day.

[118892] 15.10. And if the emperor charged anyone with some business without her consent, affairs came to such a turn of fortune for that man that not long after he was both stripped of his honor with great insult and destroyed by a most shameful death.

[119138] 15.11. For Justinian it was easy to do everything, not only because of his easiness of mind, but because he was for the most part sleepless, as has been said, and most accessible of all men.

[119330] 15.12. For much license was granted to men, even though they were utterly inglorious and obscure, not only to meet this tyrant, but even to confer with him and consort with him in secret.

[119519] 15.13. But to the empress not even one of the magistrates had access except with much time and labor; rather, all sat in attendance forever in a kind of slavish waiting, in a narrow and stifling chamber the whole time. For to be left out was an unbearable danger to any of the magistrates.

[119810] 15.14. And they stood continually on the tips of their toes, each straining to hold his face higher than his neighbor's, so that the eunuchs coming out from within might see him.

[119990] 15.15. And some of them were called, and that with difficulty and many days later; and going in to her with much fear, they would depart as quickly as possible, having only done obeisance and touched the edge of each foot with the tip of their lips.

[120241] 15.16. For to speak or to ask for anything, unless she bade it, was permitted to no one. For the state had come to servility, having her as its teacher of slavishness.

[120410] 15.17. And so the affairs of the Romans were being ruined, by the tyrant's apparent excess of simplicity,

[120517] 15.18. and by Theodora's harshness and exceeding peevishness. For in the simplicity was instability, and in the peevishness was the impossibility of getting anything done.

[120690] 15.19. In their mind and manner of life, then, the difference between them appeared; but common to them were avarice and the lust for murders and the speaking of truth to no one.

[120870] 15.20. For both were exceedingly apt at lying; and if anyone of those who had offended Theodora was said to have committed some small fault and one in no way worth mention, she would at once fabricate against the man charges that did not belong to him, and magnify the matter to a great evil.

[121164] 15.21. And a multitude of accusations would be heard, and there was a court for the overthrow of the established order, and judges were assembled, gathered by her, who were to contend with one another, which of them might be most able to please the empress, beyond the others, by his inhumanity in judgment, in accordance with her will.

[121502] 15.22. And thus she would at once have the victim's property entered in the public registers, and, having most bitterly tortured him—though perhaps he was of noble descent from of old—she by no means disdained to punish him with exile or death.

[121748] 15.23. But if it chanced that one of those she favored was convicted of unjust murders or of any other of the greatest crimes, she would deride and mock the eagerness of the accusers and compel them, unwilling, to keep silent about what had happened.

[122000] 15.24. And the most serious matters she would turn into farce whenever she pleased, as though she had made a business of it on a stage and in a theater.

[122154] 15.25. And once a certain patrician, an old man who had long held office—whose name, though I know it well, I shall by no means mention, lest I make my insult of him unending—being unable to recover from one of her servants a great sum of money owed him, came in to her, intending to accuse the man who had contracted with him and to beg her to help him to his just rights.

[122529] 15.26. But Theodora, learning of this beforehand, charged the eunuchs that, when the patrician should come to her, they should all encircle him and listen to her as she spoke, and she told them beforehand what they were to answer in response.

[122773] 15.27. And when the patrician came into the women's quarters, he did obeisance as he was accustomed to do obeisance to her, and, like one in tears, said: "Mistress, it is a hard thing for a man of patrician rank to be in need of money.

[123010] 15.28. For what brings to others pardon and pity, this in the case of this dignity is set apart for insult.

[123119] 15.29. For any other man whatever, in the utmost want, may by saying this very thing to his creditors at once be quit of the trouble from them; but a man of patrician rank, having no means to pay his creditors what he owes, would be most ashamed to say this; and saying it he could never persuade them that it is impossible for poverty to dwell together with this order.

[123491] 15.30. And if he should persuade them, it would befall him to have suffered the most shameful and grievous of all things.

[123614] 15.31. Well then, mistress, I have creditors—some who have lent me their own money, and some who have borrowed from me.

[123735] 15.32. And those who have lent, most constantly pressing me, I am unable, out of respect for my rank, to put off; but those who owe me, since they are not of patrician rank, resort to certain inhuman pretexts.

[123946] 15.33. I beseech you, therefore, and supplicate and beg you to help me to my just rights

[124036] 15.34. and to deliver me from my present troubles." So he spoke. And the woman answered in a chant, "Patrician So-and-so," and the chorus of eunuchs, taking it up, answered back,

[124216] 15.35. "You have a great hernia." And again, when the man had supplicated and made a speech like to what he had said before, in the same way the woman answered and the chorus answered back, until the wretched man, giving up, did obeisance as was his custom and went off home.

[124493] 15.36. For the greater part of the year she stayed in the suburbs by the sea, and especially in the place called Herion; and from this the great throng of her attendants were held by much hardship.

[124692] 15.37. For they were short of necessities and exposed to the dangers of the sea, especially if a storm, as it might be, fell upon them, or the whale appeared somewhere there.

[124868] 15.38. But they themselves thought the troubles of all other men no matter at all, provided only that it was open to them to live in luxury.

[125010] 15.39. But what manner of character Theodora showed toward those who had offended her, I shall now make clear, mentioning, of course, a few things, lest I should seem to labor without end.

[125200] 16.1. When Amalasuntha, wishing to be quit of her sojourn among the Goths, resolved to change her life and was planning to travel to Byzantium, as I have said in my earlier books, Theodora, reckoning that the woman was of noble birth and a queen, and exceedingly fair to look upon and most acute at devising whatever she wished, but holding her magnificence and especially her masculine bearing in suspicion, and at the same time fearing her husband's fickleness, conceived her jealousy on no small grounds, but resolved to entrap the woman to her death.

[125756] 16.2. So she at once persuaded her husband to send Peter alone as ambassador to Italy.

[125844] 16.3. As he set out, the emperor charged him with the things I have related in the appropriate books—where, for fear of the empress, it was impossible for me to make known the truth of what was done.

[126045] 16.4. But she charged him only this: that he should as quickly as possible make away with the woman, and to set him riding on the hope of great rewards if he should do what was bidden,

[126231] 16.5. once he had disposed of the woman. And he, when he came to Italy (for human nature does not know how to go reluctantly to unjust murder, when perhaps some office or great sums of money lie before it in hope), I do not know what exhortation he made to Theudatus, persuaded him to make away with Amalasuntha. And from this he advanced to the rank of master, and to the greatest power and, most of all men, to hatred.

[126653] 16.6. So the affair of Amalasuntha ended in this.

[126704] 16.7. Now Justinian had a secretary, Priscus by name, exceedingly wicked and a Paphlagonian and fitted to please his patron in character, but very well-disposed toward him and supposing that he met with like treatment from him; for which reason he very quickly became master of great wealth unjustly.

[127006] 16.8. This man, as one who put on airs and tried to set himself against her, Theodora slandered to her husband.

[127119] 16.9. And at first she accomplished nothing; but not long after she herself, putting the man aboard ship in the middle of winter, sent him wherever she pleased, and, shaving him, compelled him to become a priest against his will.

[127350] 16.10. And he, pretending that he knew nothing of what was being done, neither searched out where in the world Priscus was, nor kept him thereafter in memory, but sat in silence as though seized with lethargy; the man's money, however, of which a little had been left him,

[127624] 16.11. she plundered entirely. And when a suspicion fell on her of being love-stricken for one of her servants, Areobindus by name, a barbarian by birth, but comely and a young man, whom she had appointed her steward, wishing to clear herself of the charge—although, they say, she was demonically in love with the man—she resolved for the present to torture him most bitterly for no cause; but as for the rest, we have learned nothing concerning him, nor has anyone seen him to this day.

[128113] 16.12. For if she wished to conceal any of her doings, this remained unspoken and unremembered by all, and it was no longer permitted either for one who knew to report it to any of his intimates, or for one who wished to learn to inquire, however curious he might be.

[128382] 16.13. For no such fear of any tyrant has there been since men have existed, since it was not even possible for any of those who had offended her to escape notice.

[128547] 16.14. For a multitude of spies reported to her what was said and done both in the marketplace and in the houses.

[128662] 16.15. So whenever she did not wish the punishment of a victim to become known, she did the following.

[128766] 16.16. Summoning the man, if he happened to be one of distinction, she alone, handing him over to one only of her servants, would charge him to convey him to the farthest bounds of the Roman empire.

[128966] 16.17. And he, at an untimely hour of the night, putting the man, veiled and bound, aboard ship, and going with him to the place where he had been ordered by the woman, would there hand him over most secretly to one well able for this service, charging him to guard the man most securely and forbidding him to tell anyone, until either the empress should take pity on the wretch, or he should, after a long time, in a lingering death by the hardship there, waste away and die; and then he would depart.

[129470] 16.18. And a certain Basianus, a Green, a young man of no obscurity, who had reviled her, she held in anger. Therefore Basianus (for he was not unaware of this anger) fled to the church of the Archangel.

[129675] 16.19. But she at once set upon him the authority over the populace, charging him not at all with the reviling, but bringing against him the charge of pederasty.

[129838] 16.20. And the authority, raising the man from the sanctuary, tortured him with an unbearable punishment; and the whole populace, when they saw a body that was free-born and reared in indulgent living from the first in such calamities, were at once stung with grief at the suffering, and with wailing cried out as loud as heaven, begging off the young man.

[130196] 16.21. But she punished him still more, and cutting off his genitals destroyed him without refutation, and entered his property in the public registers.

[130350] 16.22. So whenever this little woman was in anger, neither was any sanctuary secure, nor the prohibition of any law, nor the intercession of a city, sufficient to rescue the victim, nor did anything else of all things avail her.

[130580] 16.23. And holding a certain Diogenes in anger as being a Green—a man of wit and beloved by all and by the emperor himself—she was no less eager to bring a false charge of unnatural intercourse against him.

[130788] 16.24. So persuading two of his servants, she set them up as accusers and witnesses against their master.

[130895] 16.25. But since he was examined not secretly and most privily, as was the custom, but in public, with many judges chosen, and not undistinguished, on account of Diogenes' reputation—when the words of the servants did not seem, to the judges who weighed things exactly, sufficient for the verdict, especially as they were mere boys, she shut up Theodore, one of Diogenes' intimates, in the customary little chambers.

[131313] 16.26. There she went about the man with many flatteries and many tortures. And when nothing advanced for her, she ordered them to wind a cord of oxhide around the man's head about the ears and to twist and tighten the cord.

[131539] 16.27. And Theodore supposed that his eyes had leaped out, leaving their proper place; yet he resolved to fabricate nothing of what had not happened.

[131690] 16.28. Therefore the judges acquitted Diogenes, the suit being unsupported by witnesses, and the city held a festival on this account with all the people.

[131846] 17.1. But this turned out thus. And it was told at the beginning of this account what things she did to Belisarius and Photius and Bouzes.

[131986] 17.2. Two partisans of the Blues, Cilicians by birth, setting upon Callinicus, the governor of Cilicia Secunda, with much uproar, began to lay unjust hands upon him, and killed his groom, who stood close by and tried to defend his master, with the governor and the whole populace looking on.

[132279] 17.3. And he put to death by law the partisans, who were convicted both of many other crimes and of this murder; but she, learning of it, and to show that she espoused the cause of the Blues, while he still held the office impaled him on the tomb of the murderers for no reason.

[132559] 17.4. And the emperor, pretending to weep and lament the dead man, sat grunting, and though he made many threats against those who had assisted in the deed, he did nothing; the money of the dead man, however, he by no means disdained to plunder.

[132806] 17.5. But Theodora was also at pains to devise punishments for sins against the body. At any rate, gathering up more than five hundred prostitutes in the middle of the marketplace, who plied their trade for three obols—just enough to live on—and sending them across to the opposite shore, she shut them up in the monastery called Repentance, compelling them to change their way of life.

[133194] 17.6. Some of these threw themselves down from a height by night, and thus were released from the unwilling change.

[133311] 17.7. There were two girls in Byzantium, sisters, born not only of a father and three generations of consuls, but from of old of the first blood in the senate.

[133472] 17.8. These, having already come to marriage, it befell to become widows on the death of their husbands. At once Theodora, selecting two common and loathsome men, was eager to wed them to these sisters, charging that they were not living chastely.

[133721] 17.9. And fearing lest this should happen, they fled to the sanctuary of Sophia, and coming to the divine font held fast with all their might to the basin there.

[133884] 17.10. But the empress brought upon them such necessity and hardship that, wishing to be quit of the troubles there, they were eager to exchange these for their marriage. So no place remained for her undefiled or inviolate.

[134109] 17.11. These, then, were wedded against their will to beggarly and outcast men far below their own rank, although there were noble suitors at hand for them.

[134267] 17.12. And their mother, who had herself become a widow, daring neither to wail nor

[134352] 17.13. to weep aloud at the calamity, was present at the betrothal. But afterward Theodora, expiating the pollution, resolved to console them with public misfortunes.

[134520] 17.14. For she appointed each of the men to office. And to the girls there came no comfort even so, but irreparable and unbearable sufferings were inflicted by these men upon nearly all the subjects, which will be told in my later books.

[134759] 17.15. For to Theodora neither the dignity of office nor of the state nor anything else was a care, provided only that her will be accomplished.

[134905] 17.16. Now she happened to have conceived by one of her lovers, while she was still on the stage; and perceiving the misfortune late, she did everything, as was her custom, to procure abortion, but by no device could she destroy the untimely child, since it was not far from having become of human form.

[135210] 17.17. Therefore, since nothing advanced, she let the attempt go and was compelled to bear it. And the father of the child, seeing her at a loss and vexed, because, having become a mother, she would no longer be able to work with her body as before, and perceiving truly that she would make away with the infant, took it up and, naming it John, since it was male, departed for Arabia, whither he was bound.

[135618] 17.18. And when he was about to die, and John was now a youth, the father told him the whole story concerning his mother.

[135741] 17.19. And he, when his father had vanished from among men, having performed all the customary rites for him, after some time came to Byzantium and announced the matter to those who always made the approaches to his mother.

[135966] 17.20. And they, supposing that she would reckon nothing in a human manner, reported to the mother that her son John had come.

[136094] 17.21. And the woman, fearing lest the report should become known to her husband, bade the boy come into her sight.

[136211] 17.22. And when she saw him present, she handed him over to one of her servants,

[136293] 17.23. to whom she was wont always to commit such matters. And in what manner the wretch vanished from among men I cannot say, but no one has been able to see him to this day, not even after the empress' death.

[136505] 17.24. At that time it befell nearly all women too to be corrupted in character. For they sinned against their husbands with all license, the deed bringing them no danger or harm, since even those caught in adultery remained unscathed; and going at once to the empress, and turning the tables, and bringing a countersuit on charges that had never occurred, they would haul their husbands to court.

[136904] 17.25. And it remained for the men, though unconvicted, to repay the dowry double, and, having been scourged, to be carried off for the most part to prison, and again to look on at their adulterous wives decked out and being lechered by their lovers more fearlessly. And many of the adulterers even obtained honor from this very deed.

[137240] 17.26. Therefore most men thereafter, suffering unholy treatment from their wives, most gladly kept silent, unscourged, granting them their license by seeming not to have detected them.

[137427] 17.27. She thought fit to govern all the affairs of the state by her own judgment. For she appointed the offices and the priesthoods, examining and guarding most carefully against this one thing only: that the man seeking the dignity should not be honorable or good, and so unable to serve her in what she commanded.

[137745] 17.28. And she administered all marriages by a certain divine authority, and people no longer made any voluntary betrothal before marrying.

[137886] 17.29. For a wife suddenly came to each man, not because she pleased him, as is the custom even among the barbarians, but because it was Theodora's pleasure.

[138045] 17.30. And this same thing befell the women who were married to suffer in turn. For they were compelled, by no means willing, to live with their husbands.

[138201] 17.31. And often, taking the bride down from the bridal chamber for no reason, she would dismiss the bridegroom unwed, saying only, with irritation, that the woman displeased her.

[138382] 17.32. This she did to many others and to Leon, who held the office of referendary, and to Saturninus, the son of Hermogenes who had been master. For this Saturninus had a cousin betrothed to him, a maiden, free-born and respectable, whom her father Cyrillus had betrothed to him after Hermogenes had departed from life.

[138704] 17.33. And when the bridal chamber had been prepared for them, she shut up the bridegroom, and he was led to the other bridal chamber, and married, wailing and lamenting, the daughter of Chrysomallo.

[138905] 17.34. This Chrysomallo had long ago been a dancing-girl and again a courtesan, but at that time, with another Chrysomallo and Indaro, she had her dwelling in the Palace.

[139077] 17.35. For instead of the phallus and the life in the theater, they now managed affairs here.

[139172] 17.36. And Saturninus, having lain with the bride and found her deflowered, disclosed to one of his intimates that he had married no virgin.

[139314] 17.37. And when this came to Theodora, she ordered her servants, since he was giving himself airs and being puffed up about nothing that concerned him, to toss him aloft, like children who go to a schoolmaster, and, scourging him many times across the back, she forbade him to be a chatterer.

[139608] 17.38. What things she did to John the Cappadocian have been told in my earlier books. These she contrived in anger against the man—not for the wrongs he was doing the state (and here is the proof: of those who later did more dreadful things to the subjects, she did this to none), but because in other respects he dared to go openly against her, and slandered her to the emperor, so that she came near to being set at war with her own husband.

[140054] 17.39. For of the causes, as I said, it is here most necessary for me to state the truest.

[140146] 17.40. And when she had shut him up in Egypt, having suffered what I have already disclosed concerning him, even so she got no satiety of the man's punishment, but never ceased searching out false witnesses against him.

[140367] 17.41. And four years later she was able to find two Greens of the partisans in Cyzicus, who were said to be among those who had risen against the bishop.

[140523] 17.42. And she went about them with flatteries and arguments and threats, so that one of them, terrified and lifted up by hopes, referred the pollution of the murder to John.

[140699] 17.43. But the other by no means resolved to go against the truth, although he was so racked by the torture that he was likely very soon to die.

But when, the next year, the yield of the crops was no longer equally abundant, the grain-bearing fleet came to Byzantium more scantily than the need required; and Peter, at a loss in his straits, thought fit to buy a great quantity of grain from the regions in Bithynia and Phrygia and Thrace.

[172184] 22.18. And it was necessary for those dwelling there to carry the loads to the sea with much labor, and to bring them to Byzantium with danger, and to be paid a small price, nominally, by him, while the loss to them came to such magnitude that they were glad if anyone allowed them to make a gift of the grain to the public household and to pay out yet another sum for it.

[172558] 22.19. This is the burden which they are wont to call "compulsory purchase." And since not even so was there enough grain in Byzantium for the need, many made complaint of the matter to the emperor.

[172758] 22.20. And at the same time the soldiers too, nearly all of them, since they had not received their accustomed pay, were held by uproar and much disturbance throughout the city.

[172937] 22.21. The emperor, then, both came to seem angry with him, and wished to relieve him of the office, both for these reasons that have been stated, and because he had heard that demonically great sums of money had been hidden away by him,

[173176] 22.22. which he chanced to have stolen from the public treasury. And so it was. But Theodora would not allow her husband. For she loved Barsymes extravagantly, it seems to me, on account both of his wickedness and of his harming the subjects so signally.

[173432] 22.23. For she herself was most cruel and utterly full of inhumanity, and thought fit that her servants should be in their character as suited to her as possible.

[173596] 22.24. And they say that she became unwillingly well-disposed to him because she had been bewitched by Peter.

[173707] 22.25. For this Barsymes was overcuriously zealous about sorcerers and demonic things, and admired the so-called Manichaeans and by no means disdained to be their patron openly.

[173886] 22.26. And yet, even hearing this, the empress did not let go her goodwill toward the man, but rather resolved on this account to shelter and cherish him still more.

[174053] 22.27. For she too, having consorted with magicians and sorcerers from childhood, since her pursuits led her to this, lived believing in this matter and ever taking courage from it.

[174236] 22.28. And they say that she made Justinian pliant not so much by flattering him as by the constraint from the demons.

[174356] 22.29. For this man was not so good-hearted or just or steadfast toward the good as ever to be superior to such a plot, but he was plainly a slave to the love of murders and of money, and not hard for those who deceived and flattered him to lead.

[174604] 22.30. And even in the actions most zealously pursued by him he would change for no reason and was most like dust.

[174720] 22.31. For this reason none even of his kinsmen or otherwise his acquaintances ever had any secure hope in him, but he was forever shifting his mind toward his pursuits.

[174891] 22.32. So, being thus easy of access to the sorcerers, as has been said, and brought under Theodora's hand with no trouble, the empress especially cherished Peter beyond measure, as one zealous in such matters.

[175103] 22.33. So the emperor barely relieved him of the office which he had formerly held, but, at Theodora's insistence, not long after appointed him commander of the treasuries, relieving John of this honor—who chanced to have taken it up a few months before.

[175359] 22.34. Now this John was a Palestinian by birth, gentle and exceedingly good, and neither knew how to provide himself resources of unjust money, nor ever harmed any of all men.

[175537] 22.35. At any rate the whole people loved him especially. For this very reason he in no way pleased Justinian and his consort, who, whenever they saw among those who served them anyone honorable and good beyond their expectation, dizzy and exceedingly vexed, were eager by every device to thrust him out as quickly as possible.

[175866] 22.36. So, then, Peter, succeeding this John too, presided over the royal treasuries and again became most responsible to all for great calamities.

[176015] 22.37. For, cutting off the greater part of the money which, by way of solace, was ordained of old to be furnished each year to many by the emperor, he himself, improperly, grew rich on the public funds and brought a portion of them to the emperor.

[176265] 22.38. And those deprived of the money sat round in great mourning, since he thought fit to issue the gold coin not as before, but having made it lighter—a thing never done before.

[176447] 22.39. So matters concerning the magistrates stood thus for the emperor. But how he everywhere destroyed the owners of the lands I now proceed to tell.

[176600] 22.40. It would suffice for us, having mentioned a little before the magistrates sent to all the cities, to indicate the sufferings of these people too. For these magistrates first, by force, plundered the owners of the lands; and even so, all the rest shall be told.

[176869] 23.1. First, whereas it was the custom from of old for each holder of the Roman empire not once only but often to grant to all his subjects remission of the remnants of public debts, so that neither those in want, who had no means at all of paying these remnants, might be continually choked, nor the tax-collectors might have pretexts to bring false charges against those of the taxpayers who owed nothing—this man, in two-and-thirty years' time, did nothing of the kind for his subjects.

[177360] 23.2. And from this it was necessary for those in want to flee and never return to anyone.

[177452] 23.3. And the informers wore down the more respectable men, brandishing accusation against them as having from of old paid the tax more scantily than the levy laid upon the land.

[177632] 23.4. For the wretches feared not only the new exaction of the tax, but also being burdened for so great a length of time with taxes that did not concern them at all.

[177800] 23.5. At any rate many, surrendering their own property to the informers or to the public treasury, departed.

[177911] 23.6. Then, when the Medes and Saracens had plundered the greater part of the land of Asia, and the Huns and Sclaveni and Antae the whole of Europe, and had razed some of the cities to the ground, and most exactly extorted money from others, and enslaved the people with all their goods, and made every region empty of its inhabitants by their daily inroads, he remitted the tax to none of all, except only a year's tax to the cities that had been captured.

[178370] 23.7. And yet if, like the emperor Anastasius, he had resolved to grant the captured cities seven years' remission of taxes, I think that even so he would not have done what was needful, since Cabades departed having done the buildings hardly any harm, but Chosroes burned everything and razed it to the ground, and inflicted greater sufferings on those who fell in his way.

[178746] 23.8. And to these men, to whom he remitted this ridiculous part of the tax, and to all the others, who had often received the Median army, while the Huns and Saracens continually plundered the eastern land, and the barbarians of Europe no less ever did these things, day by day, to the Romans there—this emperor at once became harsher than all the barbarians.

[179108] 23.9. For by compulsory purchases and by the so-called impositions and assessments the owners of the lands were ruined the very moment the enemy had withdrawn.

[179269] 23.10. What these names are and mean I shall make clear.

[179327] 23.11. They compel the owners of the lands to maintain the Roman army according to the measure of the levy laid upon each, the prices paid being not as the present need allows, but as is permitted and fixed, and not inquiring whether the necessities happen to be available to them in their region.

[179626] 23.12. And it has come about that these wretched men are under necessity to bring in the provisions for soldiers and horses, buying all these at much higher prices, and that too from a region, as it might be, lying far from the place where the camp happens to be, and to measure them out to the suppliers of the soldiers not, as is the law for all men,

[179980] 23.13. but as those men wish. And this is the matter which is called "compulsory purchase," from which it befalls all the owners of the lands to be enervated.

[180140] 23.14. For from it they are under necessity to pay a yearly tax not less than tenfold, since to them it befell not only, as has been said, to furnish supplies to the army, but also often to convey grain to Byzantium—men who had suffered these things. For not only the so-called Barsymes dared to commit this abomination, but before him the Cappadocian, and after Barsymes those who took over this dignity of office.

[180557] 23.15. Such, then, is the matter of compulsory purchase. But the name of imposition is a kind of ruin, unforeseen, suddenly coming upon the owners of the lands and rooting out from them the hope of their livelihood.

[180774] 23.16. For when the tax of lands that have become deserted and impoverished—whose owners and farmers it has already befallen either to perish utterly, or, abandoning the land of their fathers, to hide on account of the evils pressing upon them—they do not disdain to lay upon those not yet entirely ruined.

[181082] 23.17. Such is the name of imposition too, which, as was likely, became prevalent especially in this time. As for the assessments, having stated them most briefly, I shall be quit of them, and they stand thus.

[181293] 23.18. It was necessary, especially in these times, for the cities to be involved in many penalties; the occasions and modes of which I forbear to state at present, lest my account be endless.

[181487] 23.19. These the owners of the lands paid off, depositing a price according to the measure of the levy laid upon each.

[181607] 23.20. But the evil did not stop with these things for them; rather, when the plague encompassed the whole inhabited world, and not least the Roman empire, and made away with the greater part of the farmers, and from this the lands had, as was likely, become deserted, he used no mercy toward their owners.

[181915] 23.21. For he never ceased exacting the yearly tax, not only as he had laid it upon each, but also the share of the neighbors who had perished.

[182060] 23.22. And besides them there were all the other things I mentioned just now, ever pressing upon those who had been unfortunate in the possession of their lands; and besides, they were to serve the soldiers, who were lodged in the finest and most precious of their chambers, while they themselves, all this time, had their dwelling in the meanest and most neglected of their little houses.

[182451] 23.23. All these things it befell men to suffer continually under the reign of Justinian and Theodora, since in this time neither war nor any other of the greatest evils chanced to abate.

[182640] 23.24. And since we have mentioned dwellings, neither must we pass over this, that the owners of the houses in Byzantium, providing lodging there for the barbarians, who were about seventy thousand, not only could in no way profit from their own property, but had other troubles inflicted on them besides.

[182947] 24.1. Nor indeed must the things he did to the soldiers be passed over in silence, over whom he set the most wicked of all men, bidding them gather as much money as possible from this source, knowing well that a twelfth part of what was raised would be set aside for them.

[183221] 24.2. And he gave them the name accountants. And these contrived the following each year. The soldiers' pay is by law furnished not equally to all alike, but to those still young and lately enlisted the allowance is smaller, while to those who have toiled and are now about midway in the register it advances to a greater amount.

[183552] 24.3. To those, however, who have grown old and are about to be discharged from service, the allowance is far more ample, so that they themselves, living thereafter privately, may have enough to subsist on, and, whenever it befalls them to measure out their life, may be able to leave some solace of their property to those of their household.

[183897] 24.4. So time, ever raising the lower soldiers to the grades of those who have died or been discharged from service, apportions to each, by seniority, the pay from the public treasury.

[184083] 24.5. But the so-called accountants would not allow the names of the dead to be removed from the registers, although they were destroyed in great numbers, especially in the frequent wars; nor indeed did they any longer fill up the registers, and that for a long time.

[184352] 24.6. And from this it came about that the number of those in service was ever more deficient for the state, and that the surviving soldiers, thrust by those long since dead into a lower grade than their due, were left behind, and received their pay at a lower rank than was fitting, while the accountants apportioned to Justinian the soldiers' money all this time.

[184719] 24.7. And besides, they wore down the soldiers with many other forms of penalty, as though in requital for their dangers in the wars, charging some that they were Greeks—as though it were not permitted for any of those from Greece ever to be brave—and others that they were serving without the emperor's command, though they showed letters of the emperor about this, which the accountants dared to impugn with no hesitation; and others, that some days had been left them away from their comrades.

[185217] 24.8. Later, some of the guards in the Palace too, being sent throughout the whole Roman empire, searched, nominally, in the registers for those least fit for service, and dared to strip the belts from some of them as useless or grown old, who thereafter, begging their food from the pious in the public part of the marketplace, became to all who met them ever occasions of tears and lamentation; while from the rest, lest they too suffer the same, they exacted great sums of money, so that it befell that the soldiers, enervated in many ways, became poorer than all and were in no way eager to make war.

[185823] 24.9. Whence it befell the Romans that their affairs in Italy too were ruined. There Alexander the accountant, being sent, dared with no hesitation to bring these charges against the soldiers, while from the Italians he exacted money, saying that he was punishing what they had done in their dealings with Theoderic and the Goths.

[186155] 24.10. And not only the soldiers were oppressed by poverty and want at the hands of the accountants, but also those who served all the generals, who were very numerous and formerly of great repute, were afflicted with hunger and dire poverty.

[186399] 24.11. For they had no means from which to provide themselves the accustomed things.

[186485] 24.12. And I shall add yet one other thing to these, since my account of the soldiers leads me to it. The emperors of the Romans in former times established everywhere on the borders of the state a very great number of soldiers for the guarding of the frontiers of the Roman empire, especially in the eastern part, checking thereby the inroads of the Persians and Saracens,

[186860] 24.13. whom they called the limitanei. These the emperor Justinian at first treated so carelessly and shabbily that their paymasters were four or five years in arrears with their pay; and whenever peace came about between the Romans and Persians, these wretched men were compelled, as though they too were to enjoy the benefits of the peace, to make a present to the public treasury of the pay owed them for a stated period; and later he took from them even the very name of military service for no reason.

[187368] 24.14. Thereafter the borders of the Roman empire remained without garrisons, and the soldiers suddenly looked to the hands of those who were wont to be pious.

[187529] 24.15. Other soldiers, not fewer than three thousand five hundred, were from the beginning established for the guarding of the Palace, whom they call scholarii.

[187691] 24.16. And to these the public treasury was ever wont to furnish more pay than to all the others. These the former emperors selected by merit from the Armenians and brought to this honor.

[187880] 24.17. But from the time Zeno took over the kingship, license was given to all, even men utterly unmanly and unwarlike, to lay claim to this title.

[188029] 24.18. And as time went on, they bought this service even by depositing slaves as the price. So when Justin took over the kingship, this Justinian appointed many to this honor, gathering great sums of money.

[188238] 24.19. And when he perceived that there was no longer any vacancy in these registers, he inserted yet another two thousand, whom they called supernumeraries.

[188397] 24.20. But when he himself held the kingship, he at once shook off these supernumeraries, paying them back not one thing of their money.

[188535] 24.21. As for those within the number of the scholarii, he contrived the following. Whenever it was likely that an army would be sent to Libya or Italy or against the Persians, he would charge them to make ready, as though they would campaign with it, though he knew well that they were least fit for service; and they, fearing lest this should happen, would for a stated period give up their pay. This the scholarii often befell to suffer.

[188977] 24.22. And Peter, all the time he held the office of the so-called master, ever, day by day, wore them down with unspeakable thefts.

[189111] 24.23. For he was gentle and least of all knew how to commit outrage, but he was the most thievish of all men and utterly full of shameful filth. This Peter I mentioned in my earlier books also, as having wrought the murder of Amalasuntha, the daughter of Theoderic.

[189379] 24.24. There are also others in the Palace much more worthy, since the public treasury is wont to furnish them more pay too, inasmuch as they deposit greater prices for the title of service, who are called domestics and protectors and are from the beginning unpracticed in the works of war.

[189671] 24.25. For only for the sake of rank and appearance are they wont to be enrolled in the Palace. And some of them are settled in Byzantium, others, from of old, in Galatia and other regions.

[189862] 24.26. But these too Justinian, ever frightening them in the manner described, compelled to give up the pay that belonged to them.

[189994] 24.27. In summary it shall be told thus. It was the law that every five years the emperor should present each of the soldiers with a fixed sum of gold.

[190147] 24.28. And sending every five years everywhere in the Roman empire, they furnished five gold staters to each soldier.

[190266] 24.29. And it was by no means possible for this not to be done always by every means. But from the time this man administered the state, he did nothing of the kind, nor intended to, though now thirty-two years' time has been worn away, so that some forgetfulness of this practice came upon men.

[190562] 24.30. I proceed to tell yet another mode of his plundering of the subjects. Those who, for the emperor and the offices in Byzantium, either bear arms or handle documents or render any other service, are at the beginning placed last in the registers, but as time goes on, ever rising into the place of those who die or withdraw, each advances in his own rank to this point, until a man, having mounted the first grade, comes at last to the term of his honor.

[191022] 24.31. And to those who have come to this dignity such great sums of money have been ordained of old that more than a hundred centenaria of gold each year were gathered for these men, and they were cared for in their old age, and many others for the most part shared in the benefit with them from this source, and the affairs of the state ever advanced thereby to great prosperity.

[191405] 24.32. But this emperor, depriving them of nearly all these things, brought evils both upon them and upon other men. For poverty, fastening on them first, then made its way through the others too, who had formerly had some share in the benefit.

[191651] 24.33. And if anyone should reckon up the loss that befell them from this source over the two-and-thirty years, he will find the measure of what it befell them to be deprived of.

[191831] 25.1. Thus did this tyrant treat the soldiers. But what he did to merchants and sailors and craftsmen and traders, and through them to all the rest, I now proceed to tell.

[192004] 25.2. There are two straits, one on either side of Byzantium: one at the Hellespont, between Sestos and Abydos; the other at the mouth of the so-called Euxine Sea, where the place called the Sanctuary is.

[192210] 25.3. Now in the strait of the Hellespont there was no customhouse in the public service, but a certain official, sent by the emperor, sat at Abydos, examining whether any ship bearing arms was going to Byzantium without the emperor's leave, and whether anyone was setting out from Byzantium without carrying the documents and seals of the men on whom this office is laid (for it is not lawful for anyone to set out from Byzantium without having been released by the men who serve in the office of the so-called master); and he exacted from the owners of ships a toll that gave no offense, but the man holding this office thought fit to receive it as a kind of wage for this work.

[192892] 25.4. The man sent to the other strait, however, ever received his wage from the emperor, and examined exactly both these things which I have mentioned, and whether anything was being conveyed to the barbarians settled along the Euxine Sea, which it is not lawful to convey from Roman soil to the enemy. But it was not permitted to this man to take anything from those who sailed there.

[193280] 25.5. But from the time Justinian took over the kingship, he established a public customhouse in each strait, and, sending two salaried officials there always, gave them the agreed wage, but charged them to bring him from there as much money as possible by every means.

[193551] 25.6. And they, eager to show nothing but their goodwill toward him, departed having plundered absolutely all the prices of the goods from those who sailed.

[193709] 25.7. So in each strait he did these things; but in Byzantium he contrived the following. He set up one of his intimates, a Syrian by birth, Addaeus by name, whom he charged to procure him some profit from the ships that put in there.

[193945] 25.8. And this man no longer let go from there any of the ships that put in at the harbor of Byzantium, but either penalized the shipmasters by the prices of their own vessels, or compelled them to carry cargoes to Libya and

[194171] 25.9. Italy. And some of them no longer wished either to take on cargo or to work the sea, but, burning their own ships, gladly departed at once.

[194318] 25.10. Those, however, who were under necessity to make their living from this trade, took triple wages thereafter from the merchants and carried cargo; and it came about for the merchants that they must make good this loss for themselves at the expense of those who bought the goods; and thus it befell that the Romans were starved to death by every device.

[194678] 25.11. So these things went on in the state. But what he did to the coinage, too, I think I must not pass over.

[194791] 25.12. For whereas the money-changers were formerly wont to give out, for one gold stater, two hundred and ten obols, which they call folleis, to those who dealt with them, they themselves, contriving private gains, ordained that only a hundred and eighty obols should be given for the stater. In this way they cut off a sixth part of each gold coin from all men.

[195156] 25.13. And since these emperors, having gathered most of the wares into the so-called monopolies, ever, day by day, choked those who wished to buy anything—only the clothing-shops being left untouched by them—they devised the following concerning these too.

[195415] 25.14. Garments of silk were wont from of old to be made in Berytus and Tyre, cities of Phoenicia.

[195515] 25.15. And the merchants and master-craftsmen and artisans of these wares dwelt there from of old, and from there this merchandise was carried to all the earth.

[195677] 25.16. But when, under Justinian's reign, those engaged in this trade in Byzantium and the other cities sold this clothing at a higher price, alleging that at the present time more than before was being paid for it to the Persians, and that there were now more tithe-houses in the land of the Romans, the sovereign, giving everyone the impression that he was indignant at this, by law forbade all that this kind of clothing should be more than eight gold pieces the pound.

[196151] 25.17. And a penalty was laid on those who should transgress the law, that they should be deprived of their existing property. These things seemed to men utterly impracticable and impossible. For it was not possible for the merchants, having bought these goods at a higher price, to sell them at a lower to those who dealt with them.

[196486] 25.18. Therefore they no longer thought fit to carry on this trade, but more stealthily made little by little their sales of the goods left to them—plainly to certain of their acquaintances, to whom it was a pleasure, or in some way a necessity, to spend their money in adorning themselves with such things.

[196795] 25.19. And the empress, perceiving these things from certain who whispered them, although she did not investigate the rumors, at once took away all the goods from the men, and fined them a centenarium of gold besides. Now the head of this trade, among the Romans, is the man set over the royal treasuries.

[197102] 25.20. So, having appointed Peter, surnamed Barsymes, to this honor, not long after they allowed him to do unholy deeds.

[197224] 25.21. For he required all the rest to keep the law exactly, but, compelling the craftsmen of this work to work for himself alone, he sold the goods, no longer concealing it, but in the public part of the marketplace—the ounce of ordinary dye at not less than six gold pieces, and of the royal dye, which they are wont to call holovera, at more than twenty-four gold pieces.

[197600] 25.22. And from this he brought the emperor great sums of money, while he himself, surrounding himself with more, escaped notice;

[197731] 25.23. and this, beginning with him, remained forever. For he alone, to this time, is established openly as both merchant and retailer of this merchandise.

[197888] 25.24. So the merchants who formerly carried on this trade in Byzantium and in every city, both by sea and on land, bore, as was likely, the evils that came from the trade.

[198062] 25.25. And in the cities the whole populace, so to speak, of those mentioned suddenly became beggars. For craftsmen and handworkers were compelled, as was likely, to wrestle with hunger, and many on this account, changing their country, fled and departed to the land of the Persians.

[198347] 25.26. And the commander of the treasuries alone, ever making a business of this merchandise, thought fit to bring to the emperor a portion, as has been said, of the revenue from it, while he himself, carrying off the greater part, grew rich on public calamities. So these things went on thus.

[198642] 26.1. In what manner he was able to destroy the ornaments and adornments of the cities, both in Byzantium and in every city, we shall now tell.

[198787] 26.2. First he resolved to abolish the dignity of the advocates. For he at once took away all their rewards, by which they were formerly wont to live in luxury and to pride themselves, being relieved from their pleading, and ordered the litigants to stand by sworn statements; and from this they were insulted and fell into much despondency.

[199130] 26.3. And when he had taken away, as has been said, all the properties of the senators and of the others reputed prosperous in Byzantium and in the whole Roman empire, nothing remained for this profession but to be idle.

[199352] 26.4. For men had nothing worth any legal dispute over which they might contend with one another. So at once, from being many they became few, and from being distinguished they became utterly inglorious everywhere on earth, and were held, as was likely, by much poverty, and departed carrying off only the insult that came from their work.

[199693] 26.5. And further, he made the physicians and the teachers of the liberal arts to be in want of necessities. For the maintenance which the former emperors ordained to be furnished from the public treasury to these professions, all this he took away.

[199944] 26.6. And moreover, whatever revenues, civic or for spectacles, the inhabitants of all the cities had provided from their own resources, these too he dared to transfer and to mingle with the public taxes.

[200150] 26.7. And thereafter no account was made of physicians or teachers, nor was anyone able to take thought for any public building, nor were lamps lit in the cities at public expense, nor was there any other solace for their inhabitants.

[200386] 26.8. For the theaters and hippodromes and wild-beast hunts were for the most part all idle—where his wife happened to have been born and reared and educated.

[200546] 26.9. And later he ordered these spectacles to be idle in Byzantium too, so that the treasury might not furnish the accustomed sums to many men, almost beyond number,

[200714] 26.10. whose living came from this source. And there was, both privately and publicly, grief and dejection, as though some other affliction from heaven had fallen upon them, and life for all was without laughter.

[200928] 26.11. And nothing else at all was carried about among men in their conversations, whether they were at home or shopping or spending time in the sanctuaries, except misfortunes and sufferings and an excess of ever-newer disasters.

[201160] 26.12. So things stood thus for the cities. What remains for the account it is worth while to state. There were two consuls of the Romans each year, one in Rome, the other in Byzantium.

[201347] 26.13. And whoever was called to this honor was to spend on the state more than twenty centenaria of gold—a little of his own,

[201475] 26.14. but the greater part received from the emperor. And this money, going for the most part to the others I mentioned, and to the poorer among the livelihoods, and especially to all those on the stage, ever raised up the affairs of the city.

[201721] 26.15. But from the time Justinian took over the kingship, these things were no longer done at the proper times; rather, at first a consul was established for the Romans after a long interval, and in the end they did not see the thing even in a dream—from which the affairs of men were continually squeezed by a certain poverty, the emperor no longer furnishing the accustomed things to his subjects, while he took away their existing property by every means from every quarter.

[202201] 26.16. So that this destroyer swallowed up all the public moneys and took away the properties of the senators, each privately and all in common—

[202347] 26.17. I think I have related sufficiently. And how, going about with false accusations, he was able to take away the money of the others reputed prosperous, I think I have stated most fully—not, however, the soldiers and those who served all the magistrates and those serving in the Palace, the farmers and the possessors and owners of lands and those whose pursuits are in learning, but also the merchants and shipmasters and sailors and craftsmen and handworkers and traders and those whose living comes from pursuits on the stage, and indeed almost all the rest, so to speak, to whom the harm from this reaches.

[202964] 26.18. And what he did to the beggars and the common people and the poor and those held by every infirmity, we shall now tell; for what he did concerning the priests will be told in my later books.

[203163] 26.19. First, as has been said, having encompassed all the shops and established the so-called monopolies of the most necessary wares, he exacted from all men more than triple the prices.

[203352] 26.20. And as for the other things, since they seemed to me innumerable, I would not, for my part, contend to recount them in an endless account; but from those who bought bread he plundered most bitterly for all time—men who, being handworkers and poor and held by every infirmity, could not but buy bread.

[203661] 26.21. For he himself thought fit to carry off from this three centenaria each year, so that the loaves became poorer and full of ash; for not even to this impiety of base gain did this emperor go reluctantly.

[203872] 26.22. And under this pretext those on whom this office was laid, contriving private gains, themselves most easily came round to a great wealth, while they ever, beyond expectation, wrought a man-made famine for the poor in times of plenty, since it was not at all permitted to bring grain in from anywhere else, but it was necessary for all to eat the bread they bought.

[204245] 26.23. And seeing the aqueduct of the city broken and bringing only a small portion of water into the city, they took no notice of it and would not pay out anything at all for it, although a great throng was ever choking about the fountains, and all the baths were closed; and yet on sea-buildings and other senseless things he poured out a quantity of money for no reason, contriving them everywhere in the suburbs, as though the royal palaces could not contain him, in which all the former emperors had ever wished to live.

[204772] 26.24. So it was not from thrift of money, but for the destruction of men, that he resolved to neglect the building of the aqueduct, since no one in all time was more ready than this Justinian of all men both to acquire money wickedly and to pour it out at once still more wickedly.

[205056] 26.25. So of the two things left to the utterly poor and beggarly in drink and food—water and bread—this emperor harmed them in both, as I have related, making the one unavailable to them and the other much dearer.

[205272] 26.26. And he did these things not only to the beggars in Byzantium, but also to some of those dwelling elsewhere, as I shall presently relate.

[205417] 26.27. For when Theoderic had taken Italy, he left the soldiers serving in the Palace of Rome where they were, so that some trace of the old state might be preserved there, leaving one allowance a day for each.

[205629] 26.28. And these were very numerous. For among them were the so-called silentiaries and domestics and scholarii, to whom nothing was left but the name of military service, and this allowance, barely sufficient for them to subsist on, which Theoderic ordered them to pass on to their children and descendants.

[205939] 26.29. And to the beggars who had their dwelling beside the church of Peter the Apostle, he ordained that the treasury should ever furnish three thousand bushels of grain each year; all of which these men continued to receive until Alexander Scissors came to Italy.

[206206] 26.30. For this man at once, with no hesitation, resolved to take all of it away. On learning this, Justinian, sovereign of the Romans, approved this deed and held Alexander in still greater honor than before. On this journey Alexander did the following to the Greeks too.

[206480] 26.31. The guard-post at Thermopylae the farmers of the regions there cared for from of old, and guarded the wall there in rotation, whenever an inroad of some barbarians was likely to fall upon the Peloponnese.

[206693] 26.32. But this Alexander, being there at the time, pretending to take thought for the Peloponnesians, said he would not entrust the guard-post there to farmers.

[206856] 26.33. So, establishing there about two thousand soldiers, he ordained that their pay should not be furnished from the public treasury, but transferred all the civic and spectacle moneys of all the cities in Greece to the public treasury under this pretext, that from there these soldiers should be fed; and from this, in all the rest of Greece, and not least in Athens itself, neither was any public building renewed nor was any other good thing able to come about.

[207324] 26.34. Justinian, however, with no delay confirmed what had been done there by Scissors.

[207414] 26.35. So these things went on thus. But we must come also to the poor in Alexandria. There was a certain Hephaestus among the rhetoricians there, who, taking over the rule of Alexandria, put a stop to the populace's rioting, appearing terrible to the partisans, but displayed to all the inhabitants there the utmost of utmost evils.

[207749] 26.36. For at once establishing all the shops of the city in the so-called monopoly, he allowed no other of the merchants to carry on this trade, but, having become the sole retailer of all, he delivered all the wares, plainly fixing their prices by the power of his office; and the city of Alexandria was choked by the scarcity of necessities, where formerly all things had been cheap enough even for the utterly poor; and especially he pressed them concerning bread.

[208219] 26.37. For he himself bought the grain from the Egyptians alone, not allowing anyone else to buy so much as a single bushel, and in this way disposed of the loaves and the prices of the loaves as he wished.

[208427] 26.38. So he himself in a short time surrounded himself with untold wealth, and satisfied the emperor's desire in this matter.

[208555] 26.39. And the populace of the Alexandrians, in fear of Hephaestus, bore their present lot quietly; but the sovereign, out of respect for the money ever brought in to him, cherished the man beyond measure.

[208762] 26.40. And this Hephaestus, planning how he might be able to win the emperor's mind much more, contrived in addition the following.

[208895] 26.41. Diocletian, who had been sovereign of the Romans, ordained that a great quantity of grain should be given each year from the public treasury of the Alexandrians to those in need.

[209082] 26.42. This the populace then distributed among themselves and passed it on to their descendants down to the present.

[209201] 26.43. But Hephaestus took away from those who lacked necessities up to two hundred thousand bushels yearly from this source and put it into the public treasury, writing to the emperor that these men had received it neither justly nor as was expedient for affairs up to now.

[209477] 26.44. And from this the emperor, confirming the deed, held him in greater esteem, while those of the Alexandrians who had this hope of their livelihood were, in their most pressing need, deprived of it by this inhumanity.

[209701] 27.1. The deeds of Justinian, then, are so many that not even all time could suffice for the account of them.

[209812] 27.2. But it will be enough for me, having selected a few out of all, to tell those by which his whole character will be plainly evident to those who come after, as one who was a dissembler, and cared neither for God nor for priests nor for laws, nor for the populace though it seemed zealously devoted to him, nor indeed for any shame at all, or for the advantage of the state, or for whatever might be of service to it, or for how his deeds might find some pretext; nor did anything else enter his mind but the taking away of the money lying in all the earth. I shall begin here.

[210395] 27.3. He appointed a high priest for the Alexandrians, Paul by name. And there happened to be a certain Rhodon, a Phoenician by birth, holding at that time the rule of Alexandria.

[210576] 27.4. To him he gave orders to serve Paul in all things with all eagerness, so that none of his commands might remain unfulfilled.

[210708] 27.5. For in this way he thought it would be possible to win over the heretics of Alexandria to the synod at Chalcedon.

[210829] 27.6. Now there was a certain Arsenius, a Palestinian by birth, who had become an intimate of the empress Theodora in matters of the greatest urgency, and from this, surrounding himself with much power and great wealth, came to senatorial rank, though he was most foul.

[211100] 27.7. This man was a Samaritan, but, in order not to give up his existing power, resolved to take the name of the Christians.

[211227] 27.8. His father and brother, however, trusting in his power, continued in Scythopolis, sheltering their ancestral belief, and, by his counsel, wrought irreparable things upon all the Christians.

[211424] 27.9. Therefore the citizens, rising against them, killed both by a most pitiable death, and from this many evils befell the Palestinians.

[211564] 27.10. At that time, then, neither Justinian nor the empress did him any harm, although he had been most responsible for all these troubles, but they forbade him to go to the Palace any longer; for on his account they were most constantly troubled by the Christians.

[211832] 27.11. This Arsenius, thinking to gratify the emperor, not long after was sent with Paul to Alexandria, to serve him in other things and to help him win over the Alexandrians with all his power.

[212028] 27.12. For he insisted that during this time, when it befell him to be shut out of the Palace, he had become not unpracticed in all the doctrines of the Christians.

[212194] 27.13. This grieved Theodora; for she pretended to go against the emperor in this, as I have said in my earlier books.

[212314] 27.14. So when they came to Alexandria, Paul handed over to Rhodon a certain deacon, Psoes by name, to be put to death, declaring that he alone stood in his way against carrying out what the emperor had decided.

[212527] 27.15. And Rhodon, led by the emperor's letters, which were many and very urgent, resolved to torture the man. And he, racked by the torture, died at once.

[212684] 27.16. When this came to the emperor, the empress pressing most strongly, the emperor at once stirred up everything against Paul and Rhodon and Arsenius, as though he had forgotten all that he himself had commanded these men.

[212911] 27.17. So, appointing Liberius, a man of patrician rank from Rome, to the rule of Alexandria, he sent some of the distinguished priests to Alexandria to make a judgment of the matter, among whom was also Pelagius, the archdeacon of Rome, who assumed the person of Vigilius the high priest, this having been enjoined upon him by Vigilius.

[213250] 27.18. And the murder being proven, they at once deposed Paul from the priesthood; and the emperor took off the head of Rhodon, who had fled to Byzantium, and entered his property in the public registers, although the man produced thirteen letters which the emperor had written to him, urging and most earnestly insisting and commanding him to serve Paul in everything he ordered and to oppose him in nothing whatever, so that he might be able to carry out what had been decided in the way of doctrine.

[213754] 27.19. And Liberius, by Theodora's decision, impaled Arsenius, and the emperor resolved to confiscate his property, although he had nothing to charge against him except that he had lived with Paul.

[213953] 27.20. Whether these things were done by him rightly or in some other way, I cannot say; but for what purpose I have said them I shall now make clear.

[214105] 27.21. Paul, after some time, coming to Byzantium and paying out seven centenaria of gold to this emperor, thought fit to recover the priesthood, on the ground that it had been taken from him by no law.

[214309] 27.22. And Justinian received the money graciously and held the man in honor, and promised to appoint him at once high priest of the Alexandrians, although another held the honor—as though he did not know that he himself had killed those who had lived with him and dared to serve him, and had taken away their properties.

[214632] 27.23. So the Augustus, insisting most earnestly, pressed the matter, and Paul was plainly likely to recover the priesthood by every means.

[214773] 27.24. But Vigilius, who was present at the time, by no means resolved to yield to the emperor when he gave such an order. For he said he was not able himself to make his own verdict subject to revision,

[214978] 27.25. indicating the judgment of Pelagius. So nothing else ever mattered to this emperor except the taking away of money. And another thing of the same kind shall be told.

[215152] 27.26. There was a certain Faustinus, a Palestinian by birth, a Samaritan from of old, but taking the name of the Christians under the compulsion of the law.

[215311] 27.27. This Faustinus came to senatorial rank and held the rule of the region; from which, being relieved not long after and coming to Byzantium, certain of the priests slandered him, bringing the charge that he was sheltering the customs of the Samaritans and had done unholy things to the Christians dwelling in Palestine.

[215637] 27.28. And Justinian seemed to grow fierce and to make much of it on this account, that, while he held the Roman empire, the name of Christ should be derided by anyone whatever.

[215816] 27.29. So those of the senate who made the judgment of the matter punished Faustinus with exile, the emperor pressing them.

[215941] 27.30. But the emperor, having received from him as much money as he wished, at once made his own verdict subject to revision and reversed what had been judged.

[216103] 27.31. And Faustinus, again holding his former dignity, consorted with the emperor, and, being appointed administrator of the royal lands in Palestine and Phoenicia, more fearlessly accomplished everything that was his pleasure.

[216333] 27.32. In what manner, then, Justinian thought fit to shelter the rights of the Christians—though not many things have been said by me, but from these few it is possible to draw proof.

[216519] 27.33. And how, with no hesitation, he shook the laws when money was at stake shall be shown in a very brief account.

[216638] 28.1. There was a certain Priscus in the city of the Emesenes, who knew well how to imitate other men's handwriting, and was exceedingly skillful at this evil art.

[216803] 28.2. Now the church of the Emesenes happened to have become heir to a certain distinguished man many years before.

[216920] 28.3. This man was of patrician rank, Mammianus by name, brilliant in birth and in abundance of money.

[217024] 28.4. And under Justinian's reign Priscus, searching out all the houses of the aforesaid city, if he found any flourishing in wealth and able to bear the loss of great sums, having investigated their forefathers exactly, and lighting upon their old writings, made many documents as though written by those men, acknowledging that they would pay great sums of money to Mammianus as having received these from him by way of deposit.

[217456] 28.5. And the gold acknowledged in these forged documents came to not less than a hundred centenaria.

[217559] 28.6. And he imitated demonically the handwriting of a certain man who, while Mammianus was alive, sitting at the time in the marketplace and holding great repute for his truthfulness and his other virtue, executed all the documents of the citizens, sealing each with his own hand—whom the Romans call a tabellio—and handed them over to those who administered the affairs of the church of the Emesenes, who had agreed that a certain portion of the money to be procured from this source should be set aside for him.

[218075] 28.7. But since the law stood in the way, bringing all other suits within the bar of thirty years, and barring a few others, and the so-called mortgage suits, by the length of forty years,

[218265] 28.8. they contrived the following. Coming to Byzantium and paying out great sums to this emperor, they begged him to help them work the destruction of the citizens, who owed nothing.

[218450] 28.9. And he, having received the money, with no delay wrote a law that the churches should be barred from the suits belonging to them not within the proper times, but only after a hundred years, and that this should be valid not only in Emesa, but throughout the whole Roman empire.

[218735] 28.10. And he appointed to adjudicate this matter for the Emesenes a certain Longinus, a man of action and exceedingly strong in body, who later held the office over the populace in Byzantium.

[218929] 28.11. And those who administered the affairs of the church, having first brought a suit for two centenaria against one of the citizens out of the aforesaid documents, at once obtained judgment against the man, who had nothing at all to plead in his defense on account of so great a length of time and his ignorance of what had then been done.

[219274] 28.12. And all the other men, alike exposed to the false accusers, sat in great mourning, and most of all the most distinguished of the Emesenes.

[219421] 28.13. And as the evil was now flowing over most of the citizens, a certain providence of God came about, as follows.

[219540] 28.14. Longinus ordered Priscus, who had wrought this whole intrigue, to bring him all the documents together; and when he drew back from the act, struck him with all his might.

[219719] 28.15. And he, in no way bearing the blow of an exceedingly strong man, fell on his back, and, now trembling and terrified, and suspecting altogether that Longinus had perceived what had been done, confessed; and so, the whole intrigue being brought to light, the false accusation came to an end.

[220017] 28.16. And these things he was ever, day by day, doing not only against the laws of the Romans, but this emperor was also eager to abolish those honored by the Hebrews.

[220187] 28.17. For if ever the recurring time brought their Passover feast before that of the Christians, he would not allow the Jews to keep it at the proper times, nor in it to do anything to please God or to perform any of the rites among themselves.

[220434] 28.18. And many of them those appointed over the offices, on the ground that they had tasted lamb at this time, fined with great sums of money, charging them with transgression against the state.

[220631] 28.19. Knowing, then, countless other such deeds of Justinian, I would not set them down, since an end must be given to my account. For even by these it will be enough to indicate the man's character.

[220833] 29.1. That he was both a dissembler and false, I shall now make clear. This Liberius, whom I mentioned just now, he relieved of the office he held, and appointed in his place John, an Egyptian by birth, surnamed Laxarion.

[221056] 29.2. When Pelagius learned of this, being a great friend of Liberius, he inquired of the sovereign whether the report about Laxarion was true.

[221201] 29.3. And he at once denied it, asserting that he had done nothing of the kind, and put into his hands letters to Liberius, charging him to hold fast most firmly to the office and in no way to give it up.

[221407] 29.4. For he did not wish, he said, to relieve him of it at present. Now John had in Byzantium an uncle, Eudaemon by name, who had come to the rank of consul and was invested with much money, being at the time administrator of the emperor's private property.
