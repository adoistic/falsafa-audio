[103936] 18.1. When the army with John and Isaaces came to Epidamnus and joined Belisarius, John saw fit that all, ferrying across the gulf and going on foot with the whole army, should bear in common what should befall; but to Belisarius this seemed inexpedient, but rather that it would be more advantageous for them to sail to the regions about Rome. 18.2. For going on foot, more time would be wasted for them and perhaps some hindrance would meet them; but John, going through Calabria and the nations there, should drive out the barbarians there (who were quite few) and, making subject the parts within the Ionian Gulf, should join them, coming to the regions about Rome. 18.3. Where Belisarius himself too purposed to put in with the rest of the army. For he thought that, since the Romans were being most bitterly besieged, even, as is natural, the very shortest delay would do irreparable harm to affairs. 18.4. And sailing, if a favourable wind fell upon them, it would be possible to put in at the harbour of the Romans on the fifth day, but for an army going on foot from Dryus, not even in forty days could they be in Rome. 18.5. Having given John these instructions, Belisarius set out from there with the whole fleet; but a hard wind falling upon them, they put in at Dryus. 18.6. When the Goths perceived this, all who had been ranged for the siege of the fort there, breaking off the siege, were immediately conveyed to the city of Brentesium, distant two days' journey from Dryus, but lying by the shore of the gulf and being unwalled, suspecting that Belisarius would immediately cross the strait there, and reported their situation to Totila. 18.7. And he both made the whole army ready as though to meet him and ordered the Goths in Calabria, wherever it should be possible for them, to keep watch over the passage. 18.8. But when Belisarius, a favourable wind blowing, sailed away from Dryus, the Goths, being careless, kept a not earnest mode of life among the Calabrians, and Totila, remaining quiet, guarded still more the entrances into Rome, in order that nothing of the provisions might be able to be brought in. 18.9. And in the Tiber he devised the following. Observing a place where the river especially comes down in a narrow channel, about ninety stades from the city, he laid there very long beams reaching from one bank to the other into the form of a bridge. 18.10. And building two wooden towers by either bank, he established a watch-station of warlike men, in order that no longer should it be possible for any skiffs or other vessels sailing up from Portus to come into the city. 18.11. In the meantime Belisarius put in at the harbour of the Romans, and the army with John remained. And John ferried across to Calabria, the Goths by no means perceiving it, who were staying, as has been said, about Brentesium. 18.12. And taking two of the enemy going on the road for scouting, he killed one straightway, but the other, laying hold of his knees, begged to be taken alive. 18.13. “For I shall not be useless,” he said, “to you and the Roman army.” And when John asked him what he, not being killed, would ever be of advantage to the Romans and to himself, the man promised to set him over the Goths when they were least expecting it. 18.14. And he said that he would not at all fail of his entreaty, but that he ought first to show him the pastures of the horses. So when the barbarian agreed to this too, he went with him. 18.15. And first, finding the horses of the enemy grazing, all who happened to be on foot mounted upon them; and they were many and excellent. Then they advanced at a run against the camp of their opponents. 18.16. And the barbarians, being unarmed and altogether unprepared, and struck with consternation at the unexpectedness, the most of them perished there, being mindful of no resistance, while some few escaping were conveyed to Totila. 18.17. And John, consoling and winning over all the Calabrians, brought them into goodwill toward the emperor, promising that many good things would come to them both from the emperor and from the Roman army. 18.18. And setting out as fast as he could from Brentesium, he occupied a city named Canusium, which lies about the middle of Apulia and is distant from Brentesium a journey of five days for one going westward and toward Rome. 18.19. Twenty-five stades from this Canusium is Cannae, where they say the great disaster befell the Romans in earlier times, Hannibal commanding the Libyans. 18.20. There a certain Tullianus, the son of Venantius, a Roman man, having great power among the Bruttii and Lucanians, coming into the sight of John, charged the emperor's army with what they had previously wrought against the Italians, but agreed that, if they should henceforth use any fairness toward them, he would put into their hands the Bruttii and Lucanians, who would again be subject to the emperor in the payment of tribute none the less than they were before. 18.21. For they had not come over willingly to barbarians who were Arians, but had done so as being especially forced by their opponents and wronged by the emperor's soldiers. 18.22. And when John affirmed that for the future all good things would come to the Italians from them, Tullianus went with him. 18.23. And from this the soldiers no longer held the Italians in any suspicion, but the most of the parts within the Ionian Gulf became both friendly to them and subject to the emperor. 18.24. When Totila heard this, selecting three hundred Goths, he sent them to Capua. And he instructed them, when they should see the army with John going from there to Rome, to follow them, giving them no perception of it; 18.25. for the rest would be his concern. And John, fearing on account of this lest he should fall into some encirclement of the enemy, no longer went to Belisarius, but advanced into the Bruttii and Lucanians. 18.26. Now there was among the Goths a certain Recimundus, a man of repute, whom Totila had happened to establish over the garrison of the Bruttii, having some of the Goths and Roman soldiers and Moorish deserters, in order that with them he might guard both the strait at Scylla and the shore there, so that none might be able to set out from there to Sicily or to sail down from the island to there too fearlessly. 18.27. Upon this army, between Rhegium and Vebo, John, falling unexpectedly and self-announced, struck them with consternation suddenly as they were mindful of no resistance, and straightway turned them to flight. 18.28. And they fled to the mountain that rises there, which was hard to traverse and otherwise precipitous, but John, pursuing and coming with the enemy to the steep ground, and coming to close quarters with them before they had yet strengthened themselves in the difficult places, killed very many both of the Moors and Roman soldiers, who defended themselves most stoutly, and took Recimundus and the Goths with all the rest by agreement. 18.29. John, then, having accomplished these things, remained there, and Belisarius, always waiting for John, remained quiet. And he found fault that John had not, by risking himself and coming to close quarters with the three hundred guarding at Capua, undertaken to make the passage, although he had with him barbarians gathered for their valour. But John, despairing of the passage, advanced into Apulia, and remained quiet in a place called Cervarium.

[111360] 19.1. Belisarius, then, fearing for the besieged, lest they should do something irreparable through the want of necessities, purposed in whatever way to bring provisions into Rome. 19.2. And since he by no means had a power adequate to settle matters with the enemy by battle in the plain, he first devised the following. 19.3. Yoking together two exceedingly broad skiffs and binding them very firmly to each other, he constructed a wooden tower upon them, much higher than those made by the enemy on the bridge. 19.4. For he had previously happened to measure these exactly, sending some of his followers who were sent ostensibly as deserters to the barbarians. 19.5. And surrounding two hundred warships with wooden walls, he let them down into the Tiber, making holes everywhere in the wooden walls, in order that it might be possible to shoot at the enemy from there. So putting grain and many other provisions into these warships, he filled them with the most warlike of the soldiers. 19.6. And he stationed other soldiers, both foot and horse, on either side in certain strong places about the mouths of the Tiber, whom he ordered to remain there and, if any of the enemy should go against Portus, to prevent it with all their might. 19.7. And bringing Isaaces into Portus, he gave over to him both the city and his wife and anything else he happened to have there. And he instructed him by no means to depart from there, not even if he should learn that Belisarius had perished at the hands of the enemy, but always to hold to the guard, in order that, if any obstacle should meet them, they might have a place to which to flee and be saved. 19.8. For they had no other fortress in this region, but everything everywhere was hostile to them. 19.9. And he himself, embarking on one of the warships, led the fleet and ordered the skiffs to be dragged along, 19.10. on which he happened to have constructed the tower. And on the top of the tower he placed a certain skiff, filling it with pitch and sulphur and resin and all the other things which are wont to become the swiftest food of fire. 19.11. And along the other bank of the river, which is on the right as one goes from Portus to Rome, a foot-army also went as support. 19.12. And sending to Bessas on the day before, he had ordered him on the following day to sally out with a large army and throw the camps of the enemy into confusion—which indeed he had often instructed him before. 19.13. But Bessas neither before nor in this battle resolved to carry out what was enjoined. 19.14. For there was still grain left to him alone, since of all that the rulers of Sicily had previously sent to Rome to suffice both the soldiers and the whole people, he had given the people only a very little, but kept hidden the most, which he had received on account of the soldiers, and which, selling to those of the senate for great sums of money, he was by no means willing to dissolve the siege. 19.15. So Belisarius and the Roman fleet went sailing up with toil, since the current was against them. But the Goths by no means came to meet them, but remained quiet in their stockades. 19.16. And now the Romans, coming close to the bridge, met a guard of the enemy, who had been ranged for the watch with an iron chain on either side of the river, which Totila had not long before happened to place there, reaching from one bank of the Tiber to the other, in order that the enemy might not be able even to come easily to the bridge. 19.17. And shooting at them, they killed some, and routing the rest and taking up the chain, they went straight to the bridge. And as soon as they came to it, they set to work, and the barbarians defended themselves most stoutly from the towers. 19.18. And now they rose up from their stockades and advanced at a run to the bridge. Then indeed Belisarius, bringing the skiffs on which the tower had been made very close to one of the towers of the enemy, which stood on the road by Portus over the very water of the river, ordered them to set fire to the skiff and throw it down on top of the enemy's tower. 19.19. And the Romans did so. And falling upon the tower, the skiff straightway set it on fire, and with it all the Goths, who were about two hundred. 19.20. And Osdas was burned with them, who commanded them, being the most warlike of all the Goths. And the Romans, now taking courage, shot at the barbarians who had come up from the stockades still more than before. 19.21. And they, struck with consternation at what had happened, turning their backs, rushed into flight as each was able; and the Romans laid hold of the bridge and were about immediately to destroy it and go forward and be conveyed to Rome, no one any longer withstanding them. 19.22. But since these things were not the wish of fortune, a device of one of the envious daemons came about, which destroyed the affairs of the Romans in the following manner. 19.23. While the armies were going on thus, as has been said, in the meantime a certain report, coming to Portus for the harm of the Romans, went about, that Belisarius was conquering and had taken up the chain, having destroyed the barbarians there, and the other things which I have mentioned before. 19.24. And Isaaces, hearing this, was no longer able to remain within himself, but was eager to share in this glory. And disregarding the instructions of Belisarius, he went off as quickly as possible to the other bank of the river. 19.25. And taking a hundred horsemen of the soldiers whom Belisarius had happened to establish there, he rode against the stockade of the enemy, which Roderic commanded, a man good in matters of war. 19.26. And coming suddenly upon the barbarians there, he struck, among others, Roderic, who came to meet him. 19.27. And the Goths straightway, leaving the camp, withdrew, either suspecting that a multitude of the enemy was behind Isaaces, or deceiving their opponents in order that they might be able to take them, which is what happened. 19.28. So the men with Isaaces, getting into the stockade of the enemy, plundered the silver which lay there and the other goods. 19.29. But the Goths, turning back immediately, killed many of their opponents and took Isaaces alive with some few others not many. And horsemen, coming to Belisarius at a run, announced that Isaaces was held by the enemy. 19.30. And Belisarius, struck with consternation at what he had heard, and not searching out by what device Isaaces had been taken, but thinking that both Portus and his wife had perished and all their affairs were destroyed, and that no other fortress was left to which they could flee and be saved for the future, fell into speechlessness, which had not happened to him before. 19.31. Wherefore he quickly led the army back, intending to attack the enemy while they were still in disorder and to recover the place by every device. So the Roman army withdrew from there unsuccessful. 19.32. And coming to Portus, Belisarius learned of the madness of Isaaces and perceived the confusion which had befallen him unbefittingly, and being in great pain at the opposition of fortune, became sick in body. 19.33. For a fever, coming upon him, distressed him prodigiously for a length of time and brought him into danger of death. 19.34. And two days later it befell Roderic to die, and Totila, being grievously distressed at the calamity, put Isaaces to death.

[118767] 20.1. And Bessas, still more selling the grain, grew rich, the necessity of those in want dispensing for him the prices. And being wholly devoted to anxiety about these things, he attended neither to the guard of the wall nor to anything else concerning safety, but it was possible for the soldiers to be slack who wished, and there was a garrison of some few on the walls, and this too most neglected. 20.2. For those on whom the guard always lay had much liberty to sleep, since no one was set over them who would make any account of such a thing; nor did any go around the walls in a circle, as was wont, and see fit to search out what the guards were doing; nor indeed could any of the inhabitants help them keep guard. 20.3. For there were very few left there, as has been said by me, and they themselves prodigiously maltreated by famine. 20.4. Four Isaurians, then, having guard about the Asinarian gate, watching the time of night at which it fell to those who had it to sleep, while the guard of the wall there lay upon them, fastening a rope from the battlements reaching to the ground, and taking hold of this with both hands, got outside the wall, and coming to Totila agreed to receive into the city both him and the Gothic army; for they affirmed they were able to do this with no trouble. 20.5. And he, agreeing that he would have abundant gratitude toward them if they should accomplish this, and would make them masters of great sums of money, sent two of his followers with them to observe the place from which the men said it was possible for the Goths to come into the city. 20.6. And these, when they came to the wall, taking hold of the ropes, went up onto the battlements, no one there either speaking or perceiving what was being done. 20.7. And when they were there, the Isaurians showed the barbarians everything, that if they wished to come up no obstacle would arise, and that to those who came up there would be much liberty, no one whatever withstanding them; and ordering them to report these things to Totila, they sent them away. 20.8. When Totila heard this, he was somewhat pleased at the message, but, being even so gripped by suspicion against the Isaurians, saw fit not to trust them too much. 20.9. And not many days later the men came to him again, urging him to the deed. And he sent two others with them, in order that they too, searching out everything exactly, might report. 20.10. And they, returning to him, announced everything in like manner to the former men. In the meantime many Roman soldiers, sent out for scouting from the city, fell in with ten Goths going on the road not far off, whom they took and brought straightway to Bessas. 20.11. And he inquired of these barbarians what Totila was purposing, and the Goths said they were in hope that some of the Isaurians would give over the city to them; for the report had now become known to many of the barbarians. 20.12. But Bessas and Conon, hearing even this, held it in much disregard and made no account of it. And a third time the Isaurians, coming into the sight of Totila, urged the man to the deed. 20.13. And he sent others with them and one of his kinsmen, who, returning to him and reporting the whole account, strengthened him for the deed. 20.14. And Totila, as soon as night had come, arming the whole army quietly, led it against the Asinarian gate. And he ordered four of the Goths who were well off in courage and strength to go up to the battlements through the nooses with the Isaurians, at that time of night, of course, at which the guard of the wall there fell to these Isaurians, the others taking their sleep in turn. 20.15. And these, when they were inside the wall, went down to the Asinarian gate, no one withstanding them, and destroyed with axes the wood which, fitted by insertion into each wall, they were wont to use to fasten the gates, and all the irons with which the guards, putting in the keys, were always wont both to lock the gates and to open them as need required. 20.16. And thus, throwing open the gates as they wished, they received into the city Totila and the Gothic army with no trouble. And Totila, gathering them there into one place, by no means allowed them to scatter; for he feared lest some ambush by the enemy should befall them. And when confusion, 20.17. as is natural, and tumult fell upon the city, the most of the Roman soldiers fled with their commanders through another gate, as it became easy for each, but some few took refuge with the rest of the Romans in the churches. 20.18. And of the patricians Decius and Basilius with certain others (for they happened to have horses present) were able to flee with Bessas. 20.19. But Maximus and Olybrius and Orestes and certain others fled to the church of Peter the apostle. Of the people, however, it befell that five hundred men were left in all the city, who took refuge in the churches with difficulty. 20.20. For of all the rest, some had previously gone off to other countries, and some had perished of famine, as has been said by me before. Many, then, announced to Totila by night that Bessas and the enemy were fleeing. And he, saying that they had spoken a pleasant word, would not allow them to pursue. 20.21. For what could be sweeter to a man, he said, than that the enemy should flee? 20.22. And when it was now day and no suspicion of any ambush was any longer left, Totila went to the church of Peter the apostle to pray, while the Goths destroyed those who had fallen in their way. 20.23. And there died in this manner twenty-six of the soldiers and sixty of the people. And when Totila came to the church, Pelagius came into his sight bearing the oracles of the Christians in his hands, and in every way putting forward his supplication, said, “Spare your own, O master.” 20.24. And he, mocking and exulting, said, “Now, O Pelagius, you have come to supplicate me.” “When,” Pelagius answered, “God has made me your slave. 20.25. But for the future, O master, spare your slaves.” Totila, receiving this supplication, forbade the Goths to kill for the future any of all the Romans, but to take out for himself the most precious of the goods, and the rest to plunder for themselves at their will. 20.26. Much, then, he found in the houses of the patricians, but most of all where Bessas lodged. For the unholy prices of the grain, as has been shown, this wretched man was gathering for Totila. 20.27. And thus it came about for the rest of the Romans, and for those of the senate, and not least for Rusticiana, who had become the wife of Boethius and was the daughter of Symmachus, and was always putting forth her substance to the needy, to live, clothed in the garments of slaves and country folk, begging bread or some other necessity from the enemy. 20.28. And going about the houses and constantly knocking at the doors, they begged that food be given them, the deed bringing them no shame. 20.29. And the Goths were eager to kill Rusticiana, charging that, putting forth money to the commanders of the Roman army, she had destroyed the statues of Theoderic, avenging the murders of her father Symmachus and of Boethius her husband. 20.30. But Totila did not allow her to suffer anything unpleasant, but kept both her and all the others free from violence, although the Goths were exceedingly eager to come to their beds. 20.31. Wherefore it befell that none of them was violated in body, neither a married woman nor a maiden nor a widow, and Totila won great fame for chastity from this deed.

[126295] 21.1. On the following day Totila, calling all the Goths together, spoke as follows: “Not to make to you any new or unknown exhortation have I gathered you here, fellow-soldiers, but to say those things which I have often said to you, and which, when you received them, it befell the greatest of goods to come to pass. 21.2. Do not, then, through this use any disregard toward the exhortation now. 21.3. For of words that lead to prosperity it is not natural that there be satiety for a man, even if one should seem to weary him by their length; since it is not right to refuse the benefit arising from them either. 21.4. And I say that lately, when twenty myriads of most warlike soldiers gathered together, using prodigious wealth and displaying a certain abundance, as great as possible, of horses and arms, and a great throng of most intelligent elders—which seems most advantageous to those who set themselves to contests—we were defeated by seven thousand Greeks, and have been deprived of the rule and of all the rest for no reason. 21.5. But now, having been reduced to a few, naked and pitiable and experienced in nothing whatever, it has befallen us to master the enemy, who are more than twenty thousand. 21.6. The things done, then, to speak in a word, are such; but the causes of things befalling thus I shall now make plain to you who know them: that the Goths formerly, setting justice below all other things, wrought unholy deeds both toward one another and toward the Roman subjects, by which, as is natural, God, being led against them, then campaigned with the enemy. 21.7. And on account of these things, although surpassing our opponents by far in numbers and in valour and in the rest of the preparation for war, we were defeated by a certain power unseen and least of all knowable. 21.8. Therefore the guarding of these goods will lie with you, preserving plainly justice. For if you change, the things from God too will immediately be hostile. 21.9. For it is not wont to ally itself either with a race of men or with the nature of nations, but with those by whom the reckoning of justice is more honoured. And there is no toil for it to transfer good things to others. 21.10. For for a man only not to do wrong lies in his purpose, but for God all things naturally lie in his power. 21.11. I say, then, that you ought to keep justice both toward one another and toward your subjects. For it would be the same thing as to say that you preserve your prosperity for ever.” 21.12. Saying so much to the Goths, Totila, calling together those of the Roman senate, reproached and reviled them much, who, having received many good things from Theoderic and Athalaric, and being themselves established for ever over the whole rule and managing the state, and being girt with a great quantity of wealth, then, gripped by much thanklessness toward the Goths their benefactors, had looked to a revolt, which they ought not, to their own harm, and had brought in the Greeks against their country, having suddenly become traitors to themselves. 21.13. And he inquired whether they themselves had ever suffered any evil at the hands of the Goths. 21.14. And he compelled them to say if any good had befallen them from the emperor Justinian, enumerating everything in order: that they had been deprived of nearly all the magistracies, and, being maltreated by the so-called logothetes, had been compelled to render accounts of their dealings in office toward the Goths, and, being worn down by the war, paid the public taxes to the Greeks no less than in time of peace; and many other things he put into his speech, such as it is likely a hostile master should reproach those he has enslaved. 21.15. And, pointing out to them Herodian and the Isaurians who had given over the city to him, “You,” he said, “though brought up with the Goths, did not resolve to give over to us even a deserted place to this day, but these men received us both into Rome itself and into Spolitium. 21.16. Wherefore you have come to be in the position of slaves, while these men, having become, as is natural, friends and kinsmen to the Goths, will for the future hold your 21.17. magistracies.” So the patricians, hearing this, kept silence. But Pelagius did not cease begging off Totila on behalf of men who had failed and been unfortunate, until he dismissed them, making a promise of kindness. 21.18. After this he sent Pelagius and one of the orators in Rome, Theodorus by name, as envoys to the emperor Justinian, binding them with the most dreadful oaths, that they would use goodwill toward him and would be eager to return to Italy as quickly as possible. 21.19. And he instructed them to obtain peace for him from the emperor with all their might, in order that he might not be compelled to raze the whole of Rome to the ground and destroy those of the senate, and to carry the war into Illyricum. And he wrote a letter to the emperor Justinian. 21.20. Now the emperor had already heard what had happened in Italy. And when the envoys came to him, they reported what Totila instructed them and put the letter into his hands. 21.21. And the writing made plain the following: “As to what has befallen to happen in the city of the Romans, since I think you have learned all, I have resolved to keep silent. 21.22. But for what reasons I have sent these envoys you will know at once. We ask that you both receive yourself the good things from peace and concede them to us. 21.23. Of which we have the finest memorials and examples in Anastasius and Theoderic, who reigned not long before, and filled all the time of their reign with peace and good things. 21.24. For if this should ever be your wish, you would justly be called my father, and you would have us 21.25. for the future as allies against whomever you should wish.” When the emperor Justinian saw this brought to him and heard all the words of the envoys, he dismissed them immediately, making them this answer alone, and writing to Totila, that he had made Belisarius supreme commander of the war, and that he therefore had the authority to settle matters with Totila in whatever way he wished.

[132448] 22.1. While these envoys were being sent to Byzantium and again to Italy, in the meantime in the Lucanians it befell that the following happened. 22.2. Tullianus, gathering the country folk there, guarded the entrance, which was very narrow, in order that the enemy might not go to do mischief to the regions of Lucania. 22.3. And three hundred Antae kept guard with them, whom John, at the request of Tullianus, had previously happened to leave there; for these barbarians are good at fighting in difficult ground above all. 22.4. When Totila learned this, he thought it inexpedient to send Goths to the work, but gathering a multitude of country folk and sending with them some few Goths, he ordered them to make trial of the entrance with all their might. 22.5. And when these came to close quarters with one another, there was much shoving on both sides, but the Antae, by their own valour, and at the same time the difficult ground also helping them, together with the country folk of Tullianus turned their opponents to flight. 22.6. And there was much slaughter of them. When Totila learned this, he resolved to raze Rome to the ground, and to leave the most of the army somewhere there, and with the rest to go against John and the Lucanians. 22.7. So of the wall in many places he razed as much as about a third of the whole. And he was about to set fire to the finest and most notable of the buildings and to make Rome a pasture for sheep, but Belisarius, learning this, sent envoys and a letter to him. 22.8. These, when they came into the sight of Totila, told for what reasons they had come and put the letter into his hands. And the writing made plain the following: “To make the beauties of a city that do not exist would be the inventions of prudent men who know how to live in a civilized way; but to destroy those that exist would befit the senseless, and they would not be ashamed to leave this mark of their nature to later time. 22.9. Now Rome, of all the cities that are under the sun, is agreed to be the greatest and most notable. 22.10. For it has been wrought by the valour not of one man, nor has it come to such size and beauty by the power of a short time, but a multitude of kings, and many companies of the best men, and length of time and abundance of wealth, were able to gather here both all other things from all the earth and craftsmen. 22.11. And thus, building little by little such a city as you see, they left memorials of the valour of all to those who come after, so that wanton outrage against these would, as is natural, seem a great wrong against the men of every age. 22.12. For it takes from those who lived before the memory of their valour, and from those who come after the sight of the works. 22.13. These things being so, know this well, that one of two things is of necessity. For either you will be defeated by the emperor in this struggle, or you will prevail, as may chance. 22.14. If, then, you should conquer, by destroying Rome you would have destroyed not another's possession, most excellent man, but your own, and by preserving it you will be enriched, as is natural, with the finest of all possessions; but if it should befall that the worse fortune be fulfilled for you, by saving Rome much gratitude would be preserved for you from the victor, but if you destroy it, there will be left no account of kindness, and there will be added the getting no benefit from the deed. 22.15. And a repute worthy of the deed will overtake you from all men, which stands ready for you on either side of your purpose. 22.16. For whatever the works of rulers may be, it is of necessity that such a name also be borne concerning them.” So much Belisarius wrote. 22.17. And Totila, having read the letter often and made himself exactly acquainted with the exhortation, was persuaded and did no further harm to Rome. And signifying to Belisarius his purpose, he immediately dismissed the envoys. 22.18. And he ordered the greater part of the army to encamp not far from Rome, but about a hundred and twenty stades westward, in a place called Algedon, and to remain quiet, in order that there might be no power for Belisarius and his men to go anywhere outside Portus; and he himself with the rest of the army went against John and the Lucanians. 22.19. He kept with himself, however, those of the Roman senate, but sent all the others, together with their wives and children, to the regions of Campania, leaving no man in Rome, but leaving it altogether deserted. 22.20. And John, learning that Totila was coming against him, no longer saw fit to remain in Apulia, but came at a run to Dryus. And the patricians who were being led to Campania, sending some of their household to the Lucanians, by the resolve of Totila ordered their country folk to release themselves from what they were doing and to till the fields as they were wont; for they announced that good things would come to them from their owners. 22.21. And these released themselves from the Roman army and remained quiet in the fields; but Tullianus fled and went off, and the three hundred Antae resolved to withdraw to John. 22.22. So all the parts within the Ionian Gulf, except Dryus, again became subject to the Goths and Totila. And the barbarians, now taking courage and scattering in companies, went about all the regions in a circle. 22.23. When John learned this, he sent many of his followers against them. And these, falling unexpectedly upon the enemy, killed many. 22.24. And from this Totila, becoming afraid, and gathering all those with him, encamped about Mount Garganum, which rises about the middle of Apulia, in the stockade of Hannibal the Libyan, and remained quiet.

[138149] 23.1. In the meantime one of those who had fled from Rome with Conon when the city was being taken, Martinianus by name, a Byzantine by race, coming to Belisarius, saw fit to be sent ostensibly as a deserter to the enemy, promising to do the Romans great good; and this seeming best to Belisarius, he went off. And Totila, when he saw him, was prodigiously pleased. 23.2. For he had both heard of and often seen the youth distinguishing himself in single combats. And since his two children and his wife were among the captives, the wife and one of his children he gave back to the man straightway, but the other he kept in the position of a hostage and sent to Spolitium with certain others. 23.3. Now it happened that the Goths, when, Herodian giving it over, they took Spolitium, had razed the wall of the city to the ground, but of the place for hunting before the city, which they are wont to call an amphitheatre, blocking up the entrances exactly and establishing there a garrison of Goths and Roman deserters, 23.4. in order that they might guard the regions there. So Martinianus, when he came to Spolitium, was able to win over fifteen soldiers, whom he persuaded that, having done some great deed against the barbarians, they should thus return to the Roman army. 23.5. And sending some men to the commander of the garrison in Perusia, he ordered him to send an army as quickly as possible to Spolitium, making known to him the whole account. 23.6. Now Odolgan, a Hun, then commanded the garrison in Perusia, since Cyprianus had been removed from among men by treachery at the hands of one of his guards, as has been said by me before. He went with an army to Spolitium. 23.7. And Martinianus, perceiving that this army was somewhere close, together with the fifteen soldiers suddenly destroyed the commander of the garrison and, throwing open the gates, received all the Romans into the fort. And they killed very many of the enemy, and taking some alive led them to Belisarius. 23.8. A little later a thought came to Belisarius to go up to Rome and to behold to what fortune it had come. So selecting a thousand of the soldiers he went there. 23.9. But a Roman man, coming at a run to the enemy, who were encamped at Algedon, announced the army of Belisarius. 23.10. And the barbarians, laying ambushes in the places before Rome, when they saw Belisarius and his men coming somewhere close, rose from the ambushes and went against them. 23.11. And when a fierce battle had taken place, the Romans by their own valour routing the enemy and destroying very many, withdrew immediately to Portus. These things, then, proceeded thus. 23.12. Now there is a city of the Calabrians by the sea, Taras, distant about two days' journey from Dryus, on the way thence to the Thurii and Rhegini. 23.13. There John came with some few, the people of Taras inviting him, having established the rest at the guard of Dryus. 23.14. And when he saw the city to be exceedingly great and altogether unwalled, he thought he would by no means be able to guard the whole; but seeing the sea of the city to the north running into a gulf about a certain very narrow place on either side, where the harbour of the people of Taras is, and an isthmus, as is natural, being the space between, no less than twenty stades, he devised the following. 23.15. Cutting off the portion of the isthmus from the rest of the city, he surrounded it with a wall from one part of the sea to the other and dug a deep trench about the wall. 23.16. And there he gathered not only the people of Taras but all who dwelt in the regions there, and left them a garrison worthy of account. 23.17. And in this way all the Calabrians, having now become in safety, looked to a revolt from the Goths. These things were going on thus. 23.18. And Totila, occupying a most strong fort in the Lucanians lying somewhere close to the borders of Calabria, which the Romans call Acherontis, and establishing there a watch-station of no fewer than four hundred men, himself went with the rest of the army toward Ravenna, leaving some of the barbarians in the regions of Campania, on whom the Romans of the senate who were there lay as a guard.

[142339] 24.1. And to Belisarius there came at that time a provident daring, which at first seemed madness to those who saw and heard it, but which issued in a deed of valour lofty and divinely prodigious. 24.2. For leaving some few soldiers for the guard in Portus, he himself with the rest of the army came to Rome and wished to occupy it with all his might. 24.3. And since he was not able to rebuild in a short time as much of the wall as Totila had razed, he did as follows. 24.4. Gathering the stones that were somewhere near, he threw them upon one another in no order, having nothing whatever inside, since he had neither lime nor anything else of the kind, but only in order that the face of the building might be preserved, and he set a great quantity of stakes outside. 24.5. And he had previously happened to dig deep trenches about the whole wall, as has been said by me in the previous narrative. 24.6. And as the whole army wrought these things with all eagerness, in twenty-five days all of the wall that had been razed was completed in this manner. 24.7. And those of the Romans who dwelt about the regions there gathered there, both out of desire of dwelling in Rome and because, having for a while been in want of the necessities, they found there an abundance which Belisarius was able to make, filling as many ships as possible with all the necessities and bringing them into Rome through the river. 24.8. When Totila heard this, setting out immediately with the whole army he came against Belisarius and Rome, Belisarius not having yet been able to fit gates to the wall. 24.9. For Totila had happened to destroy all of them, which Belisarius had not had time to make for want of craftsmen. 24.10. And when the army of the barbarians came near, for the moment, encamping, they bivouacked by the river Tiber, and on the following day, with the sun rising, gripped by much anger and tumult, they went against the wall. 24.11. And Belisarius, selecting the most warlike of the soldiers, stationed them in the place of the gates, and ordered the rest to defend themselves against the assailants from above on the walls with all their might. 24.12. There was, then, a fierce battle. For the barbarians at first had hope of taking the city at the first shout, but the matter withstanding them, and the Romans defending themselves most stoutly, using anger they pressed upon the enemy, their rage urging them beyond their power to daring. 24.13. And the Romans held out beyond expectation, the danger, as is natural, leading them to courage. 24.14. There was, then, much slaughter of the barbarians, being shot at from above, and both sides were gripped by much weariness and hardship, and the battle, beginning in the morning, ended at nightfall. 24.15. After this the barbarians, coming into their camps, bivouacked, attending to the wounded among them, while of the Romans the rest held guard within the wall, but those who were well off in courage above all guarded the place of the gates in turn, setting many caltrops in front, so that the enemy might not come against them by a sudden raid. 24.16. The caltrops are of this kind. Fitting together at the back four stakes of equal length, they make their straight lines into the form of a triangle on every side, and they throw them on the ground wherever they chance. 24.17. And in this way three of the stakes always stand most firmly on the ground, and the one remaining, rising alone, always becomes a hindrance to both men and horses. 24.18. And whenever anyone rolls this caltrop, the one which before had drawn the lot of the upright space in the air stands to the ground, and another taking its place is a hindrance above to those wishing to go forward. Such are the caltrops. So both sides bivouacked thus after the battle. 24.19. On the following day Totila resolved to assault the wall again with the whole army, and the Romans defended themselves in the manner described. And having become superior in the engagement, they took courage to sally out against the enemy. 24.20. And as the barbarians withdrew, some of the Romans, pursuing, got somewhere far from the wall. These the barbarians were about to encircle, so that they might not be able to return to the city. But Belisarius (for he observed what was being done), sending many of those about him there, was able to save the men. 24.21. And thus the barbarians, thrust back, withdrew, having lost many of their fighting men and bringing very many wounded to the camp. 24.22. And there they remained quiet, treating their wounds and attending to their arms (for many of them had now been destroyed) and making all the rest in full readiness. And many days later they went against the wall as though to assault it. 24.23. But the Romans, meeting them, came to close quarters. And by some chance he who bore the standard of Totila, being struck a mortal blow, both fell from his horse and threw the standard to the ground. 24.24. And the Romans who fought in the front rushed to seize the standard and the corpse. But the most daring of the barbarians, anticipating, seized the standard and, cutting off the left hand of the corpse, took it with them. 24.25. For he who had fallen happened to be wearing on it a golden bracelet worthy of account, on which they were by no means willing that their opponents should glory, fleeing plainly the shame arising from the deed. 24.26. And the army of the barbarians turned to withdrawal in no order, while the Romans both spoiled the rest of the corpse, and pursuing the enemy for the most part killed many, and came into the city altogether unharmed. 24.27. Then those who were notable among the Goths, coming to Totila, both reviled him and without restraint reproached him for his ill-counsel, that, having taken Rome, he neither razed it all to the ground, so that it might no longer be capturable by the enemy, nor held it himself, but what had been wrought by them with toil and much time, this he himself destroyed for no reason. 24.28. So is it implanted in men always to order their judgement according to the issues of affairs, and to make their mind go along with the current of fortune, and from this to make abrupt changes of opinion. 24.29. Wherefore the Goths, when Totila prospered in his undertakings, regarded him as equal to a god, calling him invincible and unconquerable, when he allowed a portion of the walls of captured cities to be destroyed; but when he failed, as has been said, they did not refuse to revile him, forgetting what they had lately said about him, and going against it with no shrinking. 24.30. But these and such things it is not possible for men not always to err in, since they are wont to happen even by nature. 24.31. And Totila and the barbarians, breaking off the siege, came to the city of Tibur, having destroyed nearly all the bridges of the Tiber, in order that the Romans might not be able easily to come against them. 24.32. One bridge, however, which is named after Molvius, they were by no means able to destroy, since it happened to be close to the city. And they resolved with all their might to rebuild the fort in Tibur; 24.33. for they had previously happened to raze it. And depositing all their goods there, they remained quiet. 24.34. And Belisarius, now more fearlessly fitting the gates of the wall of Rome on every side and binding them about with iron, sent the keys again to the emperor. And the winter was ending, and the twelfth year of this war which Procopius has written was coming to a close.

[149878] 25.1. Now Totila had much earlier happened to send an army against Perusia, who, encamping about the wall of the city, exactly besieged the Romans there. 25.2. And when they perceived that they were now in want of provisions, sending to Totila, they begged him to come there with the whole army, thinking that thus they would more easily and with less toil take both Perusia and the Romans there. 25.3. But Totila, seeing the barbarians not very eager for what was enjoined, wished to make some exhortation to them. 25.4. Wherefore, calling all together, he spoke as follows: “Seeing that you, fellow-soldiers, are not rightly angry at me, but are vexed at the opposition of fortune that has befallen, I have now resolved to gather you together, in order that, taking away from you an opinion by no means right, I may be able to set your judgement to the better, and that you may neither seem to be thankless toward me, which in no way befits you, nor use thanklessness toward the Deity through folly. 25.5. For human things are by nature wont sometimes to fail, and whoever, being a man, then shows himself ill-tempered toward what befalls, will himself bear, as is natural, the repute of ignorance, but will in no way escape the necessity arising from fortune. 25.6. And I wish to remind you of what was done before, not so much for the sake of dissolving the causes of what has befallen, but also to show that these more justly belong to you. 25.7. For Wittigis, at the beginning of setting himself to this war, razed the walls of Fanus and Pisaurus, the cities by the sea, but Rome and all the other cities of Italy he left unharmed in no way. 25.8. From Fanus and Pisaurus, then, nothing base befell the Goths, but from the wall of Rome and the other forts there, in the way that you know, affairs went against the Goths and Wittigis. 25.9. I, then, when I received the rule from your giving, reckoned that it was better to follow those of his deeds which seemed the better than to give harm from those that damaged affairs. 25.10. For by nature men seem to differ in nothing great from one another, but experience, becoming a teacher to some, sets the man who has learned by far above those who have not had the teaching. 25.11. Since, then, we took Beneventum, razing its walls we straightway mastered the rest, of which alike we resolved to raze the walls, in order that the army of the enemy might not, setting out from some strong place, be able to outwit the war, but might be compelled, coming down straight to us into the plain, to come to close quarters. 25.12. And they fled, while I ordered the cities that were taken to be razed. 25.13. And you, admiring the good counsel, served this judgement, making the deed, as is natural, your own. For he who praises the doer becomes none the less the author of what is done. 25.14. But now you have changed, O dearest Goths, since it befell that Belisarius, choosing daring beyond words, prevailed beyond expectation in his undertakings, and from this it befalls that you are struck with consternation at the man as being brave. 25.15. For the bold are more easily called daring than the provident safe. For he who has dared against established custom is honoured by the thought of his seeming energy, but he who, with a provident judgement, has shrunk from the danger, if he fails, draws upon himself the charge of what has befallen, and even if he accomplishes what he intended, seems to the ignorant to have wrought nothing himself. 25.16. And apart from these things, you do not reckon that you are angry at me on account of those things by which it befalls you now to be vexed. Or do you think that Belisarius has distinguished himself against you, who, being captives in war and having become fugitives, taking up arms while I was general, have often been able to surpass him in war? 25.17. And yet if it befell that you wrought those things by my valour, you ought, being ashamed of it, to keep silent, reflecting that in the failures of men nothing is wont to remain in itself; but if some fortune awarded that mastery to you, it will be more advantageous for you to revere it than to be ill-tempered toward it, so that, having given offence, you may not be forced to learn good sense over again. 25.18. And yet how would it not appear far from the temperate character that those who, not long before, were girt with many and great successes, having failed now in a brief matter, should thus have their spirit enslaved? For such a thing is nothing else than to refuse and deny to be men. 25.19. For to fail in no time at all would be the property of God. Laying these things aside, then, I say you ought to go against the enemy in Perusia with all eagerness. For if you should be able to take them, your fortunes will again be set fair. 25.20. For what has befallen not even all eternity could make undone, but when other successes come upon those who have failed, it befalls that the memory of their evils is abated. 25.21. And the mastery over Perusia will be for you with no toil. For Cyprianus, who commanded the Romans there, has been removed by fortune and our plans, and a multitude without a leader, especially when in want of the necessities, is by nature least able to play the man. 25.22. Nor indeed will anyone do mischief to us from behind; for the bridges of the river I have resolved to destroy for this very reason, that we may not suffer anything unexpected by a sudden raid, and it befalls that Belisarius and John are suspicious of each other, which it is possible to see through what they have done. 25.23. For purposes fighting with one another are convicted through their deeds. They have therefore by no means been able to mingle with one another to this time. For the suspicion of each toward the other holds back both. And in those in whom this is implanted, envy and hatred are of necessity settled. 25.24. And when these fall between them, it is impossible that anything of what is needful be done.” Saying so much, Totila led the army to Perusia, and coming there encamped close to the wall and, sitting down, set himself to a siege.

[156007] 26.1. While these things were being done thus, in the meantime to John, besieging the fort of Acherontis and accomplishing nothing, there came a thought and a daring which was able both to save the senate of the Romans and to gird himself with great and prodigious fame among all men. 26.2. For hearing that Totila and the army of the Goths were assaulting the wall of Rome, selecting the most notable of the horsemen, with no warning to anyone, he went, relaxing neither night nor day, to the Campanians (for there Totila had happened to leave them), in order that, falling upon them unexpectedly, he might be able both to snatch up and to save those of the senate, since the regions there were altogether unwalled. 26.3. Now it befell about this time that Totila, fearing, as happened, lest some of the enemy should come to do mischief to the captives, sent an army of horsemen to Campania. 26.4. And when these came to the city of Menturna, it seemed best to them that the most should remain quiet there and attend to the horses (for it befell them to have met with much weariness on this journey), but to send some to observe what was at Capua and the regions there. And the country between happens to be no more than three hundred stades. 26.5. So four hundred, who happened to have fresh horses and bodies, they sent for scouting. 26.6. And by some chance it befell on that day, about the same time, that the army with John and these four hundred barbarians were at Capua, neither having heard anything before of their opponents. 26.7. And a fierce battle suddenly took place; for the moment they saw each other, they came to close quarters; and the Romans conquered by force and straightway killed the most of the enemy. 26.8. And some few of the barbarians were able to flee, and going at a run came to Menturna. When the others saw them, some bathed in blood, others still bearing the missiles in their bodies, others neither uttering anything nor reporting any of what had befallen, but still gripped by their withdrawal and showing the fear in themselves, they straightway leaped onto their horses and fled with them. 26.9. And coming to Totila, they reported that the multitude of the enemy was beyond telling, healing in this way, of course, the shame from their flight. 26.10. Now there happened to be Roman soldiers, no fewer than seventy of those who had previously deserted to the Goths, going to the regions of Campania, 26.11. who resolved to come over to John. And John found there some few men of the senate, but nearly all the women. 26.12. For when Rome was being taken, many men, fleeing and following the soldiers, came to Portus, but it befell all the women to be taken. 26.13. Clementinus, however, a patrician man, taking refuge in one of the churches there, was by no means willing to follow the Roman army, since, having previously given over to Totila and the Goths a fort which is close to Naples, he feared the emperor's anger, as is natural; and Orestes, who had been consul of the Romans, happened to be somewhere close, but for want of horses was by no means willing to go, and remained there. 26.14. So those of the senate, together with the seventy soldiers who had come over, John straightway sent to Sicily. 26.15. And Totila, hearing this, was in great mourning and hastened to avenge himself upon John for this deed. Wherefore he marched against him with the greater part of the army, leaving a portion of his followers there for the sake of a garrison. 26.16. Now John happened to have made his camp with those about him, who were a thousand, among the Lucanians, and to have previously sent scouts, who, searching out all the roads, kept watch lest an army of the enemy should go against them to do mischief. 26.17. And Totila, having such a thing in mind, that it was not possible for John and his men to sit in the camp without scouts, leaving the accustomed roads, drove against them through the mountains, which there rise many, precipitous and exceedingly high—which one could not have suspected, since these mountains are reckoned impassable. 26.18. Now those sent for the scouting by John, perceiving that an army of the enemy had come about the regions there, but learning nothing clear concerning this, but fearing, as happened, drove themselves toward the Roman camp. 26.19. And it befell them to come there together with the barbarians by night. And Totila, now gripped by much anger and not provident judgement, reaped the folly from his rage. 26.20. For having with him an army tenfold that of his opponents, and it being plain that it is advantageous for a superior army to fight in the open, and that he ought rather to come to close quarters with the enemy at dawn, in order that they might not be able to escape notice in the dark, this he by no means guarded; for he would, having netted them all, have straightway taken his opponents; but gratifying his rage, at an unseasonable hour of the night he stood over the army of the enemy. 26.21. And none of them at all looked to resistance, since the most were still sleeping, yet the Goths were not able to kill many, but the most, rising, were able to escape notice in the dark. 26.22. And getting outside the camp, into the mountains which rise many somewhere close, they ran up and were saved. 26.23. Among whom was John himself and Arufus the leader of the Eruli. And there died about a hundred Romans. 26.24. Now there was with John a certain Gilacius by name, an Armenian by race, commander of some few Armenians. This Gilacius knew neither how to speak Greek nor to utter the Latin or Gothic or any other tongue except only Armenian. 26.25. Some Goths, falling in with him, inquired who in the world he was. For they were by no means willing to kill whoever fell in their way, in order that they might not be compelled to destroy one another in the night-battle, as was natural. 26.26. And he was able to answer them nothing else than that he was Gilacius the general. For the dignity, which he had happened to receive from the emperor, he was able to learn by often hearing it. 26.27. So the barbarians, perceiving from this that he was an enemy, for the moment took him alive, but not long afterward put the man to death. 26.28. John, then, and Arufus with their followers fled with all their might and came at a run to Dryus, and the Goths, having plundered the Roman camp, withdrew.

[162412] 27.1. So the armies among the Italians went on thus. And the emperor Justinian resolved to send another army against the Goths and Totila, led by the letters of Belisarius, since he urged him to this, often signifying their present situation. 27.2. First, then, he sent Pacurius the son of Peranius and Sergius the nephew of Solomon with some few men. And these, coming to Italy, immediately joined the rest of the army. 27.3. After this he sent Verus with three hundred Eruli and Varazes an Armenian by race with eight hundred Armenians, and, removing Valerianus the general of the Armenians from there with the guards and shield-bearers following him, who were more than a thousand, ordered him to go to Italy. 27.4. Verus, then, putting in first at Dryus and leaving his ships there, was by no means willing to remain there, where the camp of John was, but riding with those about him went forward. 27.5. For this was a man not in earnest, but for the most part given over to the disease of drunkenness, and from this always gripped by unconsidered boldness. 27.6. And when they came close to the city of Brentesium, encamping there they remained. When Totila learned this, he said, “Verus is one of two things: either he is using a great power, or he is gripped by much folly. 27.7. Let us go against him at once, then, in order that we may either make trial of the man's power or that he may perceive his own 27.8. folly.” Saying this, Totila went against him with a great army, but the Eruli, observing the enemy now present, took refuge in a certain wood that was somewhere near. 27.9. And the enemy, encircling them, destroyed more than two hundred, and were about to seize Verus himself and the rest who were hiding in the thorns, but a certain fortune that befell them saved them unexpectedly. 27.10. For the ships in which Varazes and the Armenians with him were sailing suddenly put in at the shore there. When Totila saw this, suspecting that the army of the enemy was more than it was, setting out immediately he drove away from there, and Verus and his men gladly came at a run to the ships. 27.11. And Varazes resolved to sail no farther forward, but came with them to Taras, where John the nephew of Vitalian arrived with the whole army not long afterward. These things, then, proceeded thus. 27.12. And the emperor wrote to Belisarius that he had sent him a large army, with which he ought, joining the enemy in Calabria, to come to close quarters. 27.13. And now Valerianus too, having come near the Ionian Gulf, thought it inexpedient to ferry across for the present at least. 27.14. For the provisions would by no means suffice for the soldiers and horses at that time of the year, since it was about the winter solstice. 27.15. And sending three hundred of his followers to John, he agreed that, having wintered, he too would come at the beginning of spring. 27.16. Belisarius, then, when he had read the emperor's letter, selecting nine hundred for their valour, seven hundred horse and two hundred foot, and establishing all the rest for the guard of the country there, and setting Conon over them as commander, immediately sailed for Sicily. 27.17. And from there he set out, purposing to put in at the harbour of Taras, having on his left the place called Scylaeum, on which the poets say Scylla came into being—not because the bestial little woman, as those men say, was there, but because a great quantity of pups, which they now call little dogs, happen to be there in the strait from of old even to my time. 27.18. For names, at the beginning, are always fitting to things, but report, carrying them around to some other men, makes there opinions that are not right, through ignorance of the true. 27.19. And time, going on thus, immediately becomes a strong artificer of the myth, and, as is natural, by the license of his art makes the poets witnesses of what did not happen. Thus the people of the place anciently named one promontory of the island of Corcyra which is to the east the Dog's Head, but others wish from this that the people there are dog-headed. 27.20. And indeed they call some of the Pisidians Lycocranitae, not because they have the heads of wolves, but because the mountain that rises there was called Lycu Cranus (Wolf's Skull). But let these things seem and be said in whatever way is pleasing to each. But I return to the point from which I digressed.

[166820] 28.1. Belisarius, then, was eager to go straight to Taras. Now there is a certain crescent-shaped shore there, where, the beach receding, the sea goes up, as it were in a gulf, far into the land. 28.2. And the whole sailing-course of this shore extends to a thousand stades, and on either side, by the outlet of the current, lie two towns: one, Croton, toward the setting sun, and Taras toward the rising. 28.3. And in the middle of the beach the city of Thurii is settled. And as the storm was against him and the wind, with much surge, forced him, and by no means permitted the ships to go farther forward, he put in at the harbour of the people of Croton. 28.4. So Belisarius, since he neither found any fort there nor had any place from which provisions might be brought for the soldiers, himself with his wife and the foot-soldiers remained there, in order that he might be able from there to summon and direct the army with John; 28.5. but he ordered all the horsemen to go forward and encamp by the entrances of the country, setting Phazas the Iberian and Barbation the spearman over them. 28.6. For thus he thought they would most easily provide all the provisions for themselves and their own horses, and would be able, as is natural, to repel the enemy in the narrow place. 28.7. For the mountains of the Lucanians, extending to the Bruttii and coming together in a narrow space, make there only two very narrow entrances, of which one is called Petra Haematos (Rock of Blood) in the Latin tongue, and the other the people of the place are wont to call Lavula. 28.8. There by the shore is Ruscianae, the port of the Thurii, and above it about sixty stades the ancient Romans built a most strong fort. This John had long before happened to occupy and establish a garrison worthy of account. 28.9. So the soldiers of Belisarius, going somewhere there, fell in with an army of the enemy, which Totila had sent to make trial of the fort there. 28.10. And coming immediately to close quarters with them, they routed them by their valour with no toil, although being inferior by far in number, and destroyed more than two hundred. 28.11. And the rest, fleeing, came to Totila and reported all that had befallen; and the Romans, encamping there, remained, and, being without a leader and victorious, used a more fearless mode of life. 28.12. For neither, being gathered, did they remain quiet, nor, sitting close to the narrow place, did they guard the entrances, but, turning to disregard, by night they slept scattered as far as possible from one another, and in the day went about searching for provisions, neither sending out any for scouting nor having deliberated anything else concerning safety. 28.13. Totila, then, when he learned all this, selecting three thousand horsemen from the whole camp, went against the enemy. 28.14. And falling upon them unexpectedly, not drawn up but going about thus as has been said, 28.15. he struck them all with consternation and threw them into confusion. There Phazas (for he happened to be encamped somewhere close), meeting the enemy and displaying deeds worthy of valour, became the cause for some of escaping, but he himself with all those about him died. 28.16. And this was a great disaster for the Romans, since all placed their hope in them as being pre-eminent in matters of war. 28.17. All who were able to flee, however, were saved as it became possible for each. And first Barbation the guard of Belisarius, fleeing with all his might with two others, came to Croton. And reporting the present fortune, he added that he thought the barbarians would be present immediately. 28.18. When Belisarius heard this, he was in great mourning and leaped into the ships. And setting out from there and meeting a wind, on that day they put in at Messana of Sicily, which is distant seven hundred stades from Croton and lies opposite Rhegium.

[170711] 29.1. About this time an army of Sclaveni, crossing the river Ister, wrought irreparable deeds upon all the Illyrians as far as the people of Epidamnus, killing and enslaving all who fell in their way, even to manhood, and plundering their goods. 29.2. And now they were able to take even many forts there which had seemed strong before, no one defending them, and went about everywhere searching them out at their will. 29.3. And the commanders of the Illyrians, having an army of fifteen thousand, followed, yet by no means dared to go anywhere near the enemy. 29.4. At that time, too, earthquakes often occurred in the winter season, exceedingly hard and prodigious, both in Byzantium and in other places, all by night. 29.5. And those who dwelt there, suspecting that they would be buried, were in great fear, yet nothing base befell them to suffer from this. 29.6. At that time too the river Nile, rising more than eighteen cubits, both flooded Egypt and watered the whole of it; but in the Thebaid, which is higher up, it gave its waters subsiding and withdrawing at the appropriate times to those who dwelt there to sow the land and attend to the other things, as was wont; 29.7. but from the land lower down, when it had once overflowed, it no longer departed, but, troubling it, passed the whole time of sowing—a thing which had not happened before in all eternity—and there were places where, the water having abated, it flooded again not long afterward. 29.8. And thus all the seed rotted, as much as had happened to be thrown into the land in the meantime. And the men were gripped by much helplessness at the strangeness of the calamity, and of the other animals the most were destroyed by want of food. 29.9. At that time too the sea-monster which the Byzantines called Porphyrius was taken. This sea-monster had troubled Byzantium and the regions about it for more than fifty years, not continuously, however, but leaving off, as it chanced, for a long time in between. 29.10. And it sank many ships, and threw the crews of many into confusion and forced them, carrying them very far off. It had been a care, then, to the emperor Justinian to master this beast. But he was by no means able to accomplish his purpose. How it now befell to be taken 29.11. I shall make plain. There happened to be great calm holding the sea, and a very great multitude of dolphins gathered somewhere close to the mouth of the Euxine Sea. 29.12. And these, suddenly seeing the monster, fled as each was able, but the most came to the outlets of the Sagaris. Some of them, then, the monster, overtaking, was straightway able to swallow. 29.13. And whether still gripped by hunger or by rivalry, it none the less pursued, until it fell, unawares, somewhere close to the land. 29.14. And there, meeting exceedingly deep mud, it forced and moved everything, in order that it might depart from there as quickly as possible, but was by no means able to escape this shoal, but became still more in the mire. 29.15. And when this came to all the dwellers around, going against it at once at a run, they cut at it with axes most continually on all sides, yet not even so did they kill it, but dragged it off with certain stout ropes. 29.16. And putting it into wagons they found it to be in length about thirty cubits, and in breadth ten. And there, tearing it apart in companies, some of them tasted of it at the moment, while others resolved to salt the portion that fell to them. 29.17. And the Byzantines, when they both perceived the earthquakes and learned what had befallen concerning the Nile and this monster, foretold straightway that whatever was pleasing to each of them would come to pass. 29.18. For men are wont, being at a loss before present things, to make wonders of the things to come, and, worn down by what troubles them, to conjecture for no reason what will befall. 29.19. But I, leaving the divinations and the interpretations of portents to others, know this well, that the staying of the Nile upon the land became the cause of great calamities at least for the present, but the disappearance of the monster appears to be a release from many evils. 29.20. And some say that this is not the monster of which I made mention, but another, which it befell to be taken. But I return to the point from which I made the digression of my account. 29.21. Totila, then, having accomplished these things which have been shown before, when he learned that the Romans in the fort at Ruscianae were running short of the necessities, thinking that he would take them as quickly as possible if they should not be able to bring in any of the provisions, encamped very close and, sitting down, set himself to a siege. And the winter was ending, and the thirteenth year of this war which Procopius has written was coming to a close.

[175538] 30.1. And the emperor Justinian sent foot-soldiers, no fewer than two thousand, to Sicily in ships, and ordered Valerianus to go to Belisarius with no delay. 30.2. And he, ferrying across, sailed down to Dryus, where he found Belisarius with his wife. 30.3. About this time Antonina the wife of Belisarius was sent to Byzantium to beg the empress to fit out the war with greater preparation. 30.4. But the empress Theodora, having fallen sick, was removed from among men, having survived for the empire twenty-one years and three months. 30.5. In the meantime those besieged in the fort at Ruscianae, hard pressed by the want of necessities, came to a parley with the enemy, and agreed to give over the fort about the middle of the summer season, if no help should come to them in the meantime, on condition that all should remain unharmed. 30.6. Now there were in this fort many notable Italians, and Deopheron the brother of Tullianus, and of the Roman army three hundred Illyrian horsemen, whom John had happened to establish there, setting over them Chalazar the spearman, a Massagete by race, exceedingly good in matters of war, and Gudilas a Thracian, and a hundred foot-soldiers sent by Belisarius for the guard of the fort. 30.7. At that time too the soldiers whom Belisarius had ranged for the guard of Rome killed their commander Conon, bringing against him as a charge his trafficking in grain and the other provisions to their own harm. 30.8. And sending some of the priests as envoys, they affirmed that, unless the emperor should grant them amnesty for this charge and pay within a fixed time the allowances which the public treasury owed them, they would, with no delay, come over to Totila and the Goths. And the emperor fulfilled their request. 30.9. And Belisarius, summoning John to Dryus, and gathering a large fleet with him and Valerianus and the other commanders, sailed quickly straight to Ruscianae, being eager to help the besieged. 30.10. And those in the fort, observing this fleet from on high and becoming hopeful, no longer resolved to come over to the enemy, although the day agreed upon for them had now arrived. 30.11. And at first, a prodigious storm falling upon them, and the shore there being altogether without a harbour, it befell that all the ships were scattered as far as possible from one another; 30.12. and in this way it befell that a length of time was wasted for them. And again, gathering in the harbour of the people of Croton, they put out for Ruscianae. When the barbarians saw them, leaping onto their horses they came about the beach, intending to prevent the landing of the enemy. 30.13. And Totila stationed them along the most of the shore facing the prows of the ships, some holding spears, others with bows drawn. 30.14. When the Romans saw this, they were dismayed and by no means dared to go near, but for some time, holding the ships at a distance, remained quiet; and afterward, despairing of the landing, all backed water and, setting out, put in again at the harbour of the people of Croton. 30.15. There, deliberating in common, it seemed best to them that Belisarius, going to Rome, should both settle matters there as well as possible and bring in provisions, while John and Valerianus, landing the men and horses and journeying by the road, should go to Picenum, to throw into confusion those of the enemy who were besieging the towns there. 30.16. For thus they had hope that Totila, breaking off the siege, would follow them. 30.17. John, then, with his followers, who were a thousand, did so; but Valerianus, fearing the danger, sailed with his ships around the Ionian Gulf straight for Ancon. For he thought that thus he would be in Picenum in safety and would join John. 30.18. But Totila by no means resolved even so to break off the siege, but himself sat there, while, selecting two thousand horsemen from the camp, he sent them to Picenum, in order that, joining the barbarians there, they might repel John and Valerianus. 30.19. And those besieged in the fort at Ruscianae, when the necessities had now altogether failed them and they had no longer any hope of help from the Romans, sending Gudilas the spearman and Deopheron the Italian to Totila, made an embassy concerning their safety, begging him to be forgiving toward them for what had been done. 30.20. And Totila announced that he would carry out the punishment on no other than Chalazar, as having disregarded what had previously been agreed, but that he would remit the charge to all the others. 30.21. Thus, then, he received the fort. And cutting off both the hands and the privy parts of Chalazar, he straightway killed him, and of the soldiers those who wished to remain he ordered to keep their own goods, on condition that for the future they should array themselves with the Goths on equal and like terms—which indeed he was wont to do in the other forts that were taken; 30.22. but those to whom remaining was by no means pleasing, these he ordered to depart wherever they wished, stripped, so that none of all men might campaign with him unwillingly. 30.23. Eighty of the Roman army, then, leaving their goods, came to Croton, but the others remained there with their goods. 30.24. From the Italians, however, he took away all their goods, but left their bodies altogether unharmed. 30.25. And Antonina the wife of Belisarius, the empress having died, coming to Byzantium begged the emperor to summon her husband there. And she accomplished this most easily. For the Median war, now pressing most strongly, was urging the emperor Justinian to this.

[181138] 31.1. About this time certain men plotted an attack against the emperor Justinian. How they came to this plan, and in what manner, being thwarted, they by no means came to the deed, 31.2. I shall now make plain. To Artabanes, who had overthrown the tyrant Gontharis, as has been said by me in the previous narrative, there came a prodigious desire to make Praejecta the niece of the emperor, whom he held betrothed, 31.3. his wife. And this was very much her wish too, not led to it by love of the man, but because she acknowledged that she owed him much gratitude, since he had avenged the murder of her husband Areobindus, and saved her and snatched her up when she was captive and not long after about to come unwillingly to the bed of the tyrant Gontharis. 31.4. And when both wished these things, Artabanes sent Praejecta to the emperor, but he himself, although established general of all Libya, needed the emperor, having invented certain unsound pretexts, 31.5. that he should summon him to Byzantium. For the hope of the marriage led him to this, showing forth many other good things from it, and that for the future he would not be far from the throne. 31.6. For men, having unexpectedly laid hold of prosperity, cannot keep their mind there, but await further things, and always go forward in their hopes, until they are deprived even of the good fortune that had befallen them, which they ought not. The emperor, however, fulfilled his request and summoned Artabanes to Byzantium, 31.7. establishing another general of Libya in his place, as has been related by me. 31.8. And when Artabanes came to Byzantium, the people marvelled at what he had done and otherwise loved him. 31.9. For he was tall in body and handsome, and his character was liberal and he uttered few words. And the emperor honoured him among the foremost. 31.10. For establishing him general of the soldiers in Byzantium and commander of the foederati, he enrolled him in the dignity of the consuls. 31.11. But to dwell with Praejecta he was by no means able. For he had a wife previously, who was a kinswoman and had come to him in marriage from boyhood. 31.12. Whom he had long before happened to put off himself, having perhaps fallen upon some cause for which it befalls wives to give offence to husbands. 31.13. This woman, so long as the affairs of Artabanes did not stand well, sat at home in all quietness, bearing the present in silence. But when Artabanes had now become both brilliant in his deeds and great in his fortunes, the woman, no longer bearing the dishonour, came to Byzantium. And becoming a suppliant of the empress, she saw fit to recover her husband. 31.14. And the empress (for she was by nature always inclined to favour unfortunate women) resolved that he should dwell, much against his will, with her, and Praejecta John the son of Pompeius the son of Hypatius made his wedded wife. 31.15. This calamity Artabanes did not bear gently, but grew savage and said that, having done such good for the Romans... no one allowed him, willing as he was, to lead in marriage the woman willingly betrothed to him, while he was compelled to be with the woman most hostile of all to him for all time; a thing which by nature most distresses the soul of a man. 31.16. Wherefore, indeed, not long afterward, as soon as the empress had been removed from among men, he gladly sent this wife away immediately. 31.17. Now the emperor happened to have his cousin Germanus, who had a brother Boraïdes. This Boraïdes the brother of Germanus had lately died, leaving the most of his goods to his brother and the children of that brother. 31.18. And though he had a wife and one daughter, he ordered the daughter to have only so much as the law compelled. Wherefore the emperor saw fit rather to support the daughter; a thing which especially galled Germanus.

[184985] 32.1. So matters about Artabanes and Germanus stood thus for the emperor. Now there was a certain man, Arsaces by name, in Byzantium, an Armenian by race, an Arsacid man, related by birth to Artabanes. 32.2. He had been detected a little before doing mischief to the state, and was plainly convicted of treachery, as acting in concert with Chosroes the king of the Persians for a revolution against the Romans. 32.3. And the emperor did him no other harm, but, scourging him a few times on the back, exposed him to public derision, mounted on a camel through the city, yet took from him nothing either of his body or of his substance, 32.4. nor indeed punished him with exile. But Arsaces, none the less being distressed at what had befallen, began to devise plots against both Justinian and the state. 32.5. And when he saw Artabanes distressed, as his kinsman, he provoked him still more, and, catching the man with the ambushes of words, reviled him, relaxing neither night nor day, and reproached him as one who had become brave and unmanly by turns. 32.6. For toward the evils of others he had become noble and a destroyer of tyranny, when, being a friend and a guest, he had taken Gontharis with his own hand and killed him for no reason. 32.7. But at the present time, having played the coward, he sat there so unmanfully, while his country was most continually held in guard and worn down by unaccustomed taxes, and his father had been slain on the pretext of agreements and a treaty, and all his kindred had been enslaved and were always scattered everywhere over the Roman empire. 32.8. But these things being so, Artabanes thinks it enough if he be general of the Romans and be called consul only. ‘And you,’ he said, ‘being my kinsman and having suffered irreparable things, in no way share my grief; but I pity you, most excellent man, for your fortune in both your women, both her of whom you have been deprived, which you ought not, and her with whom you have been compelled to dwell. 32.9. And yet it is not likely that anyone who has even a little spirit should shrink from the murder of Justinian, neither through shrinking nor through any dread, who sits unguarded for ever in some hall at an unseasonable hour of the night, together with the oldest of the priests, eager to turn over the oracles of the Christians. 32.10. But indeed,’ he said, ‘none of the kinsmen of Justinian will set himself against you. Germanus, however, the most powerful of all, I think, will help you eagerly together with his children, who are youths boiling with both their age and their spirit against him, whom I have hope of bringing of their own accord to the deed. 32.11. For they happen to have already been wronged by him in such things as neither we nor any other of the Armenians have.’ With such things Arsaces, always bewitching Artabanes, when at some time he saw that he had given in, brought the matter to another Persarmenian, 32.12. Chanaranges by name. Now this Chanaranges was a youth, handsome in body, but not in earnest, but for the most part childish. 32.13. So when Arsaces had joined both him and Artabanes in purpose and in words, he departed, promising that he would make Germanus and his children of one mind with them for the deed. 32.14. Now Justinus the elder of the children of Germanus was a youth and just growing his first beard, but energetic and keen toward action; 32.15. so that he had mounted into the chair of the consuls not long before. And coming to him, Arsaces said that he wished to meet him secretly in a certain church. 32.16. And when both came into the church, Arsaces first saw fit that Justinus should affirm by oaths that he would never report his words to any of all men except only to his father. 32.17. And when the man had sworn this, he reproached him because, being a nearest kinsman to the emperor, he saw market men and common men, who had no right to them, holding the magistracies of the state, while he himself, being already of such age that affairs were managed by him, overlooked not only himself but also his father, although he had come to the height of valour, and his brother Justinian, sitting in the position of a private man for all time. 32.18. And it had not even befallen him to come into the substance of his uncle, of which he had become heir, at least so far as Boraïdes had resolved, and then had been deprived of it, which he ought not, for the most part. 32.19. And yet it was likely that they would be overlooked still far more immediately, as soon as Belisarius should come from Italy. For he was reported to be already somewhere in the midst of the Illyrians. 32.20. Saying so much, Arsaces urged the youth to the plot against the emperor, revealing the agreements between himself and Artabanes and Chanaranges concerning this deed. 32.21. When Justinus heard this, he was thrown into confusion, and, being seized with dizziness, openly refused Arsaces, saying that these things ought never to be done either by himself or by Germanus his father. 32.22. And Arsaces reported what had befallen to Artabanes, while Justinus carried the whole account to his father. And he, taking counsel with Marcellus, who commanded the guards in the Palatium, deliberated about the matter, whether it was advantageous for them to report it to the emperor. 32.23. Now this Marcellus was most weighty in character and for the most part given to silence, doing nothing for the sake of money, nor enduring jests in words or deeds, nor otherwise rejoicing in a slack mode of life, but always having a hard life and alien to pleasure, yet an exact guardian of justice and a most ardent lover of the truth. 32.24. He, then, would not let the account be carried to the emperor. ‘For,’ he said, ‘it is inexpedient that you become the informer of this. For if you should wish to say anything secretly to the emperor, Artabanes and his men will immediately suspect that it has been reported, and if Arsaces should be able to escape unseen, the charge will remain unproved. 32.25. And I am by no means wont, unless I have inquired most exactly, either to believe myself or to report to the emperor. 32.26. I wish, then, either to be a hearer of the words myself, or that one of my friends, by your preparation, should hear the man saying something clear about these things.’ 32.27. When Germanus heard this, he ordered Justinus his son to act so that Marcellus' instruction might be carried out. 32.28. And he was no longer able to say anything about this to Arsaces, since he had openly refused him, as has been said by me. 32.29. But he inquired of Chanaranges whether Arsaces had come to him lately by the resolve of Artabanes. ‘For I would never,’ he said, ‘have dared to leave any of the secret things to him, being such as he is. 32.30. But if you should wish to tell me anything to the purpose, deliberating in common we might perhaps 32.31. do some good.’ Concerning these things Chanaranges, having taken counsel with Artabanes, carried everything to Justinus, all that Arsaces had previously happened to tell him. 32.32. And when Justinus agreed both to accomplish everything himself and to provide his father of one mind, it seemed best to Germanus to bring Chanaranges into conversation, and a fixed day was agreed upon for the dialogue. 32.33. Germanus, signifying this to Marcellus, saw fit that he should provide one of his friends to them, 32.34. who should be a hearer of the words of Chanaranges. And he provided Leontius, the son-in-law of Athanasius, a man who laid claim to the account of justice and exceedingly understood how to tell the truth. 32.35. Him Germanus, bringing into his house, seated in a chamber, where a thick curtain hung as a screen of the couch on which he was wont to feast. 32.36. And hiding Leontius within this curtain, he himself with Justinus his son remained outside. 32.37. There, when Chanaranges came, Leontius clearly heard him saying everything which had been deliberated by him and Artabanes and Arsaces. 32.38. Among which this also came into the discussion, that if they should kill the emperor while Belisarius was still on his way to Byzantium, nothing of what had been deliberated would proceed for them; since they wished to establish Germanus as emperor, but it was likely that Belisarius would gather a multitude of an army from the regions of Thrace, and thus, the man coming against them, they would by no means be able to thrust him off. 32.39. It would be needful, then, to put off the deed until the coming of Belisarius, and, as soon as the man should come to Byzantium and be with the emperor in the Palatium, then, it being late evening somewhere, bearing daggers, to come there unexpectedly, and to kill both Marcellus and Belisarius together with the emperor. 32.40. For thus they would for the future manage matters more fearlessly as they wished. When Marcellus learned this from Leontius, not even so did he resolve to carry the account to the emperor, but was still gripped by much shrinking, lest he should bring about the ruin of Artabanes unconsidered with much haste. 32.41. Germanus, however, carried everything to Buzes and Constantianus, fearing, as happened, lest from the delay he should incur some suspicion. 32.42. And many days later, when Belisarius was reported to be already somewhere close, Marcellus carried the whole account to the emperor, and he immediately ordered that Artabanes and his men be led off to prison, and entrusted the examination concerning them to some of the magistrates. 32.43. And when the whole plot had now come to light and was expressly in writing, the emperor seated all those of the senate in the Palatium, where they are wont to make their judgements about disputed matters. 32.44. And these, having read over everything which had happened to be told by those examined, none the less brought the charge against Germanus and Justinus his son, until Germanus, providing the testimony of Marcellus and Leontius, was able to dissolve the suspicion. 32.45. For both they and Constantianus and Buzes affirmed on oath that Germanus had hidden nothing whatever of these things on their account, but that everything had befallen just as has been related by me. 32.46. So those of the senate immediately voted to acquit both him and his son, as having done no wrong against the state. 32.47. And when all came inside before the emperor, the emperor himself, sharply angered, was vexed and grew prodigiously savage against Germanus, charging him with the slowness of the disclosure; and two of the magistrates, flattering him, agreed with his opinion and seemed to share his vexation; and in this way they greatly raised the emperor's anger, being eager to gratify him with the calamities of others. 32.48. But the others, cowering, kept silence, by not opposing yielding to him whatever he wished. But Marcellus alone, speaking uprightly, was able to save the man. 32.49. For drawing the charge upon himself and forcing it with all his might, he said that Germanus had reported what was being done most opportunely, but that he himself, searching too exactly, had disclosed it rather slowly. 32.50. And thus he stopped the emperor's anger. And great fame this Marcellus girt himself with among all men, as one who laid claim to valour in the most necessary matters. 32.51. And the emperor Justinian removed Artabanes from the office which he held, but did him no other harm, nor indeed any of the others, except indeed that he held them all in custody not without honour, in the Palatium, however, not in the public prison.

[196578] 33.1. About the time of this war the barbarians became expressly masters of the whole West. And the Gothic war for the Romans, although at first they had conquered by force, as has been said by me before, came to such a pass that not only did much of their money and many of their men perish for no benefit, but Italy too was lost in addition, and they beheld nearly all the Illyrians and Thracians being ravaged and destroyed in no order by the barbarians, who had become neighbours to them. And it came about thus. 33.2. The Goths gave the whole of the Gauls that were subject to them, at the beginning of this war, to the Germans, thinking that they would not be able to array themselves against both, as has been said by me in the previous narrative. 33.3. And this deed not only could the Romans not prevent, but the emperor Justinian even strengthened it for them, so that no obstacle from these barbarians, were they made hostile, might meet him. 33.4. For the Franks never thought that they held the Gauls in safety unless the emperor set his seal on this very thing. And from this the rulers of the Germans held both Massalia, the colony of the Phocaeans, and all the regions by the sea, and mastered the sea there. 33.5. And they sit in Arelate watching the chariot-contest, and have made a gold coin from the metals in the Gauls, not putting on this stater the stamp of the Roman emperor, as is the custom, 33.6. but their own image. And yet the king of the Persians is wont to make a silver coin in whatever way he wishes, but to put his own stamp on a gold stater is lawful neither for him nor for any other king of all the barbarians, and this though he be lord of more gold, since they are not even able to pass this coin on to those who deal with them, even if it befalls that those who deal with them are barbarians. So matters went for the Franks thus. 33.7. And when the affairs of the Goths and Totila became superior in the war, the Franks made the most of Venetia their own for no reason, the Romans being no longer able to defend it nor the Goths able to carry on the war against both. 33.8. And the Gepaedes took and held the city of Sirmium and almost all the Dacias, as soon as the emperor Justinian had taken them from the Goths; and they enslaved the Romans there, and going still ever forward on their way they plundered, doing violence to the Roman empire. 33.9. Wherefore the emperor no longer gave them the allowances which they had from of old been wont to receive from the Romans. 33.10. And the Lombards the emperor Justinian presented with the city of Noricum and with the forts on Pannonia and many other places and very great sums of money. 33.11. On account of this the Lombards, setting out from their ancestral abodes, settled thence beyond the river Ister, not far from the Gepaedes. 33.12. And they plundered and enslaved the Dalmatians and Illyrians as far as the borders of Epidamnus; and when some of the captives, fleeing from there, were able to be conveyed home, these barbarians, going about the Roman empire, as being in treaty with the Romans, whenever they recognized any of those who had fled, laid hold of them as of their own slaves who had run away, and dragging them off from their parents, conveyed them with themselves to their own homes, no one withstanding them. 33.13. And the Eruli too held other places of Dacia, the emperor giving them, about the city of Singidunum, where they are settled now, overrunning the Illyrians and the regions of Thrace and plundering them for the most part. And some of them became Roman soldiers, ranged among those called foederati. 33.14. So whenever envoys of the Eruli were sent to Byzantium, these men, who plundered the subjects of the Romans, having received all their allowances from the emperor, departed with no toil.

[200402] 34.1. Thus the barbarians divided up the Roman empire among themselves. And some time later the Gepaedes and the Lombards, since they dwelt somewhere near one another, became exceedingly at variance. 34.2. And being eager for war with all eagerness against one another, both sides were keen to come to battle with the enemy, and a fixed time was set for the engagement. 34.3. But the Lombards, thinking that they would not be a match for the Gepaedes by themselves (for it befell that they were inferior in numbers to the enemy), resolved to bring in the Romans to alliance. 34.4. So sending envoys to the emperor Justinian, they begged him to send them an army. When the Gepaedes learned this, they too sent envoys to Byzantium to beg for the same things. Now at that time Thorisin commanded the Gepaedes, and the other people Auduin. 34.5. And the emperor Justinian resolved to hear the words of both, not coming together, however, but coming to him separately. 34.6. First, then, the Lombards, coming into the emperor's sight, spoke as follows: “We are dismayed, O emperor, at the impropriety of the Gepaedes, who, having transgressed against your empire in matters so great in multitude and so great in magnitude, now come to you bringing you the greatest of insults. 34.7. For these men alone would do the utmost outrage to their neighbours, who, thinking them too easy to deceive, came to them to enjoy the simplicity of the wronged. 34.8. And we ask you to consider so much, in what state of mind are the affairs of friendship for the Gepaedes. For thus you would work out what is advantageous most safely for the Roman empire, since men are able with safety to judge of what is to be from what has always gone before. 34.9. If, then, it befell that the nation of the Gepaedes had displayed its thanklessness only toward certain others, we should have needed much argument and time and outside testimony, wishing to convict the character of these men; but now it is possible to take the example near at hand from you yourselves. 34.10. For consider: the Goths held the land of the Dacians in payment of tribute in earlier times, and the Gepaedes all dwelt at the first on the other side of the Ister, so cowed by the power of the Goths that they were never able even to make trial of crossing the river, but being in treaty and most friendly with the Romans, and receiving many gifts in the name of friendship each year both from those who reigned before, 34.11. and from you none the less. We would gladly inquire what good these men have ever wrought toward the Romans in return for these things. But they would not be able to say either great or small; 34.12. so long, then, as they had nothing on which to wrong you, not by any purpose, but compelled by inability, they kept quiet. 34.13. For you saw fit to lay no claim to the regions beyond the Ister, while the fear from the Goths drove them back from this side. 34.14. And who would ever call inability good will? What confirmation of friendship will arise in the impossibility of doing wrong? It is not so, O emperor, it is not. For power alone displays the nature of a man, bringing forth into public the character of all by the license to act. 34.15. For behold: as soon as the Gepaedes saw the Goths driven out of all Dacia, and you held in occupation with the enemy, the most abominable of them dared everywhere to mount upon your land.” 34.16. “How could one reach in words the impropriety of the matter? Did they not despise the Roman empire? Did they not break the ordinances of treaties and alliances? 34.17. Did they not insult those whom they ought least? Did they not do violence to an empire of which they would pray to be slaves, if any leisure should arise for you against them? The Gepaedes, O emperor, hold Sirmium and enslave Romans, and boast that they lay claim to the whole of Dacia; 34.18. having conquered what war on your behalf, or with you, or against you yourselves? Or having made this land the prize of what contest? And this though they have often been hired by you and have received money, as has been said, for time we know not how far back. 34.19. And yet of their present embassy there has been no act more abominable in all time. For when they saw us eager for war against them, they took heart to come to Byzantium and to come into the sight of an emperor so greatly insulted. 34.20. Perhaps, too, in the abundance of their shamelessness, they will invite you to alliance against us, who are so earnestly your friends. 34.21. And indeed if they come intending to give back what they have mounted upon, which in no way belongs to them, the Lombards must be reckoned by the Romans most responsible for the benefit arising from this, since, compelled by fear, they belatedly clothe themselves in unwilling good will. 34.22. For he who has obtained a kindness will, as is natural, know the gratitude to him who has made the necessity. But if they have not even now resolved to give up what does not belong to them, what could be the excess of this their evil character?” 34.23. “Let these things, then, be said by us with barbaric simplicity, sparing of words, in no way worthy of the matters. 34.24. But do you, O emperor, considering all that has been said by us more meagrely than the need, do what will be advantageous both to the Romans and to your Lombards, considering this above all else, that to us, who are of one mind with you about God, the Romans will at the first justly be arrayed in alliance, but to those who are Arians, and for this very reason, they will go in opposition.” 34.25. The Lombards said so much. And coming on the following day to the emperor, the envoys of the Gepaedes too spoke as follows: “It is just, O emperor, that those who come to their neighbours at a request for alliance should first show that they come to beg for things both just and advantageous to their allies, and thus make the speeches for which they make their embassy. 34.26. That we have been wronged by the Lombards, then, is plain at once; for we are eager to dissolve our differences by justice, 34.27. and it in no way belongs to those who are eager to do violence to plead at law. 34.28. And that in multitude and valour the Gepaedes happen to be by far superior to the Lombards, 34.29. why should one speak at length among those who know? But to go to a contest ranged with the inferior is to go to a manifest evil, when it is possible, ranging with the more powerful, to have the victory without danger; which we think none even of those who have a little prudence would choose. 34.30. So that, going against certain others, the Gepaedes too will for the future be arrayed with you, owing you gratitude for what has been done, and from the abundance of their power providing, as is natural, the mastery over the enemy. 34.31. And indeed it would befit you to reckon this also, that the Lombards have lately become friends to the Romans, while it befalls the Gepaedes to have been from above in treaty and known to you. 34.32. And a friendship interwoven by length of time has its dissolution not easy. So that you will obtain allies not only powerful but also firm. 34.33. These, then, are the pleas we have toward you for the alliance. But behold what the Lombards are in character. 34.34. To dissolve their differences by justice, though we challenge them often, they by no means resolved, being gripped by unreasoning boldness; but when the war is now somewhere at hand, and they, going back behind affairs because they share knowledge of their own weakness, come to you, seeing fit that the Romans take up the contest on their behalf, which they ought not. 34.35. By all means these robbers put forward to you Sirmium and certain other places in Dacia as the pretext of this war. 34.36. And yet of cities and land so much remains over to your empire that you even search out men to whom you may give a portion for dwelling. 34.37. The Franks, indeed, and the nation of the Eruli, and these Lombards, you presented with such 34.38. measures of city and land, O emperor, as no one could number. But we, having the courage of your friendship, have accomplished this, which was your wish; and he who has resolved to put forth something of his possessions thinks that he who anticipated and chose the gift by his own independent judgement is far better than he who has received the kindness from him, unless, not insulting the giver, but bold because he is most especially a friend to him, he seems to have made the request of the matter; which has indeed befallen to be so for the Gepaedes toward the Romans. 34.39. Considering which, we ask you, above all, according to the alliance, to go against the Lombards with us with all your might; but if not, to stand out of the way of both. For deliberating these things you both do what is just and what is exceedingly advantageous to the Roman empire.” 34.40. So the Gepaedes said so much. And the emperor Justinian, after much deliberation, resolved to send them away unsuccessful, but, having made a sworn alliance with the Lombards, sent them more than ten thousand horsemen, of whom Constantianus and Buzes and Aratius were commanders. 34.41. And there was with them also John the nephew of Vitalian, it having been told him by the emperor that, as soon as they should have fought against the nation of the Gepaedes, he should go from there with all speed to Italy with his followers. For he happened to have come himself from Italy. 34.42. And there followed them as allies fifteen hundred Eruli, of whom, among others, Philemuth was commander. 34.43. For all the other Eruli, who were about three thousand, had been ranged with the Gepaedes, since they had not long before happened to revolt from the Romans from a cause which has been told by me before. 34.44. And a portion of the Romans who were going to the Lombards for alliance fell in unexpectedly with some Eruli, with Aordus the brother of the ruler. 34.45. And when a fierce battle had taken place, the Romans conquered, and killed both Aordus and many of the Eruli. And the Gepaedes, learning that the Roman army was somewhere close, straightway dissolved their differences with the Lombards, and these barbarians came to terms of treaty with one another, the Romans unwilling. 34.46. When the Roman army learned this, they fell into much helplessness. For they were no longer able to go forward nor had they any means to turn back, the generals fearing lest the Gepaedes and Eruli, overrunning the land of the Illyrians, should plunder it. 34.47. Remaining there, then, they reported their present situation to the emperor. These things, then, were going on thus. But I return to the point from which I made the digression of my account.
