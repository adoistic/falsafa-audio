[0] Sidonius to his friend Constantius, greetings.

[48] 1.1.1. For a long time now, my honoured lord, you have been urging me with all the weight of your persuasion—as is fitting, since in matters that call for deliberation you are the most prudent of counsellors—that if any letters of a somewhat polished kind have flowed from my pen on various occasions, drawn out as they were by the subject, the person, or the moment, I should revise the copies, refine them, and gather them all into a single volume, treading with presumptuous steps in the footsteps of the rounded style of Quintus Symmachus and the discipline and ripeness of Gaius Pliny.

[640] 1.1.2. As for Marcus Tullius, I think it better to say nothing; for in the epistolary style not even Julius Titianus, writing under the names of illustrious women, reproduced him with any worthy likeness. On that account the rest of the school of Fronto, who imitated Titianus as one of their own, mocked him for copying a worn-out manner of speech and called him "the ape of orators." To all these men, I declare—it is past words how greatly—I have always yielded in my own judgement, and I have always proclaimed that to each of them the prerogative of his own age and merits must be preserved.

[1238] 1.1.3. But of course I have obeyed you, and have entrusted these letters to your scrutiny, not to be merely reviewed (that would be too little) but, as they say, to be purged and filed down, since I know that you are an immoderate patron not only of studies but of students as well. For which reason you now drive me, very hesitant as I am, out upon this sea of reputation.

[1613] 1.1.4. And yet over a little work of this kind I had been more safely silent, content with the opinion of verses published more happily than skilfully—an opinion in which, long since, the anchor of sufficient glory has settled for me in the harbour of public judgement, after I had sailed past the Scyllas of envious barkings. But if envy does not fix its grinding tooth in these trifles too, you shall presently have from me volumes more numerous, brimming with abundant discourse. Farewell.

[2107] Sidonius to his friend Agricola, greetings.

[2152] 1.2.1. Time and again you have asked that, since the fame of Theodoric, king of the Goths, commends his courtesy to the nations, I should describe to you in writing the proportions of his person and the quality of his life. I gladly comply, so far as the page of a letter allows, and I praise in you the refinement of so delicate a curiosity. He is indeed a man worth knowing even by those who see him only in passing; so richly have God, the arbiter, and Nature, our maker, combined to endow his person with the gift of perfect felicity. As for his character, it is such that not even the envy that attends royalty can defraud his praises of anything.

[2806] 1.2.2. If you ask about his appearance: he is well-built in body, shorter than the very tall, taller and more commanding than men of middle height. The crown of his head is round, and over it the hair, drawing back a little from the level of the brow, curls toward the top. His neck does not sag, slack and sinewless, but stands firm with sinew. His shaggy brows arch over twin orbs; and when his eyelashes droop, the rim of his lids reaches almost to the middle of his cheeks. The lobes of his ears, as is the custom of his people, are covered by the falling locks of hair laid over them. His nose is most gracefully curved. His lips are thin and not widened by any stretching of the mouth's corners. The hairs that sprout beneath the cavities of his nostrils are cut back daily. His beard grows thick in the hollows of his temples, but where it rises on the lower part of his face the diligent barber plucks it out by the roots from cheeks still smooth as a boy's.

[3774] 1.2.3. The skin of his chin, throat, and neck—not fat but firm—is milk-white, and on closer view is suffused with a youthful blush; for this colour comes to him often, not from anger but from modesty. His shoulders are smooth, his upper arms strong, his forearms hard, his hands broad; his chest swells forward while the belly recedes. The spine, set lower between the ridges of the ribs, divides the expanse of the back. Each flank is well-knit with prominent muscles, and in his trim loins vigour reigns. His thighs are hard as horn, the joints behind his knees thoroughly manly, the knees themselves least wrinkled and most comely. The calves are propped on swelling muscles, and the foot, which sustains such great limbs, is small.

[4511] 1.2.4. If you would inquire into his daily routine as it is displayed to the public eye: before dawn he seeks out, with a very small retinue, the assemblies of his priests and worships with great earnestness—though, if you could secretly observe, you might mark that he keeps this reverence rather from custom than from conviction. The rest of the morning he assigns to the care of governing the kingdom. An armed companion stands beside his throne; the crowd of his fur-clad guards is admitted so as not to be absent, but kept at the threshold so as not to make a din; and so they murmur before the doors, shut out by the curtains, shut in by the railings. Meanwhile, embassies of the nations being admitted, he hears much and answers little. If a matter is to be considered, he defers it; if to be dispatched, he hastens it. It is the second hour: he rises from his throne, either to inspect his treasures or his stables.

[5436] 1.2.5. If a hunt has been announced and he sets out, he judges it beneath the royal dignity to sling the bow at his own side; yet if, at close quarters, chance offers a bird or a beast to him as he hunts or travels, a servant places it in his hand drawn back behind him, the string or thong hanging loose; for as he reckons it a boy's task to carry the bow cased, so a woman's to receive it already strung. So, taking it, he now bends it with the ends drawn back in line, now—the knot turned to the dangling end—follows with his roving finger the loosened loop of the wandering string as it slips down; then at once he seizes the arrow, fits it, looses it; and he asks you first which mark you would have struck, that you may choose; you choose what he is to hit: what you have chosen, he hits. And if either of the two must err, the marksman's shot more rarely misses than the chooser's eye.

[6330] 1.2.6. If one comes to a banquet—which on ordinary days is like a private man's—no breathless servant there piles up upon the tables, as they give way beneath it, a rude heap of tarnished silver. The chief weight then lies in the conversation, since there either nothing is told or only serious things. The couch-coverings are displayed now in embroidery and tapestry, now in purple, now in fine linen. The food pleases by its art, not its costliness; the dishes by their cleanliness, not their weight. The rare offerings of cups and bowls thirst would more readily complain of than drunkenness refuse. In short: you may see there Greek elegance, Gallic abundance, Italian dispatch—public pomp, private care, royal discipline. But of that Sabbath-day luxury of his I must spare my account; it cannot remain hidden even from persons of lower station.

[7181] 1.2.7. To return to where I began. The feast over, his midday sleep is often none and always brief. At those hours, when the dice-board takes his fancy, he gathers the dice quickly, examines them carefully, rolls them shrewdly, throws them eagerly; he banters playfully, waits patiently; at good casts he is silent, at bad he laughs; at neither is he angry; in both he plays the philosopher. He scorns either to fear or to grant a second throw; he spurns the chances of these when offered, passes them by when set against him; without commotion he is escaped, without collusion he escapes. You would think that even over the counters he is handling arms: his sole care is to win.

[7862] 1.2.8. When it is time for play, he sets aside for the moment his royal severity and urges freedom and fellowship in the game. I will say what I think: he fears to be feared; and indeed he delights in the agitation of the man he has beaten, and only then believes that his opponent has not given way to him, when the heating of his rival's choler has given proof of his own victory. And—what you may marvel at—often that gladness, arising from the smallest occasions, brings good fortune to the deserts of vast affairs. Then petitions long tossed about through the shipwrecks of advocacy find the harbour of sudden release thrown open; then even I, when I am about to ask some favour, am happily beaten, since the board is lost to me for this, that my cause may be saved.

[8635] 1.2.9. About the ninth hour that mill of governing grows hot again; the petitioners return knocking, the ushers return forcing them back; everywhere the noise of litigious ambition roars, which, drawn on into the evening, thins out when the royal dinner interrupts it, and is then dispersed among the courtiers, each according to his patron, to keep watch until the hour of deep night. Now and then, though rarely, mimes' jests are let in during the meal, yet in such a way that no guest is wounded by the gall of a biting tongue. Even so, there is there no sound of water-organs, no choir of singers under a master intoning a rehearsed concert; no lyre-player, flautist, choirmaster, drummer, or harpist sings—the king being charmed by those strings alone whose virtue soothes the mind no less than song soothes the ear.

[9458] 1.2.10. When he has risen, the palace treasure begins the night-watches; armed men stand at the entrances of the royal house to keep guard through the hours of his first sleep. But what has all this to do with my part, who promised to tell you not much about the kingdom but a little about the king? At the same time it is fitting that an end be made to my pen, since you wished to learn no more than the man's pursuits and person, and I have taken care to make not a history but a letter. Farewell.

[9959] Sidonius to his friend Philomathius, greetings.

[10008] 1.3.1. Go now, and arraign me before the Senate under the law against canvassing for office, asking why with sleepless cares I press toward a dignity that is mine by inheritance—I, whose father, father-in-law, grandfather, and great-grandfather shone in the urban and praetorian prefectures, in the offices of the Palace and of the army.

[10347] 1.3.2. And behold, my friend Gaudentius, hitherto only of tribune's rank, has overtaken the yawning sloth of our fellow-citizens by the eminence of a vicariate. The trampled nobility of our young men does indeed mutter; but he who has passed them by is moved by their detractors only to this, that he rejoices. And so they now revere one whom they hitherto despised, and, astonished at the gifts of sudden fortune, look up from their seats to the man they once looked down upon from a session beside him. Meanwhile he beats the ears of the snoring detractors with the voice of his hoarse herald; and though they are stirred against him with the goads of enmity, they shall nonetheless be assigned to the benches of his friends.

[11076] 1.3.3. Hence it would be fitting that you too should swiftly repair the loss of your antiquated honour by the privilege of the prefecture's councils, in which you are summoned to share—lest, if you come into the council without the prerogative of a councillor, you should seem to have performed merely the functions of the deputies. Farewell.

[11420] Sidonius to his friend Gaudentius, greetings.

[11467] 1.4.1. Be blessed, most worthy sir, in the high office you have won by the merit of your deserts. To gain its titles and its eminence you counted out no maternal revenues, no ancestral largesses, no wife's jewels, no father's moneys; for, on the contrary, with you it was the sincerity examined, the diligence proved, the fellowship received at the prince's house that won you praise. O three and four times blessed are you, by whose elevation joy is given to friends, punishment to the envious, glory to posterity; and besides, an example to the brisk and eager, a spur to the idle and slothful. And yet, if there are any who will hereafter emulate you, in whatever spirit, they may perhaps, if they overtake you, owe it to themselves; but to you, beyond doubt, they will owe the very thing they pursue.

[12273] 1.4.2. I seem to see—with all reverence for good men premised—that arrogant idleness which puffs itself up in the envious, and that disdain of service familiar to the lazy, when, despairing of advancement, they play the philosopher over their cups and praise the leisure of the unhonoured, doing so from the vice of sloth, not from any zeal for perfection . . . . . . 1.4.3. . . . was rejected by the judgement of our elders, that it might not be of use to boys while they were still children. Thus those who compared the little declamations of young men to woven cloths understood that the brief sayings of the young are produced with more labour than long ones are pruned down. But since this is enough on this head, I beg you to remember what underlies it: that I wish to repay you in kind for this zeal of yours, if only God, favouring my just undertakings, grants me both safe and returning. Farewell.

[13181] Sidonius to his friend Heronius, greetings.

[13226] 1.5.1. I received your letter while at Rome, in which you anxiously ask whether the beginnings of my journey are advancing according to our common plan, and also what road I have taken and how I have made it—what rivers I have seen that are famous in the poets' songs, what cities renowned for the situation of their walls, what mountains made famous by the report of their deities, what plains made notable by the rehearsal of their battles—since you judge it a pleasure to learn things you have read of more faithfully from the account of those who have seen them. For this reason I rejoice that you wish to know what I am doing; for a curiosity of this kind springs from inner affection. Well then, even if some things should turn out otherwise, yet under God's help I will begin with what is favourable—the beginning with which our forefathers were wont to open even the reports of their misfortunes.

[14132] 1.5.2. When I had gone out from the walls of our own Rhône-city, the public post was at my service, since I had been summoned by the sacred letters, and it took me through the homes of my comrades and kinsfolk. There, in truth, it was not the scarcity of post-horses but the multitude of friends that delayed my journey—friends who, holding me in close embrace, prayed for my happy going and return with competing vows. So I drew near to the ridges of the Alps, whose ascent for me was swift and easy, and the paths, amid sheer cliffs that terrify on either side, were made soft by ways hollowed in the snow into a track.

[14755] 1.5.3. The rivers too—where any are not navigable—had convenient fords, or at least passable bridges, which antiquity had vaulted from their foundations right up to the embankment crusted with paving-stone, set on arches of masonry. At the Ticinus I went aboard a "courier" (such is the boat's name), by which, swiftly carried down into the Eridanus, I laughed at the Phaethontiads so often sung by us in our revels, and at the fabled tears of the tree's amber.

[15218] 1.5.4. The reedy Lambrus, the dark Addua, the swift Athesis, the sluggish Mincius, which rise in the Ligurian and Euganean mountains, I likewise viewed in their own streams, carried up a little against their mouths. Their banks and shoals were everywhere clothed with oak and maple groves. Here sounded the sweet concert of the birds, whose piled nests, set now among the hollow reeds, now among the sharp rushes, now among the smooth bulrushes, swayed; and all these thickets, fostered by the juice of the spongy soil, had sprung up in disorder above the river's margins.

[15792] 1.5.5. And passing by, I came in due course to Cremona, whose nearness was once sighed after by the Mantuan Tityrus. Then the town of Brixillum, where, as the Venetian oarsman gave place to the Aemilian taking over, we entered only to leave again; coming a little after to Ravenna by a course bearing to the right—a place where you might doubt whether the mid-road of Caesar joins or separates the old city and the new harbour. Moreover, one branch of the Po flows between the double town, another washes its side; for the river, sundered from its main channel by the barrier of public works and drained off through the same by diverted channels, so parts its divided streams that, flowing around the walls, they afford protection, and flowing in, commerce.

[16551] 1.5.6. Here, while everything convenient for trade was brought in, especially what was fit for eating, yet—since on one side the briny sea drove against the gates, and on the other the sluggish, lazy flow of the water, stirred by the running of the boats through the sewer-like mud of the ditches, was fouled by the slimy bottom pierced by the boatmen's poles—we thirsted in the midst of the waters, because nowhere was there pure water in an aqueduct, nor a cistern that could be cleared, nor a flowing spring, nor an unsilted well.

[17086] 1.5.7. Going on from there, I came to the Rubicon, which takes the origin of its name from the reddish colour of its gravel, and which was once the boundary of Cisalpine Gaul and old Italy, when the towns of the Adriatic sea were divided between the two peoples. From here I came to Ariminum and Fanum—the one made memorable by the rebellion of Julius, the other stained by the death of Hasdrubal; since there flows the Metaurus, whose felicity, won in a single day, stretches so far into time, as if even now it carried bloody corpses into the Dalmatian sea down its discoloured streams.

[17676] 1.5.8. From here I left behind, as soon as I entered them, the remaining towns of Flaminia, passing the Picentes on my left and the Umbrians on my right. There, whether it was the Calabrian Atabulus or the pestilent region of the Tuscans, with the breath of the air poisoned by venomous blasts and alternating now heat, now cold, infected my heated body; and meanwhile fever and thirst ravaged the inmost depths of heart and marrow. To their greed not only the pleasant springs or the hidden wells (though these too), but all those flowing waters near or in my path—the glassy Velinus, the cold Clitumnus, the dark Anio, the sulphurous Nar, the pure Fabaris, the turbid Tiber—we kept promising ourselves, though fear cheated the desire.

[18414] 1.5.9. Amid these things Rome at last lay open to my sight; whose forms—nay, whose mock sea-battles too—I seemed about to drink up. There, before I even reached the city limits, I cast myself down at the triumphal thresholds of the Apostles, and straightway felt all languor driven from my weakened limbs. After these proofs of heavenly patronage I was received in a part of a hired lodging, and even now, writing these things while lying down, I devote a little effort to rest.

[18894] 1.5.10. Nor do I yet present myself at the tumultuous doors of the prince and his courtiers. For I have arrived during the wedding of the patrician Ricimer, to whom the daughter of the everlasting Augustus is being joined in the hope of public security. So now, amid this rejoicing not only of persons but of orders and parties, it has seemed more profitable for your Transalpine friend to keep to one side—since at this very time, when these lines were being written for me, scarcely through all the theatres, markets, praetoria, fora, temples, and gymnasia could the Fescennine song of Thalassio be unfolded; and now, on the contrary, studies are silent, business rests, the courts are hushed, embassies deferred, canvassing at leisure, and the whole standing of serious affairs goes wandering abroad amid the buffooneries of the players.

[19736] 1.5.11. Already indeed the maiden has been given away, already the bridegroom lays aside his crown, the consul his embroidered robe, the bride's matron her train, the honoured senator his toga, the inglorious man his cloak; and yet the whole pomp of the bridal has not finished its uproar, for the new bride has not yet moved to her husband's house. When that festivity has run its course, the rest of the struggles of my labours shall be unlocked to you—if, that is, the completed solemnity shall at last have brought an end to this busiest idleness of the whole city. Farewell.

[20317] Sidonius to his friend Eutropius, greetings.

[20363] 1.6.1. Long ago indeed I was eager to write to you, but now most of all I am driven to it—now, that is, when by Christ's favour my road is being travelled that leads to the City—and the cause of my writing is either the only one or the chief one: namely, that I may call you, drawn out from the depth of your domestic quiet, to take up the duties of service at the Palace. . . .

[20743] 1.6.2. To this is added that by God's gift there belongs to you the unbroken vigour of an age, a body, a mind; and next, that, furnished with horses, arms, dress, money, and a train of servants, you alone, unless I am mistaken, are afraid to begin, and, though you are brisk at home, an inert despair makes you tremble at undertaking the journey—if indeed a man of senatorial seed, who is daily confronted with the robed images of his forefathers, can justly say that he has travelled abroad, when once and in his youth he has seen the home of the laws, the school of letters, the senate-house of dignities, the summit of the world, the fatherland of liberty: in which one city of the whole earth only barbarians and slaves are strangers.

[21483] 1.6.3. And now—for shame!—if you are to be left among the herdsmen, the rustics, and the snorting swineherds! For if you cleave the field with the quivering plough-handle, or, stooped and bent, lay low the flowery wealth of the meadow with the curved sickle, or, a stooping digger, turn over with mattocks the vine heavy with its shoot, then for you is the highest blessedness of your prayers. Nay rather, wake up, and let your mind, grown soft and slack with fat leisure, lift itself to greater things. It is no less worthy of a man of your birth to cultivate his own person than his country-house.

[22084] 1.6.4. Finally, what you call the exercise of youth is the leisure of veterans, in whose worn-out hands the rusty swords are late exchanged for the hoe. Be it so: the new wine shall foam for you from your multiplied vineyards; your barns, bursting with heaped grain, shall yield you countless mounds besides; a fat shepherd shall pen for you the close-packed flock with their heavy udders into the milk-pail through the reeking caves of the sheepfolds. To what end is it to have enlarged a patrimony by so dung-stained a profit, and not only amid such things but—what is baser—on account of such things to have lain hidden? Would it not be unworthy that, at the time of council, you, an inglorious rustic, an old man standing in hiding, should be put down by the vote of some poor man who holds office, while you behind the seated and voting young men—men whom it would be a disgrace if they followed in our footsteps—should see with grief that they have gone before?

[23053] 1.6.5. But why say more? If you will bear my urging, I will be the partner, the helper, the forerunner, the sharer of your endeavours. But if, wrapped in the alluring nets of pleasures, you would rather, as they say, be wedded to the doctrines of Epicurus—who, once virtue's loss is admitted, defines the highest good by bodily pleasure alone—then, behold, I call our ancestors to witness, I call our posterity to witness, that I am no party to this offence. Farewell.

[23523] Sidonius to his friend Vincentius, greetings.

[23570] 1.7.1. The case of Arvandus distresses me, nor do I conceal that it distresses me. For this too is added to the praises of the emperor, that it is permitted openly to love even men condemned to death. I was a friend to the man beyond what the easiness and changeableness of his character deserved; witness the envy lately stirred up against me on his account, whose flame scorched me when I was a little incautious.

[23987] 1.7.2. But in that I stood firm in friendship, I owed it to myself; whereas in his nature he had no care to persevere. I complain freely, not insultingly: for, despising the counsels of the faithful, he was in all things the plaything of fortune. In short, I marvel not that he fell at last, but that he stood so long. O how often did he himself boast that he had endured adversities, while we from a deeper affection pitied his rashness, bound one day to ruin him, declaring that no man is fortunate who is judged to be so frequently rather than always!

[24543] 1.7.3. But you demand the course of his condemnation. Saving the reverence due to good faith, which is owed to a friend even in his affliction, I will set out the matter briefly. He governed his first prefecture with great popularity, and the one that followed with the greatest ruin; weighed down alike by the burden of debt and by the fear of his creditors, he was envious of the magnates who would succeed him. He laughed at everyone's conversation, marvelled at their counsels, scorned their courtesies; from the rareness of those who met him he incurred suspicion, from his constant presence weariness—until, walled in by the mass of public hatred, and girt with a guard before he was ungirt of his power, he was seized and consigned and brought to Rome, swelling at once because he had sailed safely past the stormy coast of Tuscany on a prosperous course, as though the very elements served him in token of a good conscience.

[25477] 1.7.4. In the Capitol he was kept by his host Flavius Asellus, count of the sacred largesses, who still revered in him the half-smouldering dignity of the prefecture lately wrung from his hands. Meanwhile the envoys of the province of Gaul—Tonantius Ferreolus, of prefectorian rank, grandson through a daughter of the consul Afranius Syagrius, and Thaumastus too and Petronius, men endowed with the greatest knowledge of affairs and of words, to be ranked among the chief glories of our fatherland—came in pursuit of Arvandus, who had gone ahead, to accuse him in the public name, with the decrees in hand.

[26085] 1.7.5. Among the other things which the provincials had charged them to do, they brought an intercepted letter, which Arvandus's scribe, when arrested, confessed his master had dictated. This document appeared to be sent to the king of the Goths, dissuading peace with the Greek emperor, declaring that the Britons settled above the Loire ought to be attacked, affirming that the Gauls ought to be divided with the Burgundians by the law of nations, and a great deal more in much the same insane vein, such as to inspire wrath in a fierce king, shame in a peaceable one. This letter the jurists interpreted as burning with the crime of treason.

[26731] 1.7.6. This proceeding did not escape me and Auxanius, a most excellent man, who reckoned it the part of a treacherous barbarian and a coward to shun, amid his adversities, the friendship of Arvandus, in whatever way it had been incurred. So we report the whole machination quickly to him, who feared nothing of the kind—a machination which those keen and fiery men were planning with the highest artifice to conceal until the time of the trial, so that they might entangle their unwary adversary, who had spurned the counsels of his comrades and rashly trusted himself alone, in some headlong profession of his answer. We tell him, then, what seemed safe to us and to his more intimate friends; we advise him to confess nothing, however trivial, if anything, even the most trifling, were demanded by his enemies; that the very dissembling would be most thorny, by which they might the more easily kindle in him the deadly persuasion of security.

[27679] 1.7.7. On hearing this he sprang up, and bursting into sudden abuse: "Be off, degenerates," he said, "unworthy of your prefectorian fathers, with this needless alarm! Leave this part of the business to my care, since you understand nothing; Arvandus has enough in his own conscience. I shall scarcely deign to allow advocates to plead for me in actions of extortion." We departed in sorrow, confounded no less by grief than by the affront; for what physician is rightly moved when madness seizes a man given up for lost?

[28201] 1.7.8. Meanwhile our accused man went hurrying through the Capitoline square, robed in white; now feeding on cunning salutations, now gladly listening, as if recognizing them, to the rattling baubles of flattery; now searching through silks and gems and all the precious wrappings of the bankers, and inspecting, handling, beating down the price, turning over, as if about to buy; and in the midst of his doings complaining much of the laws, the times, the senate, the prince, that they did not avenge him before they examined.

[28730] 1.7.9. A few days passed, and in the council-chamber a full senate met (so I learned afterward, for I had departed meanwhile). Our man advances to the senate-house, a little before shorn and pumiced, while his accusers, half in mourning and unkempt, awaited the summons from the decemvirs, and, dirty by design, had snatched away from the accused the pity due to him, by casting the odium of squalor on him. The summoned are admitted; the parties, as is the custom, take their stand on opposite sides; before the opening of the charge the prefects are offered the right of sitting. Arvandus, with that unhappy impudence of his, at a quickened pace thrusts himself almost into the very midst of the judges' benches; Ferreolus, his colleagues standing about his sides, modestly and quietly sat down at the lowest end of the benches, so that he might remember he was no less an envoy than a senator—and for this he was afterward the more praised and honoured.

[29688] 1.7.10. While this went on, those of the magnates who had been absent came in: the parties rise, the envoys set forth their case. The letter, mentioned above, is produced after the provincial mandate; and when it was being slowly read out, Arvandus, not yet even questioned, cries out that he had dictated it. The envoys reply—though most maliciously—that it was agreed he had dictated it. But when the madman, ignorant how far he was falling, transfixed himself by his confession repeated twice and thrice, it is acclaimed by the accusers, proclaimed by the judges, that he is held as confessing himself guilty of treason; and to this he was put to the sword by a thousand formulas of the law that ordain it.

[30399] 1.7.11. Then at last the wretched man is said to have grown pale with belated repentance for his loquacity, learning too late that even one who has not aspired to the garb of the purple-clad may be pronounced guilty of treason. Forthwith, stripped of the privileges of the double prefecture which he had ruled through five years with renewed authority, and—restored, not so much added, to a plebeian family—he was adjudged to the public prison. This indeed was most pitiable: as those who saw it related, because he had brought himself, adorned and polished, before the judges while his accusers were in black, a little later, when he was led away condemned, the wretch was wretched yet not pitied. For who would be too greatly moved over the state of one whom he saw, trim and perfumed, hauled off to the quarries or the workhouse?

[31233] 1.7.12. But after the trial had been adjourned scarcely twice seven days, he was sentenced to death and cast onto the island of the serpent of Epidaurus. There, disfigured to the very grief of his enemies, and as it were spat out from human affairs by the vomit of a queasy fortune, he now, by the old senatorial decree of Tiberius, drags out a life of thirty days after sentence, shuddering hour by hour at the hook, the Gemonian steps, and the noose of the turbulent executioner.

[31716] 1.7.13. We, for our part, so far as we are able, absent and present, make our vows, and double our prayers and supplications, that, the blow of the already drawn sword being held back at the very instant, the Augustan mercy may count him half-dead, though with confiscation of goods or by exile. Yet of that man, whether he awaits each extremity or endures it, nothing is more wretched—if, after so many brands burnt in and so many insults, he now fears anything more than to live. Farewell.

[32209] Sidonius to his friend Candidianus, greetings.

[32257] 1.8.1. You congratulate me on lingering at Rome—yet as though wittily and with the salt of mockery mixed in; for you say you rejoice that at last your friend sees the sun, which surely I, a drinker of the Saône, have very rarely beheld. For you reproach me with the mists of my people of Lugdunum, and complain that our daylight, blocked by the morning fog, is scarcely unbarred by the noonday heat.

[32658] 1.8.2. And is it you who babble these things at me—you, a homebred slave rather of Caesena's smoke than of its town? Of whose native pleasantness, or rather of its profit, you yourself judged when you migrated away. And so it is that, exiled more happily at Ravenna, with your ears pierced by the gnat of the Po, the chattering throng of municipal frogs leaps about you. In which marsh, by a perpetual perversion of the order of all things, the walls fall and the waters stand, the towers float and the ships sit fast, the sick walk about and the physicians lie abed, the baths are cold and the dwellings ablaze, the living go thirsty and the buried swim, the thieves keep watch and the magistrates sleep, the clergy lend at interest and the Syrians chant psalms, the merchants serve as soldiers and the soldiers trade, old men play at ball and young men at dice, eunuchs study arms and the federate troops study letters.

[33581] 1.8.3. See, then, what sort of city it is in which your household hearth is settled—one that could more easily have a territory than land. Wherefore remember that the harmless Transalpines must be spared, who, content with the dowry of their own sky, are granted no great glory if they shine by comparison with worse places. Farewell.

[33917] Sidonius to his friend Heronius, greetings.

[33962] 1.9.1. After the wedding of the patrician Ricimer—that is, after the resources of both empires had been scattered to the winds—a return was at last made to public seriousness, which opened a door and a field for the transacting of affairs. Meanwhile we, received into the hearth of Paulus, a man of prefectorian rank to be revered as much for his learning as for his sanctity, were graced with the kindly offices of his gracious hospitality. And no one is more excellent than that man in every kind of art. Good God, what riddles he makes in his propositions, what figures in his sentences, what neat phrases in his verses, what devices with his fingers! Yet this surpasses in him the very summit of all his studies: that he keeps a conscience superior to his eminent knowledge. So through him first I explored whether there was any access of favour into the court; with him I conferred which of the magnates might best be able to aid our hopes.

[34909] 1.9.2. Nor indeed was there much delay, since there were few about whose patronage, were it to be chosen, one might hesitate. There were indeed in the senate many men refined by wealth, exalted by birth, weighty in age, useful in counsel, raised by rank, sociable by courtesy; but—saving the standing of the rest—two most exalted men of consular rank, Gennadius Avienus and Caecina Basilius, were conspicuous above the others. These, in that most ample order, setting aside the prerogative of the military party, were easily the chief men after the purple-clad prince; but among these two also, though both were astonishing, their characters were various, and there was a likeness rather of genius than of disposition. Let me say, briefly, something about them.

[35672] 1.9.3. Avienus had come to the consulship by good fortune, Basilius by virtue; and so in Avienus a pleasant swiftness of dignities was praised, in Basilius a late abundance of them. Each, when by chance they went out from their houses, was hemmed in by a crowd of clients going before, following after, poured around; but, in equals, the hopes and spirits of their comrades were far unequal. Avienus, if he had any power, exerted it in advancing his sons, sons-in-law, and brothers; and since he was always distracted by his domestic candidates, he was less effectively able to dispatch the needs of suitors from outside.

[36295] 1.9.4. And in this the Decian family was preferred to that of the Corvini: that such favours as Avienus, while in office, obtained for his own, Basilius, out of office, conferred upon strangers. Avienus's mind was opened wholly and quickly, but with less profit; Basilius's to few and slowly, but with more advantage. Neither was difficult of access, neither costly; but if you courted both, you would more easily get friendship from Avienus, more easily a benefit from Basilius.

[36776] 1.9.5. After long weighing both on either hand, the mutual deliberation settled it thus: that, the reverence due to the elder consular being reserved—into whose house we came not too seldom—we should rather attach ourselves to the company of Basilius's frequenters. And so, while through this most ample man we were working out something of the petitions of the Arvernian embassy, behold, the Kalends of January, which awaited the name, to be entered in the records, of the Augustus soon to be consul.

[37279] 1.9.6. Then my patron: "Come now, my Sollius," he said, "though you are pressed by the burden of the office you have undertaken, I would have you put forth your old Muse in homage to the new consul, composing some votive piece even with hasty strings. I will provide access by getting you admitted, comfort to you as you recite, and support to you as you recite; if you believe one who has tried it, many serious matters will be advanced for you by this play." I obeyed his instructions; he did not withhold his favour from the task enjoined, and as the unconquerable surety of the devotion he had imposed he so dealt with my consul as to make me prefect over his senate.

[37952] 1.9.7. But you, unless I am mistaken, weary of the letter's length, will now more pleasurably dwell upon re-reading the verses of the little work itself. I know it; and on this account the talkative document carries the poem itself in what follows, to converse with you, in my stead, for a few days, until I come. If you would grant the poem too the points of your verdict, it would be as welcome to me as if, while I declaimed in the Comitium or among the Rostra, not only the broad-striped senators but the crash of my tribe-fellows too were roused to my "Bravo!" Yet I warn you, and forewarn you, by no means to set those trifles of mine on a level, in your hexameters, with the work of others. For my songs, compared with yours, will rightly be likened not to the trappings of heroic verse but to the dirges of epitaph-makers.

[38784] 1.9.8. Nevertheless rejoice that this very panegyric received, if not the verdict, at least the outcome of a good work. Wherefore, if grim things must be sweetened with jests, I would close the page with a glorious—that is, a Thrasonic—ending, in imitation of the Plautine Pyrgopolinices. So then, since by Christ's help I have come to the prefecture by the occasion of my pen, you are of course bidden, with the authority of a girt official, having gathered up from every side the acclamations of all praises, to bear me to the stars: if I please, my eloquence; if I displease, my good fortune. I seem to see you laughing, because you perceive that our boastfulness has run wild along with the comic soldier. Farewell.

[39505] Sidonius to his friend Campanianus, greetings.

[39553] 1.10.1. I have received your letter through the prefect of the grain-supply, in which you commend him to me, the new judge, as your old comrade. I give great thanks to him, the greatest to you, that you both have resolved either to presume safely upon my friendship or to trust it unimpaired. For my part I embrace the acquaintance and intimacy of the man not only willingly but eagerly, since I know that our own good favour too will be made the closer by this service of mine.

[40033] 1.10.2. But do you also commend to his vigilance my standing—that is, the standing and cause of my reputation; for I fear lest the roar of the theatre's hollow benches should sound out the hunger of the Roman people, and the public famine be reckoned to my misfortune. [40692] Sidonius to his friend Montius, greetings.

[40736] 1.11.1. You ask, most eloquent sir, as you are about to seek out your own Sequani, that some satire or other, if it has been written out by me, be sent to you. And indeed I marvel that you should have made this request; for it is not right that you should so quickly think ill of a friend's character. Was I likely to brood over such a theme—I, now at an age and amid a leisure such that to have written it as a young man on active service would have been presumptuous, and to have published it dangerous? For to whom did that Calabrian poet not say, even to one greeting his teacher: "If a man has composed evil verses against another, there is right and judgement to be had"?

[41415] 1.11.2. But lest you should believe anything further of the kind about your friend, what that was which the rumour and smoke of a sinister opinion breathed upon me I will set forth somewhat at length, but from the beginning.

[41641] In the days of Augustus Majorian there came into the midst of the court a document, but lacking any author's name, full of biting satiric verses—verses which, abusing quite invectively the bare mention of names, carped much at vices, more at men. At this the people of Arles, where the affair was taking place, began to murmur, and to ask which of the poets the weight of public fury deserved to crush—chiefly at the instigation of those whom the uncertain author had exasperated by certain marks of identity.

[42152] 1.11.3. It happened by chance that Catullinus, then an illustrious man, came thither from Auvergne—always dear to me, but then especially through our recent companionship in service; for travel often makes fellow-citizens into closer friends. And so both Paeonius and Bigerrus laid this snare for the unsuspecting man: that before many witnesses they should ask him, as though of one unwary, whether he recognized this new poem. And he: "If you recite it." And when they poured out various scraps as if in jest, Catullinus dissolved into laughter and with untimely approval began to cry out that it was a poem worthy that the rostral tablet, or even the Capitoline, should justly receive it, to be made everlasting in golden letters.

[42887] 1.11.4. Paeonius blazed up—he on whom that satirist had the more eagerly pressed the bite of his fiery tooth—and to the bystanders: "I have found, now," he said, "the man guilty of our common injury. You see how Catullinus is dying of laughter: it is plain that he recalls something familiar to him. For what cause has driven him to hurl out a hasty verdict, unless that he already holds the whole, who judges thus from a part? And besides, Sidonius is now in Auvergne; whence it is gathered that, with him for author and this man for hearer, the thing was woven." There is a rush into fury and into abuse of the absent, the ignorant, the innocent; nothing is reserved for conscience, for good faith, for inquiry. So a popular personage drew the levity of the light crowd whithersoever he would.

[43684] 1.11.5. For Paeonius himself was wholly the people's man, who with his tribunician blasts often drove the sea of seditions. But if you had inquired who he was by birth, whence in his home, he was born no more eminently than from a town family, and one whom, at the outset, the renown of his stepfather rather than of his father had made known. Yet, again and again, by fair means and foul, aspiring to grow, and by avarice the parent of money, by ambition prodigal: for, that he might be joined to a higher family at least through his daughter—most honourable though she was—our Chremes had, against the strictness of civic custom, promised, as they say, a splendid dowry to his Pamphilus.

[44375] 1.11.6. And when the Marcellinian conspiracy for seizing the diadem was being cooked up, he had offered himself as standard-bearer to the youth of the nobility in the faction—a man still new in his old age—until at last, on account of proofs of his fortunate audacity, the chink of the gaping interregnum gave a flash to the obscurity of his birth. For with the court empty and the commonwealth disturbed, he alone was found who dared to gird himself for the governing of the Gauls with the rods of office before the commission, and for many months mounted the tribunal of the illustrious powers as a respectable prefect; then, the year of his last service being completed, he was scarcely honoured at its very end—after the manner of the accountants, or rather of the advocates, whose dignities begin just when their actions end.

[45207] 1.11.7. So this man, prefectorian thus, senator thus—whose character, if I do not pay the praises that befit it in full, I owe to the character of his son-in-law—stirred up the hatreds of many men, more than good men, against me, who was still ignorant, still his friend, as though I alone in my age were able to wield the power of verse. I come to Arles, suspecting nothing as yet (for whence should I?), although I was thought by my enemies unlikely to be present; and, having seen the prince after a day, I go down into the forum as usual. When this was seen, at once, as the poet says, the sedition, daring nothing boldly, took fright. Yet some flung themselves at my knees more than was fitting; others, that they might not greet me, fled behind the statues, hid behind the columns; others, gloomy and downcast, walked with their sides pressed to mine.

[46066] 1.11.8. Here I marvelled what this should mean—this form of excessive pride in those, of excessive humility in these—nor yet did I of my own accord ask the reasons, when one of the factious crowd, suborned, presents himself to me to be greeted. Then, as the talk went on: "Do you see these men?" he said. And I: "I see them," I said, "and indeed marvel at their gestures, yet do not admire them." To this our interpreter: "As a writer of satire," he said, "they either curse you or dread you." "Whence? Why? When?" I replied; "who has recognized the crime? Who reported it? Who proved it?" And presently, smiling: "Go, friend," I said, "if it is no trouble, and deign to consult these swollen men in my name, whether that informer or denouncer who has falsely charged that I wrote a satire has also falsely charged that I published it; whence perhaps it may be safer, if they reconsider, for them to cease their pride."

[46987] 1.11.9. When the messenger reported this, straightway all of them—not modestly nor singly, but hastily and in throngs—gave me kisses and their right hands. Only my friend Curio, having inveighed against the treachery of the deserters, when evening came on, was carried off and brought home by chair-bearing slaves more foul than corpse-handlers.

[47334] 1.11.10. On the next day Augustus ordered that we should be present at his banquet during the circus-games. First, on the left horn of the couch, lay Severinus, consul for the year—a man who, amid the vast upheavals of princes and the unequal state of the commonwealth, was ever equal in favour; next to him Magnus, lately ex-consul, before that ex-prefect, a person doubled by his twin honours; reclining behind him Camillus, his brother's son, who, having himself run through two dignities, had likewise adorned his father's proconsulate and his uncle's consulate. Paeonius on this side, and on this Athenius, a man practised in the changes of lawsuits and of times; this one was followed by Gratianensis, a man to be set apart from all infamy, who, just as he came after Severinus in honour, so had gone before him in favour. Last of all lay I, where the left side of the purple-clad emperor stretched out on the right margin.

[48265] 1.11.11. When much of the meal was over, Caesar's talk was directed to the consul, and that briefly; thence it rolled down to the ex-consul; with whom, often taken up again because it had turned to literature, it passed from the occasion to the illustrious Camillus—so far that the prince said: "Truly you have an uncle, brother Camillus, on whose account I congratulate myself that I have conferred one consulate on your family." Then he, who wished for some such thing, watching his moment: "Not one, lord Augustus," he said, "but the first." With the greatest uproar—so that not even reverence for Augustus stood in the way—the saying was received.

[48918] 1.11.12. Then, asking Athenius something or other, the Augustan address passed over Paeonius, who had been placed above him—whether by chance or by design I do not know. And when Paeonius had taken this ill, basely—and what was baser, since the man addressed kept silence, made answer himself—Augustus smiled, since, while keeping his authority, he had given himself to fellowship and was full of jests; through which laughter no less of vengeance fell to Athenius than of injury had befallen him. So the shrewd old man gathered himself together, and, as he was ever within seething with the heat of shame that Paeonius should be preferred to him: "I do not marvel, Augustus," he said, "if he tries to snatch from me the place of standing, who does not blush to invade your place of speaking."

[49713] 1.11.13. And the illustrious Gratianensis: "A wide field," he said, "is opened to satirists by this quarrel." Here the emperor, turning his neck to me: "I hear, count Sidonius," he said, "that you write satire." And I: "I too hear this, my lord prince." Then he, but laughing: "Spare us at least." But I: "In that I abstain from what is unlawful, I spare myself." After which he: "And what shall we do with these," he said, "who provoke you?" And I: "Whoever this man is, my lord emperor, let him accuse me publicly: if I am refuted, let us, convicted, pay the deserved penalties; but if I shall have quashed the charges not improbably, I beg that, by the indulgence of your clemency, I may—saving the injury of the law—write against my accuser what I will."

[50473] 1.11.14. At this he himself, catching sight of Paeonius, began with a nod to consult him as he wavered, whether the condition pleased him. But when the other, confounded, held his peace, and the prince was consulting him as he blushed, he said: "I grant your request, if you ask for this very thing on the spot in verse." "Let it be," I said; and turning around, as if I were asking for water for my hands, and delaying only so long as the swift servants run around the circle of the couch, I gave my elbow back to the cushion. And the emperor: "You had promised that you would ask leave to write satire in impromptu verses." And I: "Whoever blames me, greatest prince, for writing satire—this I beg you to decree: let him either approve it or fear it."

[51228] 1.11.15. There followed an uproar—unless to say so is boastfulness—equal to that over Camillus's saying, which indeed earned it not so much by the dignity of the poem as by the brevity of the time. And the prince: "I call God and the public state to witness that I will henceforth never forbid you to write what you will, since the crime laid upon you can in no way be proved; and at the same time it is most unjust that a purple-clad man's verdict should grant this much to private feuds, that an innocent and untroubled nobility should be endangered, on account of certain hatreds, by an uncertain charge." When I, modestly with bowed head, was giving thanks at this very sentence, the face of my haranguer began to grow pale—a face on which, a little before, after anger, sadness had succeeded; and it lacked little but that they froze, as though bidden to offer their necks to the drawn sword.

[52127] 1.11.16. After this scarcely a few words more: we rose. We had advanced a little from the emperor's sight, and were even now putting on our cloaks, when the consul fell upon my breast, the prefectorians upon my hands; that very accused man, my friend, again and again abjectly humbled himself, while all pitied him, so that I feared lest he should stir up against me by his entreaty the ill-will which he had not roused by his accusation. I said at last, pressed by the entreaty of the assembled magnates, that he should know that nothing would be returned to his attempts in verse—that I would yet detract from my own acts in future, if he had been spared; for it ought to suffice that the charge of satire had won me fame, but him infamy.

[52869] 1.11.17. In sum, I overthrew, my honoured lord, not so much the maintainer of the calumny as the mutterer of it; but since satisfaction was made to me in such a way that so many summits and rights of powers and dignities were bowed before my breast for his guilt, I confess that the beginning of such an affront was well worth its price, whose end was glory. Farewell.

[53239] Sidonius to his friend Ecdicius, greetings.

[53284] 2.1.1. Two evils now your Auvergne endures at once. "What are they?" you ask: the presence of Seronatus and your own absence. Of Seronatus—to begin even with his name—Fortune seems to me to have played, as if foreknowing what was to come; just as, on the contrary, our forefathers called those battles "wars" (bella) than which nothing is fouler, and by a like contrariety named the Fates "Parcae" (the Sparing ones), because they do not spare. The very Catiline of our age has lately returned from the country of the Aturres, that here he might mix in full the blood and fortunes of the wretched, which there he had in part begun to drain.

[53926] 2.1.2. Know that in him, day by day, the spirit of a long-dissembled madness is being laid bare: he envies openly, dissembles abjectly, lords it slavishly, decrees as a master, exacts as a tyrant, condemns as a judge, slanders as a barbarian. All day long, armed by fear, fasting from greed, terrible from cupidity, cruel from vanity, he never ceases at once both to punish and to commit thefts; openly, and with laughers summoned, he belches out among citizens talk of battles, among barbarians talk of letters. His letters—he being not even sufficiently initiated in the first rudiments—he publicly dictates out of boastfulness and corrects out of shamelessness.

[54592] 2.1.3. Everything he covets he as it were buys, neither paying the price, which he scorns, nor receiving the deeds, which he despairs of. In the assembly he commands, in the council he is silent; in church he jests, at the banquet he preaches; in the bedchamber he condemns, at the inquiry he dozes. Daily he fills the woods with fugitives, the country-houses with guests, the altars with the accused, the prisons with clergy; exulting over the Goths and insulting the Romans, mocking the prefects and conniving with the accountants, trampling the Theodosian laws and proclaiming the Theodoricean, he hunts out old faults and new tributes.

[55233] 2.1.4. Therefore unwind your delays quickly, and cut through whatever it is that holds you back. The last liberty of your quivering fellow-citizens awaits you. Whatever is to be hoped, whatever to be despaired of, they are content that it be done with you in the midst, with you as president. If there are no forces from the commonwealth, no garrisons, if there are none—as rumour goes—of the resources of the prince Anthemius, the nobility has resolved, on your authority, either to give up its fatherland or its hair. Farewell.

[55764] Sidonius to his friend Domitius, greetings.

[55809] 2.2.1. You make it a charge against me that I am in the country, when I might rather complain that you are now kept in the city. Already spring gives way to summer, and the sun, raised along its outermost lines, is stretched toward the Scythian pole with a wandering ray. Here, what shall I say of the climate of our region? whose tracts the divine craftsman has so stretched out that we are the more subjected to the heats of the western world. What more? The world has grown white-hot: the Alpine ice is melted, and the earth is written over with the gaping bends of parched cracks; the gravel lies foul in the shallows, the mud on the banks, the dust on the fields; the very water, wherever it slides in a perpetual course, languishes and lingers; now the wave not only grows warm but is boiled.

[56609] 2.2.2. And now, while one man sweats in linen, another in silk, you—muffled in a thick cloak without and swathed in bands within, and moreover hemmed in the hollow chair of the man of Ameria—begin, yawning, to expound to pupils as pale with the heat no less than with fear: "Samian was my mother." Why rather, if anything wholesome is dear to your heart, do you not swiftly slip away from the panting straits of the city, and, gladly inserted into our company, cheat the dog-day inclemency by a most clement retreat?

[57127] 2.2.3. If indeed it is your pleasure, hear what is the situation of the estate to which I invite you. We are at Avitacum: this is the name of the property, which, because it came to me with my wife, is sweeter to me than my paternal home. There I have, by God's favour—unless you fear some evil eye—concord with my household. A mountain on the west, earthen though it is, yet steep, pours forth from itself lower hills, as from a twin source, drawn off from it for about four acres in breadth. But until the level space of a forecourt fitting for the dwelling opens out, the sides of the slopes accompany the middle valley in straight lines right up to the edge of the villa, which is extended with its fronts turned to north and south.

[57865] 2.2.4. The bath clings, on the south-west, to the roots of a wooded rock, and when the wood for cutting is felled on the ridge, it is hurled, as if by a spontaneous fall, with the falling heaps into the mouth of the furnace. From here rises the chamber of the heated waters, which squares with the anointing-room that follows it in an equal extent of space—except for the semicircle of the capacious basin, where the force of the boiling water, wound through a wall pierced with channels of flexible lead, sobs as it comes. Within the heated room there is solid daylight, and such an abundance of enclosed light that it compels all modest persons to think themselves something more than naked.

[58560] 2.2.5. From here the cold-room is spread out, which might not immodestly rival the swimming-pools built by public works. First, the peak of the roof being pointed into a cone, while the ridges of the crests meeting in four directions from the angles are tiled with intervening tiles (the room itself is squared with most exact spaciousness in fitting measures, so that, without the service of the attendants hindering one another, it can receive as many chairs as the semicircular couch is wont to hold persons), the builder set two windows opposite each other at the junction of the hanging vault, so as to open the skilfully wrought panelled ceiling to the gaze of those who look up. The inner face of the walls is content with the bare whiteness of the smoothed plaster.

[59335] 2.2.6. There no base scene stands forth in the naked beauty of painted bodies, which, as it adorns the art, so it disgraces the artist. Absent are the actors, ridiculous in dress and faces, counterfeiting with many-coloured pigments the stock of Philistio; absent are the slippery and writhing wrestlers in their bouts and grips, whose struggles—even of living men—if they are entangled too obscenely, the chaste rod of the gymnasium-masters at once breaks up.

[59797] 2.2.7. What more? Nothing will be found impressed upon those panels which it would not be more holy not to have seen. Yet a few little verses will detain the casual reader, with a by no means displeasing moderation, since neither is there desire to re-read them nor weariness to read them through. Now if you ask after the marbles: there indeed neither Paros, Carystus, nor Proconnesus, nor Phrygians, Numidians, or Spartans laid down crusts of varied rock; nor, over Ethiopian crags and purple cliffs dyed with native shellfish, do the stones feign for me a scattered bran. But even if we are enriched with no hardness of foreign crags, yet my huts—or rather my hovels—have native coolness. Nay rather, listen to what we have rather than what we have not.

[60555] 2.2.8. To this hall an appendix is joined on the east, outside—a pool, or, if you prefer to speak Greek, a baptistery—which holds about twenty thousand pints. Here a threefold entrance through the middle wall is opened by arched intervals to those who come, washed, from the heat. Nor are there piers in the middle, but columns, which the more skilful architects have named "the purples" of buildings. Into this pool, then, a stream drawn from the brow of the mountain and bent through channels carried around by the outer sides of the swimming-place, six pipes pour forth, jutting out with the feigned heads of lions; which to those who rashly enter will image real lattices of teeth, sheer fury of eyes, and certain manes of necks.

[61290] 2.2.9. Here, if the household or the guest-throng stands about its master, since by reason of the noise of the falling water the mutual exchange of voices is little understood, the company talks into one another's ears; so, pressed by another's sound, the public talk affects a ridiculous secrecy. From here, to those going out, the front of the women's dining-room presents itself, to which is joined, by a neighbouring weaving-room, the store-chamber, separated only by a partition of camp-style.

[61790] 2.2.10. On the east a portico looks upon the lake, supported rather by rounded shafts than by single columns that provoke envy. On the side of the forecourt a roofed length lies open within, not interrupted by middle walls, which, because it itself surveys nothing—even if not a hippodrome—shall at least by my own right be called a covered gallery. This, however, defrauding something of its space at the far end of the walk, makes a member well cooled, where, a public couch being set, the most talkative choir of little clients or of nurses sounds the retreat when I and my household have sought the sleeping-chamber.

[62412] 2.2.11. From the covered gallery one comes into the winter dining-room, which the fire often kindled in the arched hearth has stained with black soot. But what is this to you, whom I now by no means invite to the fireside? Rather let me speak of what pertains to you and the season. From this dining-room a passage is made into a parlour or little supper-room, to which nearly the whole lake, and the whole of the lake's view, lies open. In this is a couch and a shining sideboard, to whose floor or platform one ascends gently from the portico below by steps not shortened or narrowed. Reclining there, if you have any leisure amid your eating, you are occupied with the pleasures of looking out.

[63111] 2.2.12. Now if water cooled from that most celebrated of springs is brought to you, you will see in the goblets, suddenly poured full, the stains of snow and fragments of cloud, and that slippery brightness of the cups dulled by a kind of fatness of the sudden chill. Then drinks answering to the cups, whose icy ladles would be feared by anyone thirsty—not to say by you, who are grandly abstemious. From here you will now watch how the fisherman pushes his alder boat into the deep, how he stretches his standing nets with their cork floats, or how, with signs set out at fixed intervals, the hooked lines are balanced in a row—namely, that in their nightly excursions across the lake the most rapacious salmon-trout may be driven into snares against their kin: for what could I say more fitting than this, when fish is deceived by fish?

[63952] 2.2.13. The meal ended, a lodging will receive you, which, because least sultry, is most summery; for by this, that it lies open only to the north, it has daylight, but not sun, with a very narrow ante-room set between, where for the drowsy chamberlains there is a place rather of nodding than of sleeping.

[64260] 2.2.14. How delightful it is here to have the cicadas sound in one's ears, chirping at noon; the frogs babbling as twilight comes on; the swans and geese clanging in the first watch of night; the cocks crowing at the dead of night; the ill-omened crows with thrice-repeated voice saluting the ruddy torch of rising Dawn; and at daybreak Philomela whistling among the bushes, Procne twittering among the rafters! To which concert you may join the herdsman's pipe of seven reeds, which often, in nightly contests of song, our sleepless Tityri of the mountains ply, re-echoing among the tinkling flocks through the cropped pastures. Yet these varied modulations of voices and songs, by fostering it more deeply, will entice your slumber.

[64996] 2.2.15. When you have gone out from the porticoes, if you seek the harbour of the shore, in a green meadow there is the common brightness—though not far off a grove: two huge lindens, with their foliage joined but their stems apart, trust to make one shade, though not from one root. In whose shade, when my Ecdicius lights up my house, we give ourselves to ball; but only so far, until the trees' shadow, drawn in, is restrained within the span of the branches and there makes a gaming-place for the weary, the ball-court being used up.

[65535] 2.2.16. But since, as I have discharged the building to you, so I owe you the lake, learn what remains. The lake flows down toward the south-east, and the alluvial flood, surging as the winds stir it, wets the foundations pressed into its sand-banks of the dwelling. It is indeed, around its beginnings, full of whirlpools in its marshy soil, and unapproachable by the foot of an inspector; so the fatness of the spongy mud coalesces, with cold springs surrounding it and weedy shores. Nevertheless the moving plain of the deep is cut far and wide by roving little skiffs, when the breezes have laid still; but if a southern whirlwind has fouled it, it swells monstrously, so that the crash of the spray flung up rains down upon the leaves of the trees that stand on the margin.

[66315] 2.2.17. The lake itself, according to what they call nautical measures, runs out to seventeen stadia, entered by a river which, dashed roughly against the barriers of the rocks, grows white with its foamy impulse, and, not long withdrawn from the steep crags, is hidden in the lake; whether perchance it runs into or makes the lake, it at least passes through, forced to be filtered through subterranean strainers, that it may be impoverished not of its floods but of its fishes; which, driven back into the more sluggish pool, stretch out red flesh with white bellies: so, since they are able neither to return nor are permitted to go out, their bulk makes for them a kind of living and movable prison.

[67020] 2.2.18. The lake itself, where it is on the right, is cut, winding, and wooded; where on the left, open, grassy, level. The surface on the south-west is green along the shore, because, the foliage being stretched over into the wave, as the water bathes the gravel, so the shade bathes the waters; and from the east a like crown of woods continues this colour. Along the northern side, as there is the nature of the open sea, so the look; from the west, a common and disorderly thicket, often bent by the weight of the skiffs gliding over it; about which the slippery tendrils of bulrushes are folded, and at once the fat pages of the sedge float, and the bitterness of the grey-green willows, ever fostered by the sweet waters.

[67749] 2.2.19. In the midst of the deep is a small island, where, above mill-stones naturally heaped, a turning-post juts up, worn by the navy's circlings, by the prick-marks of the oars driven against it; against which the pleasant shipwrecks of the players dash themselves. For it was the custom here for our elders to imitate the Drepanitan contest of Trojan superstition. Now indeed the estate itself—though this is beyond what is due—is spread out in woods, painted in meadows, rich in flocks in its pastures, wealthy in shepherds.

[68280] 2.2.20. But I delay no longer, lest, if my pen sets too long a limit, autumn find you re-reading this. Therefore grant me swiftness in coming (for the delay of returning you will yourself provide), about to grant pardon for this, that a letter somewhat too scrupulous has exceeded the brevity owed it, while it anxiously searches out the whole situation of the country—though even so, in zeal to remove tedium, it has not grazed everything. Wherefore a good judge and a craftsman-reader will pronounce great, not the page that describes the spaces, but the villa, spacious, which is described. Farewell.

[75015] Sidonius to his friend Desideratus, greetings.

[75063] 2.8.1. Most sorrowful, I announce these things to you: three days ago, not without a suspension of public business, the lady Philomathia died—a complaisant wife, a clement mistress, a useful mother, a dutiful daughter, to whom at home and abroad the lesser person owed obedience, the greater service, the equal affection. [77582] Sidonius to his friend Donidius, greetings.

[77627] 2.9.1. You ask why I, who set out for Nemausus long ago, should prolong your longing by my late arrival. I render the causes of my tardier return, nor do I delay to disclose my delays, since what is sweet to me is sweet to you also: amid the most pleasant fields, the most courteous masters, Ferreolus and Apollinaris, I have spent a most delightful time. The rights of their estates are bordering, their dwellings neighbouring, with a drive between them that wearies the walker and yet is not enough for one on horseback. The hills above the houses are worked by vine-dresser and olive-tender: you would think them Aracynthus and Nysa, ridges celebrated in the songs of poets. The one house has its prospect over the plain and open country, the other over the woods; but none the less the unlike situation pleases alike.

[78450] 2.9.2. But why now say more of the situation of the estates, when the order of their hospitality remains to be unlocked? First of all, most sagacious scouts being sent out for this purpose, to spy out the road of our return, each house beset not only the tracks of the public highways but also the paths winding by short-cuts and the shepherds' by-ways, lest by any chance we should slip past the snares of their courtesies laid in our path. Into which we fell, I confess, but by no means unwillingly, and were at once compelled to give an oath that, before seven days had rolled by, we would meditate nothing of the continuation of our journey.

[79097] 2.9.3. So every morning there was a first and pleasing contention between the two sides over the guest—whose kitchen, namely, should first smoke for our meals; nor indeed could the alternation set the scale evenly to the division, although to one house I was bound by a tie of kinship through myself, to the other through my own people: for to Ferreolus, a man of prefectorian rank, his age and dignity gave the prerogative of being the first inviter, besides the kinship owed him.

[79580] 2.9.4. So we were hurried from delights to delights. Scarcely had any forecourt been entered, and behold, here the pairs of contending ball-players were redoubled amid the wheeling turns of their feints; here, amid the rivalries of dice-players' voices, was heard the frequent rattle of the clattering dice-boxes and dice; here books in abundance ready to hand (you would have thought you saw either the bookshelves of grammarians, or the wedge-rows of an Athenaeum, or the built-up cabinets of booksellers). So it was, however, that the volumes which were among the chairs of the ladies were found to be of a religious style, while those that were among the benches of the heads of households were ennobled with the buskin of Latin eloquence; though certain volumes of certain authors preserved, in unlike subjects, an equality of style: for men of like learning—here Augustine, there Varro, here Horace, there Prudentius—were read.

[80515] 2.9.5. Among whom Adamantius Origen, interpreted by Turranius Rufinus, was diligently examined by readers of our faith; and, as each had it at heart, we discoursed, judging various things—why he was disapproved by some of the chief mystics as a perverse and dangerous expositor, although he had been so translated to the word and the sense that neither did Apuleius transcribe the Phaedo of Plato so, nor Tullius the Ctesiphon of Demosthenes so, to the use and rule of Roman speech.

[80999] 2.9.6. While each of us was busied with these studies, as it had pleased, behold, one came from the head cook to warn that the time pressed for refreshing our bodies; whom indeed, a messenger keeping the increments of the hours by the spaces of the water-clock, the departing fifth hour proved to have entered fittingly. We lunched, briefly yet abundantly, after the senatorial manner, whereby it is inbred and established that many viands be set on in few dishes—though the banquet was varied by foods now roasted, now in sauce. Amid the drinking, little tales, by the knowing of which we were made merry and instructed; for, woven in a twofold strand, gaiety and skill accompanied them. What more? Holily, beautifully, abundantly we were received.

[81750] 2.9.7. Then rising, if we were at Vorocingus (this was the name of one estate), we carried our foot back to our baggage and lodging; if at Prusianum (so the other farm was called), we cast Tonantius and his brothers, the choicest princes of our noble coevals, out of their own couches, since not easily could the furniture of our beds be carried about so often. The midday torpor being shaken off, we rode a little while, that we might the more easily whet our palates, sluggish with food, to a dinner-time hunger.

[82266] 2.9.8. Each host had baths in process of building, neither in use; but when the throng of my followers and servants, drinking-companions—over whose brains the hospitable bowls, too deeply plunged, lorded it—had ceased to drink even a little, a pit was quickly dug near a spring or river, into which, when a heap of heated stones chanced to be let down, the trench, kindling, was covered over with a vault woven into the form of a hemisphere from flexible hazel-rods, yet so that, with Cilician hangings thrown over, the open intervals of the rods were darkened, the light shut out, to drive back the rising vapour which is struck off the flaming flints by the sprinkling of the boiling water.

[82960] 2.9.9. Here the hours were drawn out for us not without salt and jesting talk; among which, the place being filled and wrapped with the breath of the hissing mist, a most healthful sweat was drawn out; when which, poured forth as it pleased, we were thrust into the heated waters, and, dissolved by their warmth wiping away our crudity, we were afterward braced either by the cold of spring and well, or by the abundance of the river: since between the houses runs the river Vardo, which—except when, fed by melted snow, it grows pale—is red over its yellow gravel, and clear through its channel, quiet and pebbly, yet none the less productive of delicate fish.

[83623] 2.9.10. I would tell of the dinners too, and those most richly dressed, had not the page set a limit to our loquacity which modesty does not apply; the recounting of which also would be pleasant in the telling, did we not blush to soil the back of the letter with wet pens. But since we ourselves are on the point of setting out, and it pleases us, by Christ's help, to visit you straightway, the dinners of friends will be more readily recounted to you at my dinner and yours—provided only that the completed spaces of the past week restore us as soon as possible to a longed-for hunger, since nothing is wont better to repair a stomach burst by gluttony than abstinence. Farewell.

But to such a sentence I never assent, that I should think those fortunate who stand upon the headlong and slippery summits of the commonwealth.

[93195] 2.13.2. For it cannot be told how much, hour by hour, the life of those fortunate ones in this life bears of miseries—if indeed those are so to be pronounced who, like Sulla, presume this name for themselves: those, namely, who, having overstepped common right and law, esteem the highest power the highest blessedness, by this very thing the more wretched, that they too little understand themselves to be subject to a most restless servitude. For as kings lord it over men, so the desires of domineering lord it over kings.

[93722] 2.13.3. Here, if we pass over the misfortunes of preceding princes or of those who followed, this peculiar Maximus of yours alone could be the greatest proof to us of these things; who, although he had ascended fearlessly to the prefectorian, patrician, and consular citadel, and, unsatisfied, had repeated the magistracies he had held as if in recurring orbits, yet, when he came with all his might to the precipice of the principal eminence, suffered a kind of vertigo of immense power under the crown, nor could he endure to be a lord, who had not endured to be under a lord.

[94302] 2.13.4. In short, seek out in the aforesaid the grace, power, and long duration of his former life, and on the other side the origin, the whirlwind, the end of a principate a little more than two months long: assuredly you will find that the man was happier before he was named most happy. So he, whose feasts, manners, moneys, pomp, letters, offices, patrimonies, patronages flourished before, whose very spaces of life, in short, were so guarded that they were unfolded by the water-clocks set out hour by hour—he, named Augustus, and under this appearance shut within the Palatine thresholds, groaned before twilight that he had come to his wishes; and since, by the mass of cares, he was prevented from keeping the measure of his former quiet, he at once renounced the laws of his old rule and perceived alike that the business of a prince and the leisure of a senator cannot go together.

[95196] 2.13.5. Nor did the future deceive him as he mourned; for whereas he had run through the other court honours most tranquilly, he ruled the court itself most turbulently, amid the tumults of soldiers, of the people, of the federates; which his end too betrayed—new, swift, bitter—stained with blood by the treacherous end of a Fortune long flattering, who struck the man, like a scorpion, with her last part. A man learned, and through the merits of his talent of quaestorian rank—certainly a great part of the good party—Fulgentius was wont to say that he had often heard from his mouth, when, hating the weight of empire, he longed for his old security: "Happy you, Damocles, who endured the necessity of a kingdom no longer than one luncheon."

[95943] 2.13.6. For this Damocles, as we read, was by province a Sicilian, by city a Syracusan, and a familiar of the tyrant Dionysius. When he was extolling the goods of his patron, like everything else of course untried by him, with too great praises: "Will you," said Dionysius, "today at least at this table enjoy my goods alike with my evils?" "Gladly," he said. Then he at once enriches the joyful client—though astonished—his plebeian covering being snatched away, with a robe dyed in the shellfish of Tyrian or Tarentine purple, and sets him, gleaming with gems and pearls, on a golden couch and a silken tapestry.

[96559] 2.13.7. And when, as he was about to dine, bread made from the Leontine harvest was given him after the manner of Sardanapalus, and besides choice viands were set on in choicer dishes, and the capacious gems foamed with Falernian, and the unguents grew warm in the icy crystals, and the dining-room, perfumed with cinnamon and frankincense, scattered foreign odours to the nostrils, and the garlands of flowers poured round dried his hair wet with nard—there began, above the back of him as he thus reclined, suddenly to be brandished a drawn sword-point from the panelled ceiling, which seemed about to fall, at any instant, into the throat of the purple-clad man; for, bound by a thread of horse-hair and so threatening by its weight, it bridled with its point the throat of the frightened man after the example of Tantalus, lest the food entering should go out through the mouth, through the wound.

[97462] 2.13.8. Whence, after prayers mingled with weeping and manifold sighs, scarcely released and leaping out at a bound, he fled those riches and royal delights with that swiftness with which they are wont to be sought, brought back to the desires of the middling by fear of the highest, and taking sufficient care lest he should hereafter call or count happy one who, hedged about with arms and guards, and on that account brooding over plundered wealth, pressed by the steel, pressed the gold. Wherefore, toward a state of this kind, my lord brother, I know not whether it is agreed that the happy strive; it is at least plain that the wretched arrive. Farewell.

[98124] Sidonius to his friend Maurusius, greetings.

[98170] 2.14.1. I hear that the vintage answers to your industry and our common wishes with a richer yield than the barren year threatened. Whence I suspect that you will tarry rather long in the district of Vialoscum, which in nearer times was called Martialis on account of the winter quarters of the Julian legions; in which place you have both a fruitful vineyard, and besides it an estate worthy of no small master, which detains you and your household with a manifold dowry of crops and of lodgings.

[98669] 2.14.2. So if, with barns and store-rooms or larder filled, you intend to pass there, until the coming of the swallow or the stork, the snowy months of Janus and Numa in sooty leisure or in tunicked quiet, the causes of our delays in the town—of little profit—will likewise be at once cut off, so that, while you enjoy the country, we may enjoy you; to whom, as you recognize, a field with ample revenues is no more at heart or a pleasure than a neighbour, my equal, with good morals. Farewell.

[99165] Sidonius to his friend Avitus, greetings.

[99208] 3.1.1. By many bonds of affection, from earliest boyhood right up to the time we came into youth, the care of mutual goodwill had knit us together: first because between our mothers there was the highest tie of joined blood; then because we ourselves, born at the same times, used the same masters, were trained in the same arts, took our leisure in the same sports, were advanced by the same princes, and served out the same terms of military pay. And—what for enlarging friendships is more powerful and effective than these—in each and every person, whether equally to be sought or to be guarded against, we strove with a like parity of judgement.

[99859] 3.1.2. On account of all which (I pass over our shared conscience, which inwardly is far more excellent and eminent for you) the multitude of public actions run through together had greatly coupled our wills. But—what must be confessed—when the engines of love had long been raised on either side, you yourself placed the precious summit, enriching with a most opportune gift the church of the little township of Auvergne, over which, though undeservedly, I seem to be set; to whose possession you contributed very greatly by the suburban estate of Cuticiacum, deigning to enrich the brotherhood of our profession no less by the nearness of the place than by its revenue.

[100532] 3.1.3. And although you two seem to be the joint heirs of a sister's inheritance, yet, by the example of your faith, your surviving sister was stirred to the imitation of a good work. So to you, by heaven's justice, is repaid the merit of your own deed, the incitement of another's; whence it comes that you are found most worthy to be one whom the Divinity exalts by an unusual kind of succession—which, however, has neither long deferred to enrich your religious devotion with hundredfold gifts, and which, as we trust, will no less readily bestow heavenly things, when it now pays the earthly. For the Nicetian inheritance, if you do not know, was the heavenly price of Cuticiacum.

[101218] 3.1.4. For what remains, we beg that, as of our church, so also of our city, the care may be alike common to you—a city which, both formerly and now especially from this time, ought to belong to your patronage, or for the sake of your patrimony, of what merit that may be able to be, namely if your presence frequently visits it. Or believe the Goths, who often even disdain or give back their own Septimania, provided they may possess, even in its desolate ownership, the property of this enviable corner.

[101726] 3.1.5. But it is right, by God presiding, for you, set in the midst between them and the commonwealth, to conceive quieter things in your mind; for, even if they, the bounds of the old frontiers being broken, with all their force or with the whole mass of a turbulent possession push forward their boundaries to the Rhône and the Loire, yet your authority, by the dignity of its judgement, will so moderate each party that ours may learn what it ought to deny when it is asked, and the adversary may cease to demand when it is refused. Farewell.

[102273] Sidonius to his friend Constantius, greetings.

[102321] 3.2.1. The people of Auvergne salute you, whose small huts you, a great guest, filled—not ambitious in your retinue but to be courted for your affection. Good God, what joy it was to the afflicted when you set your holy foot within the half-ruined walls! How thronged you were, beset by every order, sex, and age! What a balance of salt in your speech toward each! How gracious the boys, how courteous the young men, how grave the old men measured you! What tears, like a father of all, you shed over the houses thrown down by fire and the half-burnt dwellings! How you grieved over the fields buried with unburied bones! What was then your exhortation, what the spirit of one urging restoration!

[103019] 3.2.2. To this is added that, when you had found the city emptied no less by civic feud than by barbarian incursion, by persuading peace to all you restored to them affection, and them to their fatherland. And as, at your warning, they returned no less to one counsel than to one town, the walls owe you the people brought back, the people brought back owe you their concord. Wherefore they reckon you altogether their own, themselves altogether yours; and—what is your greatest glory—they are by no means deceived.

[103536] 3.2.3. For it is set before the minds of each, day by day, that you, a person grave with age, frail with infirmity, sublime in nobility, venerable in religion, by the plea of love alone burst through so many bars, so many opposed difficulties of coming—namely, the length of the journeys, the shortness of the days, the abundance of snows, the scarcity of fodder, the breadth of the wastes, the narrowness of the lodgings, the gulfs of the roads, either rotten with the wet of rains or rough with the dryness of the frosts; and besides, either embankments rough with stones, or rivers slippery with ice, or hills rugged in the ascent, or valleys scraped bare by the constant fall of landslides. Through all which discomforts, since you were not seeking private comfort, you carried back the public love.

[104341] 3.2.4. For what remains, we pray God that, the goals of your life being advanced according to your wishes, you may unweariedly seek, gain, and bring back the friendships of good men, and that the affection which you leave behind may follow you; and that the favours begun through you, wherever they be, may long be repaid to you—both the foundations and the summits. Farewell.

[104719] Sidonius to his friend Ecdicius, greetings.

[104764] 3.3.1. If ever, now most of all you are longed for by my Arvernians, over whom love of you holds monstrous dominion—and that multiplied for many reasons: first because the land that bore him rightly usurps to itself the chief part in a man's affection; then because in your age you are almost the only mortal who made his fatherland feel no less longing before his birth than joy at his birth. The witnesses of these assertions are the times of your mother's travail of old, which, as the conception advanced, were numbered with the concordant prayers of the citizens.

[105334] 3.3.2. I pass over those things common indeed, but no slight incitements of affection—the clods of this turf crept over by you; I pass over that here you first broke these grasses in your walking, these rivers in your swimming, these woods in your hunting. I pass over that here you first had ball, dice-tower, hawk, dog, horse, and bow for your sport. I omit that here, for the favour of your boyhood, the studies of letters flowed together from all nations, and that what was once owed to your person—that the nobility, about to lay aside the scale of Celtic speech—was imbued now with the orator's pen, now even with the measures of the Muses.

[105982] 3.3.3. This kindled the affection of all toward you chiefly: that those whom you had once compelled to become Latins, you henceforth forbade to be barbarians. For it can never slip from the breasts of the citizens, what manner of man, and how great, all ages, orders, sexes lately beheld you from the half-ruined embankments of the walls, when, the intervening plains being traversed in the opposite direction, and accompanied by a fellowship of scarcely eighteen horsemen, you crossed through some thousands of Goths in mid-day no less than in mid-field—a thing posterity will hardly believe.

[106577] 3.3.4. At the rumour of your name and the sight of your person, amazement overwhelmed a most practised army, so that, for wonder, the leaders of the enemy party did not know how many accompanied themselves and how few you. The whole battle-line was at once drawn up onto the brow of a steep hill, which, though it had first been applied to a siege, when you were seen was not deployed for the encounter. Meanwhile you, with all the best of them slain—those whom not cowardice but audacity had made last in the column—none of your own being missed in so great a contest, alone possessed the plain, however open it was, when the battle did not give you so many comrades as the table is wont to give guests.

[107283] 3.3.5. From here, now brought back at leisure into the city, what offices, applauses, weepings, joys went forth to meet you, prayers rather attempt to conjecture than words to unlock; since one might see, in the crowded courts of a most capacious house, that very most happy ovation of your thronged return, while some snatch your dust with kisses, some take the bridle fat with blood and foam, some lay back the supports of the riding-saddles wet with sweat, some unbuckle the bands of the bending plates for your helmet about to come off your head, some are entangled in unfastening the knots of your greaves, some count up the toothed sides of the swords blunted with slaughter, some with livid fingers measure the rings of the breastplates pierced by cut and thrust.

[108055] 3.3.6. Here, though many, dancing for joy, clung to the embraces of their own, yet upon you the greatest rush of popular gladness was heaped; and at last you had come into a crowd unarmed indeed, but from which not even armed could you extricate yourself; and you bore, with elegance no doubt, the absurdities of those who congratulated you; and while you were torn apart by the tumultuous embrace of those rushing in, you had come to such a condition, most loving interpreter of public love, that it was necessary for you to return a richer gratitude to him who had done you the freer injury.

[108650] 3.3.7. I pass over next that you gathered, by private forces, the appearance of a public army, aided from outside by the small resources of greater men, and that you checked the unbridled forays of the enemy by chastising their ravages. I pass over that, with some surprise attacks, you slaughtered squadrons in their bands, scarcely two or three of your own being missed after the battle, and brought so much calamity, by unexpected contests, on the unconquered adverse party that they planned, by a more shameful counsel, to hide the great number of their slain. Since those whom the hurried night had forbidden to bury, they left with their corpses beheaded, as though it were a smaller token to be recognized by their hair than to have sent away the long-haired man truncated.

[109432] 3.3.8. And after, the light returned, they understood that the theft of their own ruin had been laid bare by a cruel cheapness, then at last they were openly occupied with funeral offices, hiding the disaster no more by fraud than the fraud by haste; yet so that they neither entombed the bones with a mass of hurried turf, to which, neither washed, did they grant garments, nor, clothed, sepulchres—paying such just rites justly to the dead. The bodies lay everywhere, carried together in dripping wagons, which, since you pressed unceasingly upon the stricken, were hastily shut up in kindled dwellings and buried with the funeral fragments of the gliding roofs.
