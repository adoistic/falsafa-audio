CHAPTER: 01-thesmophoriazusae



[0] SERVANT OF AGATHON Let all the people keep holy silence, shutting their mouths; for the sacred band of the Muses is sojourning within the halls of my master, the maker of songs. Let the windless air hold its breath, and let the grey wave of the sea make no roar —

[265] MNESILOCHUS Buzz-buzz!

[289] EURIPIDES Hush!

[306] MNESILOCHUS What is he saying?

[338] SERVANT Let the tribes of winged things lie asleep, and let the feet of forest-roaming wild beasts not be loosed —

[454] MNESILOCHUS Buzzle-buzz-buzz!

[485] SERVANT For Agathon, our captain, the lord of lovely words, is about —

[557] MNESILOCHUS To be buggered, you mean?

[596] SERVANT Who is it that spoke?

[627] MNESILOCHUS The windless air.

[658] SERVANT — about to lay down the keel-blocks, the foundations of a drama. He bends the new curves of his verses, turns some on the lathe, glues the lines together, coins his maxims, makes his metaphors, melts down his wax, rounds it off, and casts it in the mould —

[924] MNESILOCHUS And plays the catamite.

[961] SERVANT What rustic is approaching the precinct?

[1011] MNESILOCHUS One ready, against you and your lord of lovely words, to take this prick of mine — bunch it up, twist it about, and cast it into the mould against the precinct wall.

[1190] SERVANT Surely you were an insolent fellow when you were young, old man.

[1264] EURIPIDES My good man, let this one be; only call out Agathon to me here by every means.

[1354] SERVANT No need to beg; for he will come out himself in a moment. For he is beginning to compose his songs; and it being winter, it is no easy thing to bend the strophes round unless he comes out of doors into the sun.

[1574] EURIPIDES What, then, am I to do?

[1609] SERVANT Wait, since he is coming out.

[1648] EURIPIDES O Zeus, what do you intend to do with me today?

[1707] MNESILOCHUS By the gods, I want to learn what this business is. Why do you groan? Why are you distressed? You ought not to hide it from me, being my kinsman by marriage.

[1878] EURIPIDES There is some great evil kneaded up for me.

[1933] MNESILOCHUS What sort of thing?

[1966] EURIPIDES This very day it will be decided whether Euripides is still living or is undone.

[2058] MNESILOCHUS And how? Since neither are the law-courts about to give judgment now, nor is there a sitting of the Council, since it is the third day of the Thesmophoria, the middle one.

[2243] EURIPIDES That is the very thing I expect will destroy me. For the women have plotted against me, and today at the Thesmophorium they mean to hold an assembly about me, to my ruin.

[2425] MNESILOCHUS Why, what for?

[2453] EURIPIDES Because I make tragedies about them and speak ill of them.

[2523] MNESILOCHUS By Poseidon, then you would suffer justly. But what scheme have you to deal with it?

[2621] EURIPIDES To persuade Agathon the tragic poet to go to the Thesmophorium.

[2696] MNESILOCHUS To do what? Tell me.

[2730] EURIPIDES To sit in the assembly among the women and, if need be, to speak on my behalf.

[2820] MNESILOCHUS Openly or in disguise?

[2856] EURIPIDES In disguise, clothed in a woman's dress.

[2908] MNESILOCHUS The thing is neat, and very much in your style; for in the trade of trickery we take the cake.

[3016] EURIPIDES Hush!

[3033] MNESILOCHUS What is it?

[3058] EURIPIDES Agathon is coming out.

[3092] MNESILOCHUS And which one is he?

[3126] EURIPIDES The one being wheeled out there.

[3170] MNESILOCHUS But I must be blind, then; for I see no man here at all — I see Cyrene.

[3255] EURIPIDES Hush; for he is making ready to sing.

[3304] MNESILOCHUS Ant-tracks, or what is he warbling?

[3353] AGATHON (singing as both leader and chorus) "Take up, girls, the sacred torch in honor of the goddesses below, and with free fatherland raise the festal cry of the dance."

[3526] "To which of the divinities is the revel? Tell me now. My spirit is ready to reverence the gods."

[3625] "Come now, glorify, O Muse, Phoebus, drawer of the golden bow, who founded the hollows of the land in the Simoïs country."

[3749] "Hail, Phoebus, in the fair contests of the Muses bearing the sacred prize of fairest song!"

[3843] "And sing too the maiden in the oak-bearing mountains, Artemis of the wilds."

[3922] "I follow, hymning and glorifying the holy child of Leto, Artemis who knows no bed."

[4008] "And Leto, and the strains of the Asian lyre that move the foot to the dance, beat out of measure yet in measure to the Phrygian sway of the Graces."

[4159] "I revere queen Leto, and the cithara, mother of hymns, famed for its manly cry, by which the light flashed forth in the god's divine eyes, and through our sudden song. Wherefore glorify lord Phoebus with honor. Hail, blessed child of Leto!"

[4402] MNESILOCHUS How sweet the song, O lady Genetyllides — how womanish and tongue-thrust and deep-kissing, that as I listened to it a tickle crept up under my very seat! And you, young man, whoever you are, I want to question you in the manner of Aeschylus, out of the Lycurgus play. Where is this woman-man from? What is his country? What is his dress? What is this confusion of his way of life? What does the lute say to the saffron gown? What the lyre to the snood? What the oil-flask and the breast-band? How ill they go together! And what fellowship is there between a mirror and a sword? And you yourself, child — are you brought up as a man? Then where is your prick? Where is your cloak? Where are your Laconian shoes? But as a woman, then? In that case, where are your breasts? What do you say? Why are you silent? Then must I guess you from your song, since you yourself will not tell?

[5295] AGATHON Old man, old man, I heard the rebuke of envy, but I felt no pain from it. I wear my dress in keeping with my mind. A poet must, for the dramas he has to compose, have his ways in keeping with them. For instance, if one composes women's dramas, his body must share in their ways.

[5583] MNESILOCHUS So you ride astride, then, when you compose a Phaedra?

[5651] AGATHON But if one composes manly dramas, that quality is present already in the body. What we do not possess, imitation hunts down for us.

[5792] MNESILOCHUS Whenever you compose a satyr-play, then, call me, so that I may help you make it, standing erect behind you.

[5914] AGATHON Besides, it is unmusical to see a poet who is boorish and shaggy. Consider that the famous Ibycus and Anacreon of Teos and Alcaeus, who seasoned harmony, wore headbands and minced in the Ionian fashion; and Phrynichus — him at least you have heard of — was himself handsome and handsomely dressed; and that is why his dramas too were beautiful. For one must necessarily compose in accordance with one's nature.

[6334] MNESILOCHUS So that is why Philocles, being ugly, composes uglily; and Xenocles, being bad, composes badly; and Theognis again, being frigid, composes frigidly.

[6496] AGATHON It is wholly necessary. And it was knowing this that I attended to myself.

[6580] MNESILOCHUS How, in the gods' name?

[6617] EURIPIDES Stop your yapping; for I too was such a one, of that age, when I began to compose.

[6711] MNESILOCHUS By Zeus, I do not envy you your education.

[6767] EURIPIDES But let me say what I came for.

[6810] AGATHON Speak.

[6826] EURIPIDES Agathon, it is the part of a wise man to be able to cut many words short within a brief compass. I, struck by a strange misfortune, have come to you as a suppliant.

[7002] AGATHON What is your need?

[7030] EURIPIDES The women mean to destroy me today at the Thesmophoria, because I speak ill of them.

[7126] AGATHON What help, then, can there be for you from us?

[7182] EURIPIDES All help. For if you, sitting secretly among the women, taken for a woman yourself, will answer for me, you will plainly save me. For you alone could speak worthily of me.

[7365] AGATHON Then why do you not defend yourself, being present in person?

[7436] EURIPIDES I will tell you. First, I am known; and then I am grey and have a beard, whereas you are fair-faced, white-skinned, smooth-shaven, woman-voiced, soft, and comely to look on.

[7621] AGATHON Euripides —

[7642] EURIPIDES What is it?

[7665] AGATHON You once wrote, "You rejoice to see the light; do you think your father does not rejoice?"

[7765] EURIPIDES I did.

[7783] AGATHON Do not hope, then, that we will bear your evil for you. For we should be mad. No, bear what is your own yourself, as is fitting. For it is just to bear misfortunes not by craft but by suffering.

[7987] MNESILOCHUS And indeed you, you wide-arsed bugger, were made wide not by words but by sufferings.

[8086] EURIPIDES But what is it that makes you afraid to go there?

[8147] AGATHON I should perish worse than you.

[8188] EURIPIDES How?

[8204] AGATHON How? By seeming to filch the women's nightly works and to snatch away their female Cyprian rite.

[8310] MNESILOCHUS Listen to that — "filch"! By Zeus, to be buggered, rather. And yet the excuse, by Zeus, is plausible enough.

[8432] EURIPIDES Well then, will you do this?

[8472] AGATHON Do not count on it.

[8501] EURIPIDES O thrice ill-starred, how Euripides is undone!

[8559] MNESILOCHUS My dearest, my kinsman, do not betray yourself.

[8620] EURIPIDES How then am I to act?

[8653] MNESILOCHUS Bid this fellow go and howl, and as for me, take me and use me however you wish.

[8747] EURIPIDES Come then, since you give yourself over to me, take off this cloak.

[8826] MNESILOCHUS There, it is on the ground. But what do you mean to do to me?

[8901] EURIPIDES To shave this off, and to singe off the lower parts.

[8965] MNESILOCHUS Well, do it, if you think best. Would that I had never given myself over.

[9052] EURIPIDES Agathon, you always carry a razor; lend us a razor now.

[9119] AGATHON Take one yourself from there, out of the razor-case.

[9181] EURIPIDES You are a noble fellow. Sit down; puff out your right cheek.

[9253] MNESILOCHUS Ouch!

[9272] EURIPIDES Why do you cry out? I will stick a peg in you, if you do not keep quiet.

[9356] MNESILOCHUS Attatai! Iattatai!

[9388] EURIPIDES You there, where are you running?

[9433] MNESILOCHUS To the shrine of the august goddesses; for by Demeter I will not stay here being carved up.

[9538] EURIPIDES Then will you not be a laughing-stock, with one half of your head bare?

[9621] MNESILOCHUS I care little.

[9649] EURIPIDES By the gods, do not betray me. Come here.

[9702] MNESILOCHUS Ill-starred me!

[9731] EURIPIDES Keep still, and hold up your head. Where do you twist away to?

[9805] MNESILOCHUS Mmm-mmm!

[9827] EURIPIDES Why do you mumble? It is all done finely.

[9880] MNESILOCHUS Alas, ill-starred me, I shall serve as a light-armed soldier now.

[9959] EURIPIDES Do not worry; you will look quite handsome. Do you want to look at yourself?

[10047] MNESILOCHUS If you think so, bring it.

[10087] EURIPIDES Do you see yourself?

[10119] MNESILOCHUS No, by Zeus — I see Cleisthenes!

[10165] EURIPIDES Stand up, that I may singe you, and bend over.

[10223] MNESILOCHUS Alas, ill-starred me, I shall become a piglet.

[10283] EURIPIDES Let someone bring out a torch or a lamp. Bend over; mind the tip of your tail now.

[10377] MNESILOCHUS I will see to it, by Zeus — except that I am being burned. Alas, wretched me! Water, water, neighbors, before the flame catches my backside!

[10531] EURIPIDES Take heart.

[10554] MNESILOCHUS Take heart, when I am being scorched to ashes?

[10614] EURIPIDES But you will have no more trouble now; for you have got through the worst of it.

[10706] MNESILOCHUS Phew, ugh, the soot! I have become charred all about the crotch.

[10784] EURIPIDES Do not worry; another will sponge it for you.

[10841] MNESILOCHUS He will howl, whoever washes my backside.

[10896] EURIPIDES Agathon, since you grudge to give yourself, at any rate lend us a gown for this fellow and a breast-band; for you cannot say you have not these.

[11052] AGATHON Take them and use them; I do not grudge.

[11102] MNESILOCHUS What, then, shall I take?

[11141] AGATHON What? Take the saffron gown first and put it on.

[11199] MNESILOCHUS By Aphrodite, it smells sweetly of prick. Gird me up, quickly. Hand me the breast-band now.

[11304] EURIPIDES There.

[11322] MNESILOCHUS Come now, arrange the parts about my legs.

[11378] EURIPIDES We need a snood and a headband.

[11421] AGATHON Here is a wig I wear at night.

[11461] EURIPIDES By Zeus, very suitable too.

[11500] MNESILOCHUS Will it fit me?

[11529] EURIPIDES By Zeus, it is excellent. Bring a wrap.

[11580] AGATHON Take this from the couch.

[11615] EURIPIDES We need shoes.

[11641] AGATHON Take these of mine.

[11670] MNESILOCHUS Will they fit me?

[11701] EURIPIDES You like wearing them loose, at any rate.

[11754] AGATHON You attend to that. But since you have what you need, let someone wheel me inside as quickly as possible.

[11869] EURIPIDES This man of ours is now a woman, in appearance at least. But if you talk, see that you play the woman well and convincingly with your voice.

[12021] MNESILOCHUS I will try.

[12046] EURIPIDES Off with you, then.

[12077] MNESILOCHUS By Apollo, no — not unless you swear to me —

[12135] EURIPIDES What thing?

[12158] MNESILOCHUS — to help save me by every device, if any mischief befalls me.

[12234] EURIPIDES I swear, then, by the air, the dwelling of Zeus.

[12294] MNESILOCHUS Why that rather than by the tenement-house of Hippocrates?

[12366] EURIPIDES I swear, then, by all the gods together.

[12418] MNESILOCHUS Remember this, then — that the mind swore, but the tongue swore not; nor did I make him take the oath.

[12534] EURIPIDES Make haste, quickly; for the signal of the assembly appears at the Thesmophorium. And I am off.

[12641] MNESILOCHUS This way now, Thratta, follow. Thratta, behold, as the torches burn, how great a cloud goes up from the smoke. But O most fair Thesmophoroi, receive me with good fortune both here and on my way home again. Thratta, set down the box, and then take out the cake, so that I may take it and sacrifice to the two goddesses. Lady most honored, dear Demeter, and Pherrephatta, grant that I may sacrifice often and much to you — or, if not, at least that I may now escape notice. And that my little daughter may get a husband who is rich, and otherwise a fool and a simpleton; and that my little son may have sense and wit. Where, where might I sit in a good place to hear the speakers? But you, Thratta, go off out of the way; for slaves are not allowed to hear this.

[13415] CRIER-WOMAN Keep holy silence, keep holy silence! Pray to the two Thesmophoroi, to Demeter and the Maiden, and to Plutus, and to Calligeneia, and to Earth the nurse of youth, and to Hermes, and to the Graces, that this assembly and present gathering may be conducted most fairly and best, with much profit to the city of the Athenians and good fortune for us ourselves. And may she who acts and speaks the best for the people of the Athenians and the people of the women — may she carry the day. Pray for these things, and for good things for yourselves. Hail Paean, hail Paean! Let us rejoice!

[14011] CHORUS We accept it, and we entreat the race of the gods to appear, rejoicing in these prayers. O Zeus of mighty name, and you of the golden lyre who hold holy Delos, and you, all-conquering grey-eyed Maiden of the golden spear who dwell in our embattled city — come hither. And you of many names, slayer of beasts, child of golden-faced Leto, scion of her; and you, holy Poseidon of the sea, ruler of the deep, leaving the fish-haunted depths stirred by the gadfly; and you daughters of Nereus of the sea, and you Nymphs that wander on the hills. And may the golden lyre ring out upon our prayers; and may we, the noble women of the Athenians, hold our assembly to good effect.

[14691] CRIER-WOMAN Pray to the Olympian gods and goddesses, and to the Pythian gods and goddesses, and to the Delian gods and goddesses, and to the other gods: if anyone plots any evil against the people of the women, or sends heralds to treat with Euripides and the Medes for any harm to the women; or aims at tyranny, or at bringing back the tyrant; or denounces a woman for substituting a baby; or if any slave-woman, acting as a procuress, whispers tales to her master; or if anyone sent on an errand carries false messages; or if any adulterer deceives with false words and does not give what he once promised; or if any old woman gives gifts to an adulterer; or if any courtesan receives them, betraying her lover; or if any tavern-keeper, man or woman, cheats on the measure of the pitcher or the cup — pray that he and his house may perish miserably; but for all you others, pray that the gods grant many good things.

[15611] CHORUS We join in praying that these prayers may be fulfilled, fulfilled for the city and for the people; and that the best things may go to those whose part it is to win by speaking well. But all those who deceive and transgress the established oaths, for gain, to do harm; or seek to overturn decrees and the law; or tell our secrets to our enemies; or bring the Medes against the land for gain, to do harm — these are impious and wrong the city. But, O all-powerful Zeus, ratify these things, so that the gods may stand by us, women though we are.

[16163] FIRST SEMICHORUS I have never yet heard a more cunning woman than this, nor one speaking more cleverly. For she says all things justly, and she examined every aspect, and weighed everything closely and shrewdly in her mind, and found out subtle arguments well thought out; so that if Xenocles the son of Carcinus were to speak beside her, he would seem, I think, to all of you, plainly to be saying nothing at all.

[16579] SECOND WOMAN For a few words I too have come forward. For the rest she has accused him well; but what I myself have suffered, this I wish to tell. For my husband died in Cyprus, leaving me five little children, whom I barely fed by weaving garlands in the myrtle-market. So for a time I made a living, half-badly; but now this man, by composing in his tragedies, has persuaded men that there are no gods; so that we no longer sell even to half our former trade. So now I exhort and tell you all to punish this man, for many reasons; for he does us savage evils, O women, having himself been brought up among the wild greens. But I am off to the market; for I have to plait twenty garlands for men on order.

[17287] SECOND SEMICHORUS Here is yet another spirit, even cleverer than the former, that has shown itself. What things she chattered out, not unseasonable, having wits and a subtle thought — not senseless, but all persuasive. We must, for this insolence, make the man plainly pay the penalty.

[17574] MNESILOCHUS That you should be very angry with Euripides, O women, hearing such evil things, is no wonder, nor that your gall should seethe. For I myself — so may I have joy of my children — hate that man, unless I am mad. Still, we ought to give one another an account among ourselves; for we are alone, and not one word will be carried out. Why, holding these grievances, do we accuse him and bear it ill, if, knowing two or three evils of us, he has told them, when we do ten thousand? For I myself, first of all — to speak of no other — am conscious of many dreadful things in myself; and the most dreadful of all was when I had been a bride three days, and my husband was sleeping beside me. Now I had a lover, who had deflowered me when I was seven years old. He, out of longing for me, came and scratched at the door; and at once I knew it; then I go down secretly. And my husband asks, "Where are you going down?" "Where? A cramp and a pain has hold of my belly, husband; so I am going to the privy." "Go, then." And he set to grinding juniper-berries, anise, and sage; while I poured water on the door-hinge and went out to my lover; then I was had, leaning against the Aguieus-pillar, bent over, holding the bay-tree. This, you see, Euripides never told; nor that we are mounted by the slaves and the muleteers, when we have no one else — that he does not say; nor that when we have been thoroughly banged all night, in the morning we chew garlic, so that the husband, smelling it as he comes in from the wall, may not suspect us of doing any wrong. This, you see, he never told. And if he abuses Phaedra, what is that to us? Nor has he yet told that, while the woman was showing her husband her wrap, holding it up to the light to see what it was like, she sent off the adulterer wrapped up inside it — that he has not yet told. And I know another woman who said she was in labor for ten days, until she bought a baby; and the husband ran round buying drugs to hasten the birth; and meanwhile an old woman brought the baby in a pot, its mouth stopped with a honeycomb so that it should not cry. Then, when the carrier nodded, the woman cries out at once, "Go away, go away, husband; I think I am now about to give birth." For the baby kicked against the belly of the pot; and he ran off rejoicing, while she drew out the wax from the child's mouth, and it cried out. Then the filthy old woman who brought the baby runs smiling to the husband and says, "A lion, a lion is born to you, your very image, like you in all other things, every one, and his little prick is like yours, curved like a pine-cone." Do we not do these evil things? Yes, by Artemis, we do. Then are we angry at Euripides, having suffered nothing greater than we have done?

[20329] FIRST SEMICHORUS This indeed is marvelous, from where the creature was found, and what land reared up so bold a woman as this. For that the villainess should say these things openly, so shamelessly, I would never have thought that any one of us would dare. But anything may happen now; and I commend the old proverb: under every stone we must look, lest an orator bite us.

[20703] CHORUS But there is nothing worse in all respects than shameless women by nature — except, of course, women.

[20813] FIRST WOMAN You are not in your right minds, by Aglaurus, O women; rather you are bewitched, or have suffered some other great evil, in letting this pest insult us all so outrageously. If, then, anyone will help — well; but if not, we ourselves and the slave-girls will get ashes from somewhere and depilate her bush, that she may learn, being a woman, not to speak ill of women for the future.

[21209] MNESILOCHUS Not my bush, at any rate, O women! For if, when there is freedom of speech and it is permitted to all of us citizen-women who are present to speak, I then said what I knew to be just on Euripides' behalf — for that must I be plucked and pay the penalty at your hands?

[21490] FIRST WOMAN Why, must you not pay the penalty? You who alone have dared to speak up for the man who has done us many evils, deliberately finding plots wherever some wicked woman occurred — composing his Melanippes and Phaedras — but Penelope he never composed, because she was reputed to be a chaste woman.

[21798] MNESILOCHUS I know the reason. For you could not name one Penelope among the women of today, but Phaedras every one.

[21916] FIRST WOMAN Do you hear, O women, what the villainess has said of us all again?

[21997] MNESILOCHUS And by Zeus I have not yet told all I know; for do you wish me to say more?

[22086] FIRST WOMAN But you could have no more; for all you knew you have poured out.

[22165] MNESILOCHUS By Zeus, not yet the ten-thousandth part of what we do. For this, you see, I have not told: how, taking flesh-scrapers, we then siphon out the wine.

[22327] FIRST WOMAN May you be crushed!

[22360] MNESILOCHUS And how, giving the meats from the Apaturia to our procuresses, we then say the weasel did it —

[22469] FIRST WOMAN Wretched me! You talk nonsense.

[22514] MNESILOCHUS Nor have I told how one woman struck down her husband with an axe; nor how another drove her husband mad with drugs; nor how the Acharnian woman once buried her father under the bathtub —

[22715] FIRST WOMAN May you perish!

[22744] MNESILOCHUS — her father.

[22771] FIRST WOMAN Are these things to be endured?

[22816] MNESILOCHUS Nor how you, when your slave-girl bore a male child, then substituted it for your own, and palmed off your own little daughter on her.

[22964] FIRST WOMAN By the two goddesses, you shall not get off scot-free for saying this; but I will pluck out your bristles!

[23084] MNESILOCHUS By Zeus, you shall not lay a hand on me.

[23138] FIRST WOMAN There, look! (striking)

[23175] MNESILOCHUS There, look! (striking back)

[23217] FIRST WOMAN Take my cloak, Philiste.

[23255] MNESILOCHUS Just lay a finger on me, and I, by Artemis —

[23313] FIRST WOMAN What will you do?

[23344] MNESILOCHUS — the sesame-cake you ate, I will make you shit it out.

[23413] CHORUS Stop your railing; for some woman comes running to us in haste. So before she joins us, be silent, that we may learn in good order what she is going to say.

[23578] CLEISTHENES Dear women, kindred to me in my ways: that I am your friend is plain from my cheeks; for I am woman-mad and am always your patron. And now, having heard a great matter concerning you, talked of a little while ago in the market-place, I have come to tell it and report it to you, so that you may watch and be on your guard, lest some terrible and great matter fall upon you unawares.

[23974] CHORUS What is it, my boy? For it is fitting to call you "boy," so long as you have your cheeks so smooth.

[24082] CLEISTHENES They say that Euripides sent up here today a man, a certain kinsman of his, an old man.

[24183] CHORUS For what work, or with what design?

[24227] CLEISTHENES So that, whatever you might be planning and about to do, he should be a spy on your discussions.

[24337] CHORUS And how has a man escaped notice, being among women?

[24398] CLEISTHENES Euripides singed and plucked him and got him up in all respects like a woman.

[24489] MNESILOCHUS Do you believe him in this? What man is so foolish as to endure being plucked? I do not think it, by the two honored goddesses.

[24630] CLEISTHENES You talk nonsense; for I would not have come to report it, had I not learned it from those who know for certain.

[24756] CHORUS This matter is reported as terrible. But, O women, we must not idle, but look for the man and seek out where he sits hidden, escaping our notice. And you, help us find him, so that you may have this favor and that one too, our patron.

[24999] CLEISTHENES Come, let me see; who are you, the first one?

[25058] MNESILOCHUS Where can one turn?

[25091] CLEISTHENES For you must be searched.

[25130] MNESILOCHUS Ill-starred me!

[25159] FIRST WOMAN You ask who I am? The wife of Cleonymus.

[25213] CLEISTHENES Do you know who this woman is?

[50259] EURIPIDES Pipe up faster. Do you still fear the Scythian?

[50318] ARCHER A fine bottom! (to himself) You'll weep if you don't stay inside. There — good; a fine shape about the little prick.

[50443] EURIPIDES That is well. Take your cloak; it is time for us two to be going now.

[50524] ARCHER Won't she kiss me first?

[50557] EURIPIDES Certainly; kiss him.

[50589] ARCHER Oh, oh, oh, papapapai, how sweet the tongue, like Attic honey! Why doesn't she sleep with me?

[50691] EURIPIDES Farewell, archer; for this could not be.

[50743] ARCHER Yes, yes, old woman; do me this favor.

[50790] EURIPIDES Will you give a drachma, then?

[50832] ARCHER Yes, yes, I'll give it.

[50864] EURIPIDES Hand over the silver, then.

[50903] ARCHER But I haven't any; take my quiver-case.

[50951] EURIPIDES Then you will bring it back. Come, child.

[51004] ARCHER Follow, child. And you, old woman, guard this old man for me. And what is your name?

[51097] EURIPIDES Artemisia.

[51119] ARCHER I'll remember the name: Artamuxia.

[51162] EURIPIDES (aside) Hermes the trickster, you do this still finely. (to the dancing-girl) You run off, then, taking the boy; and I will loose this fellow. (to Mnesilochus) And you, see that manfully, when you are loosed, you flee at once with all speed and make for your wife and children at home.

[51459] MNESILOCHUS That shall be my care, once I am loosed.

[51513] EURIPIDES You are loosed. It is your task; flee, before the archer comes back and catches you.

[51609] MNESILOCHUS That is just what I am doing.

[51652] ARCHER (returning) Old woman, how charming your little daughter is, and not crabbed, but gentle. Where is the old woman? Alas, I am undone! Where is the old man from here? Old woman, hey, granny! I do not commend you, old woman. Artamuxia! She has tricked me, the old hag. Run off as fast as you can, you! And rightly is it called a quiver-case (subene) — for you've been "quivered." Alas, what shall I do? Where is the old woman? Artamuxia!

[52095] CHORUS You ask after the old woman who carried the lyres?

[52154] ARCHER Yes, yes. You saw her?

[52185] CHORUS She has gone off that way, herself and an old man following.

[52254] ARCHER Did the old man have on a saffron gown?

[52302] CHORUS I say so. You might still catch them, if you pursue this way.

[52372] ARCHER O filthy old woman! Which way did she run, the road? Artamuxia!

[52444] CHORUS Straight up, pursue. Where are you running? Will you not pursue back this way? You are running the opposite way.

[52565] ARCHER Wretched me! Still, I'll run. Artamuxia!

[52614] CHORUS Run now, off to the crows, with a fair wind, run! But our play has been moderate enough; so it is now time to be going home, each one. And may the two Thesmophoroi grant us in return a good reward for these things.
