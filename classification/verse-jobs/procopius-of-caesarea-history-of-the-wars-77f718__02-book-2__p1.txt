[113644] 19.1. And Belisarius, seeing Nisibis to be exceedingly strong, having no hope of taking it, hastened to go forward, that he might do the enemy some harm by a raid.

[113809] 19.2. Setting out, then, with his whole army, he went forward. And having accomplished a day's journey, they came upon a fortress which the Persians call Sisauranon.

[113976] 19.3. There was a multitude of inhabitants, and eight hundred horsemen, the best of the Persians, kept guard, over whom a notable man, Bleschames by name, was set.

[114141] 19.4. The Romans, encamping very near the fortress, undertook a siege, and making an assault on the wall were repulsed,

[114262] 19.5. having lost many in the battle. For the wall chanced to be exceedingly strong, and the barbarians most vigorously defended themselves from there against the assailants. Therefore, calling together all the commanders,

[114486] 19.6. Belisarius spoke as follows: "Experience of many wars, commanders, has given us in the perplexities of affairs to foresee what shall befall, and to be able to choose the better before the dangers.

[114690] 19.7. Know, then, how great an evil it is for an army to journey into a hostile land with many strongholds and many warlike men in them left behind.

[114840] 19.8. Which has befallen us too at present. For as we go forward, some of the enemy following secretly from here and from the city of Nisibis will, as is likely, do us mischief in places suitable for an ambush or some other plot.

[115071] 19.9. And if elsewhere another army should meet us and set itself to battle, we must of necessity draw up against both, and should thus suffer at their hands irreparable things. For I forbear to say that, stumbling in the engagement, should it so chance, we shall have no device of return to the land of the Romans for the future.

[115403] 19.10. Let us not, then, using senseless haste, appear to have despoiled ourselves, nor by contentiousness harm the affairs of the Romans. For unlearned daring carries to ruin, but prudent delay is by nature ever sufficient to save those who use it.

[115654] 19.11. We, then, sitting here, let us try to take this fortress, and let Arethas with his followers be sent to the regions of Assyria.

[115790] 19.12. For the Saracens are by nature unable to storm walls, but most apt of all for plundering.

[115888] 19.13. And some of the warlike soldiers will invade with them, that they themselves, no opposition appearing, may work irreparable things on those they fall upon, but if any hostile force should meet them, easily withdrawing they may be saved among us.

[116142] 19.14. And we, having taken the fortress, if God will, then with the whole army let us cross the Tigris river, not fearing those who will do mischief behind, and knowing well how the affairs of the Assyrians stand."

[116359] 19.15. Having said this, Belisarius seemed to all to speak well, and at once carried out the plan. He bade Arethas with his followers go against Assyria, and sent with them twelve hundred soldiers, of whom the most were of his own shield-bearers, setting over them two bodyguards, Trajan and John called Phagas, both good in war.

[116690] 19.16. And he charged them to do everything obeying Arethas, and he bade Arethas, having plundered everything in his path, thus return to the camp and report to them what the affairs of the Assyrians were as to their power.

[116915] 19.17. Those under Arethas, then, crossing the Tigris river, came into Assyria.

[116996] 19.18. There, finding a good country and long unravaged, and this unguarded, plundering by a raid many of the places there, they encompassed great wealth.

[117152] 19.19. Then Belisarius, having seized some of the Persians, learned that those in the fortress wanted provisions altogether.

[117278] 19.20. For not, as in Daras and the city of Nisibis, are they wont to store the yearly food at public expense, but, an army of the enemy having fallen upon them unexpectedly, they had not had time to bring in any of the necessaries.

[117512] 19.21. And suddenly, many having fled into the fortress, they were pressed, as is likely, by want of provisions.

[117626] 19.22. Learning this, Belisarius sent George, a most intelligent man and one who shared his secrets, to make trial of the men there, whether somehow by some agreement he might be able to take the place.

[117830] 19.23. And George, making an exhortation and saying much alluring to them, persuaded them, having received pledges about their safety, to hand over themselves and the fortress to the Romans.

[118022] 19.24. Thus Belisarius, taking Sisauranon, released all the inhabitants, being Christians and Romans from the beginning, unharmed, but sent the Persians with Bleschames to Byzantium, and razed the wall of the fortress to the ground.

[118256] 19.25. And the emperor not long after sent these Persians and Bleschames to Italy to make war on the Goths. So the affairs about the fortress of Sisauranon turned out thus.

[118430] 19.26. But Arethas, fearing lest the booty be taken from him by the Romans, was no longer willing to turn back to the camp.

[118555] 19.27. Sending, then, some of his followers ostensibly on a reconnaissance, he bade them, returning secretly as quickly as possible, signify to them that a great army of the enemy was about the crossing of the river.

[118773] 19.28. Therefore he advised Trajan and John, going by another road, to return to the land of the Romans.

[118879] 19.29. They came no longer to Belisarius, but, keeping the Euphrates river on the right, thus came to Theodosiopolis which is by the Aborrhas river.

[119029] 19.30. And Belisarius and the Roman army, learning nothing about this force, were vexed, falling into a fear and suspicion neither bearable nor moderate.

[119184] 19.31. And much time being spent for them in this siege, it befell many of the soldiers to be seized there by a difficult fever; for the Mesopotamia subject to the Persians is exceedingly parched.

[119382] 19.32. Of which the Romans, being unaccustomed, and especially those coming from Thrace, having their dwelling in a place extraordinarily parched and in certain stifling huts in the summer season, so sickened that a third of the army lay half-dead.

[119632] 19.33. The whole army, then, was eager to depart thence and to return as quickly as possible to their own land, and most of all the commanders of the troops in Lebanon, Rhecithancus and Theoctistus, seeing that the time of the Saracen offering had already passed.

[119897] 19.34. Coming often to Belisarius, they begged him to release them at once, calling to witness that, having given over the regions of Lebanon and Syria to Alamoundaras, they were sitting there for no reason.

[120106] 19.35. Therefore Belisarius, calling together all the commanders, set out counsel.

[120190] 19.36. There rising first, John the son of Nicetas spoke as follows: "Most excellent Belisarius, I think that neither Fortune nor valour have in all time made such a general as you yourself are.

[120386] 19.37. And this repute has prevailed not only among the Romans, but among all the barbarians.

[120481] 19.38. This glory, however, you will most surely preserve, if you are able to bring us alive to the land of the Romans; for now our hopes are not in a fair state. For consider thus with me about this army.

[120688] 19.39. The Saracens and the most warlike of the soldiers, having crossed the Tigris river, I know not how many days ago, came to such fortune that they could not even send a messenger to us; Rhecithancus and Theoctistus will depart, as you see, I suppose, very soon, thinking that the army of Alamoundaras is in the midst of Phoenicia, plundering all the places there.

[121058] 19.40. And of those left, the sick are so many in number that those who would tend and convey them to the land of the Romans are far fewer than they in number.

[121219] 19.41. And these things being so, if anything hostile should befall us, either remaining here or going back, no one could even report the suffering to the Romans in Daras.

[121544] 19.43. For those set in danger, especially such, not to consider their safety, but their plot against the enemy, is great folly."

[121675] 19.44. So much John said, and all the rest praised it, and falling into a tumult thought fit to make the withdrawal quickly.

[122695] 20.1. At the beginning of spring Chosroes the son of Cabades for the third time invaded the land of the Romans with a great army, keeping the Euphrates river on the right.

[122868] 20.2. And Candidus, the priest of Sergiopolis, when he learned that the army of the Medes had come very near, fearing for himself and the city, since at the agreed time he had by no means carried out what he had agreed with Chosroes, coming into the camp of the enemy, begged Chosroes not to be harsh with him on this account.

[123196] 20.3. For he had never had money, and on this account had not even purposed at all to ransom the Surenes, but had entreated the Emperor Justinian much about these things and had been of no profit to himself.

[123405] 20.4. But Chosroes held him in custody, and torturing his body most bitterly, thought fit to exact double money, as had been agreed.

[123539] 20.5. And he begged him to send some men to Sergiopolis to receive all the treasures of the church there.

[123646] 20.6. And when Chosroes did accordingly, Candidus sent with them one of his own followers.

[125064] 20.15. Therefore they came to no parley at all with the enemy, and the barbarians, held by thirst, rose up and came to Chosroes. Candidus, however, Chosroes no longer released.

[125389] 20.17. When Chosroes came to the country of the Commageni, which they call Euphratesia, he by no means wished to turn to plunder or the capture of any place, since the things in his path as far as the Syrians he had chanced before either to take or to exact money from, as has been shown in the foregoing.

[125696] 20.18. But he purposed to lead the army straight to Palestine, that he might plunder, among other things, all the treasures in Jerusalem. For he had heard by report that this country was exceedingly good and had inhabitants rich in gold.

[125935] 20.19. And all the Romans, commanders and soldiers, by no means purposed to meet the enemy or to stand in the way of their passage, but, seizing the strongholds each as he could, thought it enough to guard these and themselves to be saved.

And he, riding on the public horses, which they have been accustomed to call veredi, since he had no army with him, came with much speed to Euphratesia, while Justus, the emperor's nephew, chanced to have fled to Hierapolis together with Bouzes and certain others.

[127978] 20.27. For this would justly be called not safety, but betrayal. But come as quickly as possible to Europum, where, having gathered the whole army, I have hope, whatever God gives,

[128355] 21.1. And Chosroes, learning that Belisarius with the whole Roman army was encamped at Europum, no longer resolved to advance, but sent to Belisarius one of the royal secretaries, Abandanes by name, having a great repute for intelligence, to spy out what sort of man the general was, and ostensibly to complain that the Emperor Justinian had by no means sent the envoys to the Persians to dispose the matters of the peace according to the agreement. When Belisarius learned this, he did as follows.

[128855] 21.2. He himself, choosing out six thousand men, tall and very fair in body, set out as though to hunt far off from the camp, and Diogenes the bodyguard and Adolius the son of Acacius, a man Armenian by race, who ever served the emperor in the palace in matters of quiet (the Romans call those on whom this honour lies silentiarii), and then commanded some Armenians, he bade cross the river with a thousand horsemen and go about the bank there, ever giving the enemy an impression that, if they should wish, having crossed the Euphrates, to go by the road to their own land, they would never permit it. And they did accordingly.

[129486] 21.3. And Belisarius, when he learned that the envoy was very near, having pitched a tent of certain thick linen cloths, which they have been accustomed to call a pavilion, sat there, as in a desert place, intimating that he had come there with no preparation.

[129748] 21.4. And he arranged the soldiers thus. On either side of the tent were Thracians and Illyrians, and after them Goths, and next to these Heruls, after whom were Vandals and Moors.

[129930] 21.5. And they extended over a great part of the plain. For they did not stand always in the same place, but, standing apart from one another and making walks, they looked at the envoy of Chosroes carelessly and in no way earnestly.

[130164] 21.6. And none of them had either cloak or any other shoulder-garment, but, clad in linen tunics and trousers, then girded up, they walked about.

[130311] 21.7. And each had his horse-whip, but the weapon of one was a sword, of another an axe, of another bare bows.

[130423] 21.8. And all gave an impression that, taking no thought of all else, they were eager to hunt.

[130519] 21.9. Abandanes, then, coming into the presence of Belisarius, said that King Chosroes was indignant, because, as had been agreed before, the Caesar (for so the Persians call the Emperor of the Romans) had not sent the envoys to him, and on this account Chosroes had been compelled to come in arms into the land of the Romans.

[130847] 21.10. But Belisarius, neither cowering, though so great a multitude of barbarians was encamped very near, nor falling into any disturbance at the word, but with a laughing and relaxed face, replies, saying, "Not in this way which Chosroes has now done is it customary for men to manage their affairs.

[131150] 21.11. For all others, if anything is disputed between themselves and some of their neighbours, send embassies to them first, and when they do not obtain moderate terms, then go against them in war.

[131350] 21.12. But he, getting into the midst of the Romans, then puts forward the arguments about peace." Having said so much, he dismissed the envoy.

[131495] 21.13. And he, coming to Chosroes, advised him to depart as quickly as possible.

[131577] 21.14. For he said he had met a general most brave and most intelligent of all men, and soldiers such as he himself had never seen, whose good order he marvelled at most of all, and that the contest was not for him and Belisarius on equal terms of danger, but it differed, that, conquering, he would conquer the Caesar's slave, but, worsted, should it so chance, he would bring great shame on his kingship and on the Persian race; and that the Romans, defeated, would easily be saved in their strongholds and their own land, but of themselves, if any reverse should befall, not even a messenger would escape to the country of the Persians.

[132218] 21.15. Persuaded by this counsel, Chosroes wished to turn back to the country of the Persians, but was held by much helplessness.

[132349] 21.16. For he thought the crossing of the river was being guarded by the enemy, and was not able to ride back by the same road, being altogether empty of men, since all the provisions had now failed them which they had first with them when they invaded the land of the Romans.

[132627] 21.17. In the end, having reckoned much, it seemed most expedient to him, having risked a battle, to come to the land opposite, and to make the journey through a country abounding in all good things.

[132828] 21.18. But Belisarius well knew that not even ten myriads of men would be sufficient to check Chosroes' crossing (for the river chances to be crossable by ships in many of the places there for a great extent, and the Persian army was otherwise mightier than to be shut off from the crossing by a few enemies); but he charged those under Diogenes and Adolius with the thousand at first to go about that bank, that they might throw the barbarian into a disturbance by some perplexity.

[133312] 21.19. Whom, having frightened, as I have said, he feared lest there be some hindrance to his departing from the land of the Romans.

[133446] 21.20. And it seemed to him worth much account to drive the army of Chosroes thence, having risked no battle against many myriads of barbarians with soldiers very few and simply cowering at the army of the Medes. Therefore he bade Diogenes and Adolius remain quietly.

[133715] 21.21. Chosroes, then, having yoked a bridge with great speed, suddenly crossed the Euphrates river with his whole army.

[133837] 21.22. For all rivers are crossable by the Persians with no trouble, since for them, going by the road, hook-shaped iron pieces are in readiness, with which, fitting long pieces of wood into one another, they improvise a bridge on the spot wherever they wish.

[134098] 21.23. And as soon as he was on the bank opposite, sending to Belisarius, he said that he himself had granted to the Romans the withdrawal of the army of the Medes, but expected the envoys from them, who, it was worth saying, would be present not long after.

[134358] 21.24. And Belisarius, himself too crossing the Euphrates river with the whole Roman army, at once sent men to Chosroes.

[134480] 21.25. And they, when they came to him, praising the withdrawal much, promised that envoys would come to him from the emperor very soon, who would carry out toward him in deed the agreements made before concerning the peace.

[134706] 21.26. And they asked to use the Romans, as friends, for their journey. And he undertook to carry this out too, if they should give him some of the men of repute as hostages on this agreement, that they would carry out the things agreed.

[134945] 21.27. The envoys, then, returning to Belisarius, reported the words of Chosroes, and he, coming to Edessa, at once sent John the son of Basil, the most conspicuous of all the Edessenes in family and wealth, as a hostage to Chosroes, unwillingly.

[135193] 21.28. And the Romans held Belisarius in praise, and the man seemed to them to have won more glory in this deed than when he brought Gelimer captive or Vittigis to Byzantium.

[135369] 21.29. For it was truly worthy of much account and praise, when all the Romans were terrified and hiding in their strongholds, and Chosroes with a great army had got into the midst of the Roman realm, that a man, a general, with a few men, coming in the meanwhile at a swift run from Byzantium, should encamp over against the King of the Persians, and that Chosroes, unexpectedly, either fearing the fortune or the valour of the man, or deceived by certain devices, should no longer advance, but in deed flee, and in word desire the peace.

[135910] 21.30. Meanwhile Chosroes, disregarding the agreements, took the city of Callinicum, no one at all defending it. For the Romans, seeing its wall rotten and altogether easy to take, ever pulling down a part of it, were renewing it with new building.

[136160] 21.31. Then, indeed, having pulled down a part of it, and not yet built this remaining part, when they learned that the enemy were very near, conveying away the most precious of their goods, the well-to-do of its inhabitants withdrew to certain other strongholds, while the rest remained there without soldiers.

[136473] 21.32. And a very great number of husbandmen chanced to have been gathered there. These Chosroes enslaved, and razed the whole to the ground.

[136616] 21.33. And a little later, receiving the hostage John, he withdrew to his native land.

[136704] 21.34. And the Armenians who had gone over to Chosroes, having received pledges from the Romans, came with Bassaces to Byzantium. These things befell the Romans in the third inroad of Chosroes, and Belisarius came summoned by the emperor to Byzantium, that he might be sent again to Italy, the affairs there being now altogether wretched for the Romans.

[137059] 22.1. About these times a plague came, from which nearly all human affairs were all but extinguished. For all things that fall from heaven might perhaps have some account of cause given by daring men, such as those clever in these things love to fashion—causes by no means comprehensible to man—and to invent extravagant theories of nature, knowing well that they say nothing sound, but thinking it enough for themselves if, deceiving some of those they meet by their argument, they persuade them.

[137558] 22.2. But for this evil there is no device, either to speak a pretext in word or to reckon one in thought, except indeed to refer it to God.

[137700] 22.3. For it came not upon a part of the earth nor upon certain men, nor did it hold any one season of the year, whence one might be able to find sophisms of cause, but it encompassed the whole earth and harmed the lives of all men, although differing far from one another to the contrary, sparing neither nature nor age.

[138023] 22.4. For whether by dwelling in places, or by manner of life, or by character of nature, or by pursuits, or by anything else by which men differ from men—in this disease alone the difference availed nothing.

[138233] 22.5. And it fell upon some in summer, on others in winter, on others in the other seasons. Let, then, each say of these things as he knows, both sophist and meteorologist, but I shall go on to relate whence this disease began and in what manner it destroyed men.

[138498] 22.6. It began from the Egyptians who dwell at Pelusium. And dividing in two, it went in part to Alexandria and the rest of Egypt, and in part it came to the Palestinians, neighbours of the Egyptians, and thence seized the whole earth, ever advancing by the road and walking at the fitting times.

[138796] 22.7. For it seemed to go on by fixed terms and to have a fixed time of stay in each country, using the destruction carelessly toward no men, but scattering on either side to the extremities of the inhabited world, as though fearing lest some recess of the earth escape it.

[139071] 22.8. For it left neither island nor cave nor mountain-top having men dwelling on it; and if it passed by any country, either not touching the men there or touching them somewhat, yet at a later time, returning there again, of the dwellers round about, on whom it had fallen most bitterly before, it touched none at all, but did not stand off from that country before it had rightly and justly rendered the measure of the dead, equal to that of those dwelling round it who had chanced to perish at the former time.

[139587] 22.9. And this disease, ever beginning from the seacoast, thus went up into the inland country. And in the second year it came to Byzantium in mid-spring,

[139743] 22.10. where it befell me too to be sojourning at that time. And it came about thus. Phantoms of demons in every human shape were seen by many, and those who fell in with them thought they were struck by the man they met wherever it chanced of the body, and at the same time they saw this phantom and were at once seized by the disease.

[140081] 22.11. At first, then, those who fell in with them tried to turn them away, repeating the most divine of names and otherwise warding them off, each as he could, but they availed nothing at all, since the most, fleeing even into the temples, perished.

[140333] 22.12. Later they no longer thought fit to give heed even to their friends calling them, but, shutting themselves up in their chambers, pretended that they did not hear, though the doors were beaten upon, fearing plainly lest it be one of the demons calling.

[199938] 29.42. And when the Romans, storming the wall, accomplished nothing, they turned again to digging. And they came to such a point of this work that the foundations of the wall were no longer on the ground, but stood for the most part on emptiness, about to fall, as is likely, very soon.

[200226] 29.43. And if Dagisthaeus had at once wished to set fire to the foundations, I think the city would have been taken straightway; but as it was, awaiting the hopes from the emperor and ever delaying and spending the time, he remained quietly. So these things were being done in the Roman camp here.

[200525] 30.1. But Mermeroes, when he had passed the borders of Iberia with the whole army of the Medes, went forward, keeping the Phasis river on the right; for he by no means wished to go through the places of Lazica, lest some hindrance there should meet him.

[200780] 30.2. For he was eager to save the city of Petra and the Persians there, although a part of the wall had fallen suddenly.

[200903] 30.3. For it was suspended, as I have said; and men of the Roman army to the number of fifty, going of their own will into the city, cried out for the Emperor Justinian as gloriously victorious.

[201099] 30.4. And there led them a certain young man, Armenian by race, John by name, son of Thomas, whom they called by the surname Gouzes.

[201233] 30.5. This Thomas built many of the strongholds about Lazica, the emperor having charged him, and commanded the soldiers there, and seemed prudent to the emperor.

[201397] 30.6. John, then, when the Persians came to blows with them, being struck, at once withdrew with his followers to the camp, since no other of the Roman army came to defend him;

[201575] 30.7. but a Persian man, Mirranes by name, who commanded the garrison in Petra, fearing for the city, charged all the Persians to keep guard most exactly, and himself, sent to Dagisthaeus, held out flattering and deceitful words, agreeing with no trouble to hand over the city not long after. And by this he was able to get round him, so that the city was not at once entered by the Roman army.

[201971] 30.8. And those under Mermeroes, when they came to the narrows, there the Roman garrison, being a hundred, met them, and defended themselves vigorously, and checked those who made trial of the entrance.

[202175] 30.9. But the Persians by no means withdrew, but, others ever receiving those who were killed, went forward, forcing the entrance with all might.

[202322] 30.10. There die of the Persians more than a thousand, and the Romans, killing, gave up, and, the throng forcing them, withdrew and, running up to the heights of the mountains there, were saved.

[202518] 30.11. When Dagisthaeus learned this, he at once broke up the siege, charging the army nothing, and drove to the Phasis river; and all the Romans followed, leaving their own things in the camp.

[202713] 30.12. And the Persians, seeing what was being done, opened the gates and, coming out, went to the huts of the enemy, as though to take the camp.

[202860] 30.13. But the Tzani (for they had not chanced to follow Dagisthaeus) came to the rescue there at a run, and routing the enemy with no trouble killed many.

[203017] 30.14. The Persians, then, fleeing, got within the wall, but the Tzani, having plundered the Roman camp, went straight to Rhizaeum. And thence, coming to Athenae, through the Trapezuntines they were conveyed home.

[203232] 30.15. And Mermeroes and the army of the Medes came there on the ninth day after the retreat of Dagisthaeus; where they found left of the Persian garrison, wounded and become unfit for fighting, three hundred and fifty, and unharmed a hundred and fifty only; for all the rest had died.

[203519] 30.16. Of whom the survivors had by no means thrown the bodies outside the wall, but, choking with the foulness of the smell, held out beyond expectation, that they might give the enemy no eagerness for besieging, since most of them had perished.

[203767] 30.17. And Mermeroes, mocking, said that the state of the Romans was worthy of tears and lamentations, for whom affairs had come to such a point of weakness that they had been unable by any device to take a hundred and fifty Persians without walls.

[204017] 30.18. And he was eager to rebuild as much of the wall as had fallen; but since he had neither lime for the moment nor any other of the things suitable for building in readiness, he devised the following.

[204223] 30.19. Filling linen sacks, in which the Persians had brought provisions for themselves to the land of Colchis, with sand, he set them in the place of the stones, which, being thrown there, became instead of the wall.

[204442] 30.20. And choosing out three thousand of the warlike he left them there, for whom he stored provisions not for a length of time, charging them to take care of the building of the wall; and he himself with all the rest of the army rode back.

[204685] 30.21. And since on the road as he went thence he had no provision of necessaries, having left in Petra all that the army chanced to be bringing from Iberia, he purposed to go by some other journey through the mountains there, where he learned men dwelt,

[204941] 30.22. that they might be able to live by plunder. On this journey one of the men of note among the Lazi, Phubelis by name, ambushed the Persians as they bivouacked, bringing with him Dagisthaeus and two thousand Romans, who killed by a raid some of the Persians pasturing horses, and plundering horses made their withdrawal in a short time. Thus Mermeroes went thence with the army of the Medes.

[205339] 30.23. And Gubazes, learning what had befallen the Romans both in Petra and in the narrows, was not even so afraid, nor did he leave the guard in the narrows over against him, thinking the head of their hope was there.

[205559] 30.24. For he knew well that, even if the Persians, having forced the Romans outside the Phasis river to cross the narrows and to get to Petra, were able, they would from thence fasten no harm on the country of the Lazi, having no device to cross the Phasis, especially as no ships were present to them.

[205864] 30.25. For this river has depth, if any other, most sufficiently, and extends in breadth for a great extent.

[205974] 30.26. And its current has such force that, casting itself into the sea, it goes for a very long way by itself, in no way mingling there. And indeed drinkable water is at hand for those sailing there to draw in the midst of the open sea.

[206213] 30.27. And the Lazi have made guard-posts within the river everywhere, that the enemy, even ferrying with ships, may have no landing onto the land.

[206362] 30.28. And the Emperor Justinian sent to the nation of the Sabiri the money agreed, and gifted Gubazes and the Lazi with other money.

[206497] 30.29. And he chanced much before to have sent another considerable army to Lazica, who had not yet arrived there. Rhecithancus from Thrace led them, a man intelligent and good in war. So these things stood thus.

[206711] 30.30. And Mermeroes, having come into the mountains, as I have said, was eager to fill Petra thence with provisions. For he thought the provisions which they had chanced to bring in with them would by no means suffice the garrison there, being three thousand.

[206973] 30.31. But since what came to their hands barely sufficed for the expense of this army, being not fewer than thirty thousand, and from this they were not able to send anything worth account to Petra, reckoning, he found it better for them that the greater part of the army should depart from the land of Colchis, but that some few should remain there, who were to bring much of the provisions they should meet into the garrison in Petra, and to use the rest themselves sufficiently;

[207457] 30.32. choosing out, then, about five thousand men, he left them there, and set over them three other commanders and Phabrizus.

[207586] 30.33. For it seemed not necessary to him to leave more there, no enemy being anywhere about. And he himself with the rest of the army, coming to Persarmenia, remained quietly in the places about Doubios.

[207792] 30.34. And the five thousand, when they came nearer the extremities of Lazica, all encamped by the Phasis river, and thence, going about in small bands, plundered the places there.

[207974] 30.35. When Gubazes perceived this, he charged Dagisthaeus to come to the rescue there with haste; for the enemy would be able to do them some great harm.

[208130] 30.36. And he did accordingly, and went forward with the whole Roman army, keeping the Phasis river on the left, until he came to a place where the Lazi were encamped on the other bank of the river.

[208330] 30.37. And there the Phasis chanced to be crossable, which the Romans and Persians, through inexperience of the places there, by no means suspected, but the Lazi, knowing it, crossed there suddenly and mingled with the Roman army; and the Persians, choosing out a thousand of the notable among them, sent them, lest anyone should go to do mischief to the camp.

[208692] 30.38. Of whom two, going first on a reconnaissance and beyond expectation falling among the enemy, reported the whole matter.

[208820] 30.39. Therefore the Romans and Lazi fell suddenly upon the thousand, and none of them was able to escape, but the most were destroyed, and some of them they took alive, by whom those under Gubazes and Dagisthaeus were able to learn both the measure of the army of the Medes and the length of the road, and how their present circumstances stood.

[209167] 30.40. Setting out, then, with the whole army, they went against them, reckoning so that they might fall upon them far on in the night; and they were fourteen thousand.

[209337] 30.41. The Persians, then, having nothing hostile in mind, slept a long sleep; for they thought the river uncrossable, and that the thousand, no one having met them,

[209504] 30.42. were going somewhere very far by the road. But the Romans and Lazi, falling unexpectedly upon them at deep dawn, found some still taking sleep, and some just roused from sleep and lying naked on their bedding.

[209722] 30.43. Therefore it befell none of them to look to defence, but the most, being overtaken, died, and some the enemy took alive, among whom it chanced was one of the commanders, and a few, escaping in the dark, were saved.

[209945] 30.44. And the Romans and Lazi take the camp and all the standards, and plundered many arms and great wealth, and a great quantity of horses and mules.

[210098] 30.45. And making the pursuit for a very long way, they came far into Iberia. There too, meeting some other Persians, they killed many.

[210235] 30.46. Thus the Persians departed from Lazica, and the Romans and Lazi, finding there the rest of the provisions and a very great quantity of flour, burned all that the barbarians had brought from Iberia to bring into Petra.

[210461] 30.47. And they left many of the Lazi in the narrows, that the provisions might no longer be able to be brought in to the Persians in Petra, and with the rest of the booty and the captives they turned back.

[210669] 30.48. And the fourth year was ending of the Romans' armistice with the Persians, in the twenty-third year of the Emperor Justinian holding the supreme rule.

[210828] 30.49. And John the Cappadocian had come summoned by the emperor to Byzantium a year before. For at that time there came upon Theodora the empress the day that ends life.

[211000] 30.50. He recovered, however, none at all of his former dignities, but on his unwilling honour remained a priest; and yet a phantom had often come to the man that he would attain the kingship.

[211194] 30.51. For the demon, which is wont to glamour men, loves to hang from greater and loftier hopes those whose mind chances not to stand on a firm nature.

[211348] 30.52. And to this John, then, wonder-telling visions ever foretold many other things, and that he must put on the garb of Augustus.

[211482] 30.53. Now there was a certain priest in Byzantium, Augustus by name, who had the guard of the treasures of the church of Sophia.

[211613] 30.54. When, then, John, having had his hair shorn, was thought worthy of the priesthood by force—for he had no garment befitting a priest—from this Augustus, being near at hand, he was compelled by those set over the work to put on the chasuble and the tunic, and to this, I think, the prophecy was for him fulfilled.
