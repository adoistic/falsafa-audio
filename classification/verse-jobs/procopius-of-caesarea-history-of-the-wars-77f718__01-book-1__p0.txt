WORK: History of the Wars  (slug: procopius-of-caesarea-history-of-the-wars-77f718)

CHAPTER: 01-book-1



[0] 1.1. Procopius of Caesarea has written the history of the wars which Justinian, Emperor of the Romans, waged against the barbarians of the East and of the West, recording each of them as it happened to come about, so that the great course of time might not, by mastering deeds of surpassing magnitude and leaving them bereft of record, surrender them to oblivion and render them altogether lost—deeds whose memory he himself believed would prove a great thing and most useful both to those now living and to those who shall come after, should time at any point again bring men into some like necessity.

[604] 1.2. For to those who intend to make war, and to those who will contend in any other struggle, the display of a kindred history can furnish some benefit, revealing how the like contests have turned out for men of former times, and hinting at what sort of end, in all likelihood, the present circumstances will have for those who plan them best.

[950] 1.3. And the writer knew himself to be of all men most capable of composing this history, for no other reason than that, having been chosen as counsellor to the general Belisarius, it fell to him to be present at very nearly all that was done.

[1195] 1.4. He held it fitting that to rhetoric belongs cleverness, to poetry the invention of tales,

[1291] 1.5. but to history the truth. And so he has not concealed the base deeds even of those most intimate with him, but has set down with exactness each thing that befell them all, whether they chanced to act well or in some other way.

[1524] 1.6. Nothing will appear greater or mightier than the events that have come to pass in these wars, to anyone who wishes to judge truly.

[1661] 1.7. For in them there have been accomplished feats more marvellous than any we know of by report—unless one who reads of these things should grant pride of place to the ancient past and refuse to think the deeds of his own day worthy of wonder.

[1908] 1.8. Thus, for instance, some call the soldiers of the present day "archers," while they wish to assign the names "close-fighters" and "shield-bearers" and the like to the men of old, and they suppose that this kind of valour has by no means come down to the present time, making a judgement about it that is uninformed and very far removed from experience.

[2267] 1.9. For it has never occurred to them that the archers in Homer, who from this very skill came to be named with reproach, had no horse beneath them, no spear, no shield to ward off a blow, nor any other guard for the body; but they went on foot into battle, and were compelled to hide themselves, choosing the shield of some comrade or crouching behind a tombstone upon a grave,

[2648] 1.10. where they could neither save themselves if they turned to flight, nor fall upon the enemy as they fled, nor yet fight openly, but always seemed to be doing something stealthy in the engagement.

[2850] 1.11. And apart from this, they used the craft so feebly that, drawing the string only to the breast, they then let fly the arrow, which would naturally prove weak and of no account to those who received it. Such, plainly, was archery in former times.

[3103] 1.12. But the archers of today go into battle wearing corselets and fitted with greaves up to the knee. From the right side hang their arrows,

[3247] 1.13. from the other the sword. There are some who have a spear also slung beside them, and on the shoulders a small shield without a grip, such as to cover the parts about the face and the neck.

[3444] 1.14. They ride most skilfully, and even while the horse is running at full speed they are able without difficulty to bend the bow to either side, and to shoot both at pursuing enemies and at fleeing ones.

[3651] 1.15. The string is drawn back along the forehead, close to the right ear, filling the arrow with such force that it kills whomever it strikes, and neither shield nor corselet can in any way turn aside the rush of it.

[3870] 1.16. Yet there are some who, taking no thought of these things, reverence and stand in awe of antiquity and grant nothing more to the inventions of our own time. But none of this will prevent the events of these wars from having been the greatest and most worthy of record.

[4146] 1.17. It shall be told, then, beginning a little further back, what befell the Romans and the Medes in their wars, both to suffer and to do.

[4288] 2.1. When Arcadius, Emperor of the Romans, was about to end his life at Byzantium (for he had a son, Theodosius, not yet weaned), he was at a loss concerning both the child and the empire, being able to provide well for neither.

[4518] 2.2. For the thought came to him that, if he should furnish Theodosius with some partner in the sovereignty, he would in effect be destroying his own son, by bringing against him as an enemy one clothed with imperial power;

[4743] 2.3. but that if he should set him alone over the realm, many would lay claim to the empire, taking advantage, as was likely, of the child's defencelessness, and rising in revolt would seize the tyranny with no trouble, after destroying Theodosius—since the boy had no kinsman in Byzantium who could be his guardian.

[5061] 2.4. For he by no means hoped that Honorius his uncle would help, since the affairs of Italy were already in evil case.

[5182] 2.5. And no less did the situation with the Medes disturb him, for he feared that these barbarians might run down the youth of the sovereign and work irreparable harm upon the Romans.

[5367] 2.6. Having fallen into this perplexity, Arcadius, though in other respects not quick-witted, devised a plan which had power easily to preserve both his son and his realm—whether he consulted with some of the learned men, of whom many are accustomed to attend upon a king, or whether some divine inspiration came to him.

[5689] 2.7. For in drawing up the documents of his will, he declared the boy successor to the sovereignty, and set over him as guardian Yazdgerd, the King of the Persians, on whom he laid many charges in the will, to preserve the empire for Theodosius with all his strength and forethought.

[5974] 2.8. Thus Arcadius, having disposed of both the empire and his private affairs, died. And Yazdgerd, King of the Persians, when he saw this document brought to him—being even before this most renowned for the greatness of his character—displayed a virtue worthy of much wonder and report.

[6263] 2.9. For holding the injunctions of Arcadius in no neglect, he maintained an unbroken peace toward the Romans throughout the whole time, and preserved the empire for Theodosius.

[6442] 2.10. At once, indeed, he wrote a letter to the Roman Senate, not refusing to be guardian of the Emperor Theodosius, and threatening war if anyone should attempt to set in motion a plot against him.

[6642] 2.11. But when Theodosius had become a man and reached full maturity, and Yazdgerd had fallen sick and passed from among men, Varahran, King of the Persians, invaded the land of the Romans with a great army; yet he did no unkindness, but returned unsuccessful to his own country, in the following manner.

[6948] 2.12. The Emperor Theodosius happened to have sent Anatolius, the General of the East, alone as an envoy to the Persians; and he, when he had come very near to the army of the Medes, leaped down from his horse alone and went on foot toward Varahran.

[7199] 2.13. And Varahran, seeing him, asked those present who this man approaching might be. And they said that he was the General of the Romans.

[7340] 2.14. Then the King, astounded at the surpassing honour shown him, himself turned his horse about and rode back, and the whole host of the Persians followed him.

[7503] 2.15. And when he had come into his own land, he received the envoy with much friendliness, and granted the peace just as Anatolius desired, on this condition, however, that neither party should build any new fortification in its own territory near the borders of the other. And when this had been arranged, both sides carried on their own affairs as they wished.

[7868] 3.1. At a later time Perozes, King of the Persians, waged war against the nation of the Ephthalite Huns, whom they call White, over the boundaries of their land; and having gathered a considerable army he marched against them.

[8096] 3.2. The Ephthalites are indeed a Hunnic nation and are so called, yet they do not mingle with, nor are they neighbours to, any of the Huns we know; for they have no land bordering theirs, nor do they dwell anywhere near them. Rather they dwell next to the Persians toward the north wind, where there is a city named Gorgo close to the very edges of Persia; and there they are accustomed to dispute with one another over the boundaries of the land.

[8546] 3.3. For they are not nomads, like the other Hunnic peoples, but have settled from of old upon a good land.

[8655] 3.4. Therefore they have never made any invasion into the land of the Romans except together with the army of the Medes. Alone among the Huns these are white in body and not ill-favoured in countenance.

[8859] 3.5. Nor do they have a manner of life like theirs, nor do they live any beast-like existence as those others do; but they are ruled by a single king, and possessing a lawful constitution they deal with one another and with their neighbours always uprightly and justly,

[9130] 3.6. no less than the Romans and the Persians. The well-to-do among them make companions of friends, up to twenty, should it so chance, or even more, who become their fellow-banqueters forever, and share in all their possessions, since there is among them a kind of common right to these things.

[9427] 3.7. And whenever the one who made them his companions happens to die, it is their custom that these men be carried alive into the tomb together with him.

[9583] 3.8. When Perozes marched against these Ephthalites, there accompanied him an envoy who happened to have been sent to him by the Emperor Zeno, Eusebius by name. Now the Ephthalites, by way of giving their enemies the impression that they had taken fright at their onset and had rushed to flight, went at a run into a certain place encircled on all sides by precipitous mountains, which were thickly covered with abundant trees.

[10012] 3.9. And to one advancing within the mountains as far as possible there appeared a broad road in the midst, reaching to a great length, but it had no exit anywhere at its end; rather it terminated at the very circle of the mountains.

[10247] 3.10. Perozes, then, giving no thought to any trick, and not reflecting that he was journeying in a foreign land, pursued without taking heed.

[10391] 3.11. Of the Huns, a few fled before him, but the greater part, hiding themselves unobserved in the difficult ground, got behind the rear of the enemy's army, and did not yet wish to make themselves manifest, so that the Persians, having advanced far into the ambush and gone deep within the mountains, might no longer be able to turn back.

[10733] 3.12. When the Medes perceived this (for already some hint of the danger was appearing), they themselves, out of fear of Perozes, kept silence about their present plight, but earnestly entreated Eusebius to make some exhortation to the king—now that he was far removed from his own evils—to deliberate rather than to be rashly bold out of season, and to consider whether there might be some device that could lead to safety.

[11159] 3.13. And he, coming into the presence of Perozes, by no means disclosed the present misfortune, but beginning with a fable he said that once a lion had come upon a goat bound and bleating on a piece of ground not very high, and that the lion, eager to make a meal of it, had rushed forward to seize it, but had fallen into a very deep pit, having a circular path narrow and unending (for it had no way out at all), which the owners of the goat had cunningly contrived for the purpose, setting the goat above the pit as a snare for the lion.

[11702] 3.14. When Perozes heard this he fell into fear, lest perchance the Medes had made the pursuit against the enemy to their own undoing. And he no longer advanced, but remaining where he was took counsel about the situation.

[11926] 3.15. But the Huns, now following openly, kept guard over the entrance to the place, so that the enemy might no longer be able to ride back out.

[12072] 3.16. And then at last the Persians, having perceived clearly the evil they were in, accounted their situation a calamity, having for the future no hope at all of escaping the danger.

[12257] 3.17. The King of the Ephthalites, sending to Perozes some of his followers, reproached him much for his unreasoning rashness, by which he had brought himself and the Persian race to destruction in no orderly fashion; and yet even so he promised that the Huns would grant them safety, provided that Perozes were willing both to do obeisance to him, as having become his master, and, swearing the oaths customary among them, to give pledges that the Persians would never make an expedition against the nation of the Ephthalites.

[12786] 3.18. When Perozes heard this, he consulted with the Magi who were present and asked whether he ought to do what the enemy proposed.

[12920] 3.19. The Magi answered that, as concerning the oath, it was for him to dispose of it as he wished; but as for the other matter, he should outwit his enemy by cunning.

[13089] 3.20. For it was their custom, they said, to do obeisance to the risings of the sun each day.

[13184] 3.21. He must therefore, watching the time exactly, meet with the ruler of the Ephthalites just at daybreak, and turning somewhere toward the rising sun do obeisance; for in this way he would be able thereafter to escape the disgrace of the act.

[13431] 3.22. So Perozes gave the pledges concerning the peace and did obeisance to his enemy just as the counsel of the Magi prescribed; and with the whole Median army unharmed he gladly withdrew homeward.

[13631] 4.1. Not long afterward, disregarding what he had sworn, he wished to punish the Huns for the insult done to him.

[13746] 4.2. At once, then, gathering all the Persians and their allies from every land, he led them against the Ephthalites, leaving behind only one of his sons, Cabades by name (for at that time he chanced to have come of age), but taking with him all the rest, some thirty in number.

[14026] 4.3. The Ephthalites, learning of his approach and grieved that they had been deceived by their enemies, reproached their king for having abandoned their cause to the Medes.

[14201] 4.4. And he with a laugh asked them what it was that he had abandoned of theirs—whether the land, or their arms, or any other of all their possessions.

[14354] 4.5. And they answered that it was nothing else than the moment, on which it happens that all else depends.

[14463] 4.6. They thought, then, that they should meet the invaders with all eagerness, but he in the present case prevented them. For he insisted that nothing certain had yet become known to them about the invasion, since the Persians were still in their own land; and remaining where he was he did as follows.

[14768] 4.7. In the plain where the Persians were going to break into the homeland of the Ephthalites, he cut off a great stretch of country for a long distance and dug a trench deep and of sufficient breadth, leaving in the middle a small unbroken space of ground, enough for a passage of ten horses.

[15063] 4.8. And laying reeds over the trench above and heaping earth upon the reeds, he concealed it on the surface; and he charged the throng of the Huns that, whenever they were about to ride back from there, they should draw themselves together into a few and go more slowly through the firm ground, guarding against falling into the excavated places;

[15412] 4.9. and the salts he hung from the top of the royal standard, by which Perozes had earlier sworn the oath, which, disregarding, he had then made his expedition against the Huns.

[15592] 4.10. So long, then, as he heard that the enemy were in their own land, he waited quietly; but when he learned from his scouts that they had come to the city of Gorgo—which lies at the extreme borders of Persia—and that departing thence they were now advancing by the road against them, he himself with the greater part of the army remained within the trench, but sending a few he ordered them to be seen by the enemy in the plain far off, and being seen, then merely to flee back at full speed, keeping in mind his instructions about the pit when they came near to it.

[16163] 4.11. And they did accordingly; and when they had come very near the channel, drawing themselves together into a few they all crossed over and mingled with the rest of the army.

[16342] 4.12. But the Persians, having no understanding of the plot, pursued at full force across the very flat plain, holding to the enemy with great fury, and they all fell into the trench—not only the foremost, but also as many as followed behind;

[16586] 4.13. for, since they were making the pursuit with great passion, as has been said, they by no means perceived the disaster that had befallen those going in front, but, falling upon them with their horses and spears, they killed those men, as was likely, and themselves perished no less along with them.

[16891] 4.14. Among them was Perozes too, with all his sons. And they say that, as he was about to fall into this pit, he perceived the danger and, taking off the pearl that hung from his right ear—a pearl of the whitest hue and prized for its extraordinary size—he threw it away, so that no one might wear it afterward, since it was prodigiously worth seeing, such as had never before belonged to any other king; but to me they tell things not to be believed.

[17345] 4.15. For he would not, when in such peril, have come to give thought to anything else whatsoever; rather, I think, his very ear was crushed together with him in that calamity, and the pearl was somewhere lost.

[17557] 4.16. This pearl the Emperor of the Romans afterward was eager to buy from the Ephthalites, but was wholly unable; for the barbarians could not find it, though they made the search with much labour. They say, however, that the Ephthalites found it later and sold it to Cabades.

[17836] 4.17. What the Persians tell about this pearl is worth relating; for to someone perhaps the account may seem not altogether incredible.

[17973] 4.18. The Persians say, then, that this pearl was in an oyster in the sea which is among the Persians, and that the oyster swam not far from the shore there; and that both its shells were open, in the midst of which the pearl stood, a sight worthy of much account. For nothing else could be likened to it in all time, either in size or in beauty.

[18321] 4.19. A sea-monster of huge size and terribly fierce became enamoured of this sight and followed in its track, relaxing neither by night nor by day; and even when it was compelled to attend to its food, it would there look about for something edible, and finding and taking up something would eat it as quickly as possible, and then, overtaking the oyster again, would fill itself once more with the sight of its beloved.

[18744] 4.20. And once, they say, one of the fishermen looked on at what was happening, but in terror of the beast shrank from the danger, and brought the whole story to King Perozes.

[18921] 4.21. And on Perozes hearing it, they say there came upon him a great longing for the pearl, and with many flatteries and hopes of good things he urged on this fisherman.

[19093] 4.22. And he, unable to resist a master making his request, said this to Perozes: "O master, money is desirable to a man, but life is more desirable, and yet most worthy of all are children.

[19285] 4.23. Constrained by natural affection for them, a man might perhaps venture all things. I, then, will make trial of the beast, and I have hope of making you

[19444] 4.24. master of the pearl. And if I prevail in this contest, it is clear that from then on I shall be reckoned among those called blessed. For it is not unlikely that you, as King of Kings, will gift me with all good things; and for me it will suffice, even if, as may happen, I have gained nothing, to have become the benefactor of my master.

[19789] 4.25. But if it must be that I am taken by this beast, then it will be your task, O King, to recompense my children for their father's death.

[19932] 4.26. For thus I, even when dead, shall be paid in the dearest coin, and you will win a greater repute for virtue. For in benefiting my little ones you will do good to me, who shall in no wise know to thank you for the kindness. For this alone would be true gratitude: that which is shown toward the dead."

[20240] 4.27. Having said so much, he departed. And when he came to the place where the oyster was wont to swim and the monster to follow it, there he sat upon a rock, watching for an opportunity, if somehow he might catch the pearl bereft of its lover.

[20487] 4.28. And as soon as it happened that the monster met with some prey suitable for its meal and was occupied about this, the fisherman, leaving on the beach those who followed him for this service, went with great haste straight for the oyster, and having now laid hold of it he hastened to get clear with all speed.

[20804] 4.29. The monster, perceiving this, came to the rescue. And the fisherman, seeing it, since he was about to be overtaken not far from the shore, hurled his catch onto the land with all his might, but he himself was caught and destroyed not long after.

[21057] 4.30. Those left on the beach took up the pearl, carried it to the king, and reported all that had happened.

[21167] 4.31. Thus, then, the Persians say it befell concerning this pearl, in the way that has been told. But I return to my former narrative.

[21304] 4.32. In this way Perozes perished, and the whole Persian army. For if anyone chanced not to have fallen into the channel, he was made captive by the hands of the enemy.

[21475] 4.33. And from this a law was laid down among the Persians, never, when driving through hostile land, to make any pursuit, even should it happen that they had routed their adversaries by main force.

[21675] 4.34. Those, however, who had not joined the expedition with Perozes, but had remained in their own country—these chose for themselves as king Cabades, the youngest son of Perozes, who at that time alone survived.

[21890] 4.35. Then the Persians became subject to the Ephthalites for the payment of tribute, until Cabades, having most firmly secured his rule, no longer thought fit to render them the yearly tribute. And these barbarians ruled over the Persians for two years.

[22146] 5.1. After this Cabades, governing his rule more violently, introduced other innovations into the constitution, and in particular wrote a law that the Persians should have their women in common; which by no means pleased the people. Therefore, rising against him, they deprived him of the rule and, binding him, kept him in prison.

[22479] 5.2. And they chose as king for themselves Blases, the brother of Perozes, since no male offspring of Perozes was any longer left, as has been said, and it is not lawful among the Persians for a man of private birth to be set over the kingship, except when the royal line is wholly extinct.

[22771] 5.3. Blases, on receiving the kingship, gathered the noblest of the Persians and took counsel concerning Cabades; for it was not the wish of the majority to put the man to death.

[22951] 5.4. There, then, many opinions were spoken, tending to either side; and there came forward one of the men of note among the Persians, by name Gousanastades, of the rank of chanaranges (this would be among the Persians a general), holding command near the very edges of the land of the Persians, in a region which borders the Ephthalites; and showing the knife with which the Persians are accustomed to trim the projecting parts of their nails, in length about that of a man's finger, in thickness not even a third of a finger, he said, "You see this knife,

[23510] 5.5. small as it utterly is; this nevertheless is able at present to accomplish a task which, be well assured a little later, O dearest Persians, two myriads of armoured men would not be able to perform." Thus he spoke,

[23731] 5.6. signifying that, unless they should slay Cabades, he would at once, if he survived, cause trouble for the Persians.

[23853] 5.7. But they by no means resolved to kill a man of the royal blood; rather they shut him up in a fortress which they have been accustomed to call the Castle of Oblivion.

[24025] 5.8. For if anyone happens to have been cast into this place, the law no longer permits there to be any mention of him, but death is the penalty for one who names him; and for this reason it has obtained from the Persians this appellation.

[24266] 5.9. But once, the history of the Armenians says, the force of the law concerning the Castle of Oblivion was relaxed for the Persians, in the following manner.

[24427] 5.10. A war once arose between the Persians and the Armenians, undeclared, for two-and-thirty years, in the reign of Pacurius over the Persians, and of Arsaces, a man of the Arsacid line, over the Armenians. And in this war, as it was prolonged, it befell that both were exceedingly afflicted, and the Armenians beyond measure.

[24756] 5.11. But holding each other in much distrust, neither side was able to send a herald to the other. In the meanwhile it chanced that the Persians had to carry on a war against certain other barbarians dwelling not far from the Armenians.

[24995] 5.12. The Armenians, then, being eager to make a display to the Persians of their goodwill and peaceableness toward them, resolved to invade the land of these barbarians, having first declared this to the Persians.

[25211] 5.13. And falling upon them unexpectedly, they killed very nearly all of military age. Pacurius, overjoyed at what had been done, sent some of his intimates to Arsaces and, having given him pledges, summoned the man.

[25429] 5.14. And when Arsaces came to him, he deemed him worthy both of all other friendliness and held him as a brother on equal and like terms.

[25569] 5.15. And then, binding Arsaces with the most dread oaths, and himself swearing no less in turn that for the future the Persians and the Armenians should be well-disposed allies to one another, he straightway sent him back to go to his ancestral home.

[25822] 5.16. Not long afterward certain men slandered Arsaces, as though he wished to attempt revolutionary measures. Persuaded by them, Pacurius again summoned him, intimating that he was concerned to consult with him about matters of general import.

[26068] 5.17. And he, without any delay, came to him, bringing with him others of the most warlike among the Armenians, and Bassikius, who was his general and counsellor; for he had attained the highest degree of manliness and intelligence.

[26302] 5.18. At once, then, Pacurius reproached both of them, Arsaces and Bassikius, with many rebukes, saying that, having disregarded their oaths, they had so quickly turned to apostasy. But they denied it and swore most persistently that they had taken no such counsel among themselves.

[26586] 5.19. At first, then, Pacurius kept them in dishonour, but afterward asked the Magi what he ought to do concerning them.

[26708] 5.20. The Magi by no means thought it just to condemn men who denied the charge and had not been clearly convicted; but they suggested to him a certain device, by which Arsaces himself might be forced openly to accuse himself.

[26936] 5.21. For they bade him cover the floor of the royal tent with earth, half from the country of the Persians, and the other half from Armenia. And the king did accordingly.

[27109] 5.22. Then the Magi, seizing the whole tent with certain enchantments, bade the king take his walks there together with Arsaces, charging him with having broken the things agreed and sworn.

[27300] 5.23. And they too must be present at the conversation; for thus they would be witnesses of all that was said. At once, then, Pacurius summoned Arsaces and walked to and fro in the tent with him, the Magi being there present with them, and asked the man for what reason, having disregarded his oaths, he was again attempting to grind down the Persians and Armenians with irremediable evils.

[27692] 5.24. And Arsaces, so long as the conversation took place in the part where the soil from the Persian land lay, denied it, and swearing by the most dread oaths protested that he was a faithful slave of Pacurius;

[27905] 5.25. but when, in the midst of speaking, he came to the centre of the tent, where they stepped onto the soil of Armenia, there, constrained by I know not what, he suddenly changed these words to greater boldness, and ceased no longer threatening Pacurius and the Persians, but promised to take vengeance on them for this outrage as soon as he should become master of himself.

[28283] 5.26. And speaking thus and behaving insolently, he made the whole circuit of his walk, until, turning back, he came again onto the soil from the land of the Persians. For there, as though singing some recantation, he became a suppliant and uttered piteous words to Pacurius.

[28560] 5.27. But when he came again onto the soil of the Armenians, he passed over to threats. And being thus changed many times to either side, he concealed none of his secrets.

[28733] 5.28. Then at last the Magi condemned him for having broken faith both with the treaties and the oaths. And Pacurius, having flayed off the skin of Bassikius and made of it a wineskin and filled it wholly with chaff, hung it on a very tall tree.

[28980] 5.29. Arsaces, however (for he had no way to kill a man of the royal blood), he shut up in the Castle of Oblivion.

[29096] 5.30. At a later time one of the Armenians, who was among the most intimate of Arsaces and had followed him when he went to the country of the Persians, joined an expedition with the Persians against a certain barbarian nation; and this man proved valiant in the toil, while Pacurius looked on at what was being done, and was the foremost cause of the victory for the Persians.

[29475] 5.31. Therefore Pacurius bade him ask whatever he wished, declaring that he would fail of nothing from him.

[29584] 5.32. And he asked for nothing else than that he might wait upon Arsaces for one day in whatever way he wished.

[29697] 5.33. This grieved the king exceedingly, that he should be compelled to break so ancient a law; yet, that he might in all things keep faith, he granted that the request be fulfilled.

[29881] 5.34. And when, by the king's command, he had come to the Castle of Oblivion, he embraced Arsaces, and both, throwing their arms about each other, made a sweet lament and bewailed their present fortune, and each could hardly bring himself to loose his hands from the other.

[30156] 5.35. But when they had come to satiety of their lamentations and ceased, the Armenian bathed Arsaces and adorned him not carelessly in all else, and putting upon him the royal robe reclined him upon a couch.

[30366] 5.36. And there Arsaces feasted those present in royal fashion, just as he had been wont to do in former times.

[30479] 5.37. At this banquet many words were spoken over the cup which pleased Arsaces well, and many other things came up which were a delight to him; and as the drinking was prolonged until night, they took extraordinary pleasure in their converse with one another, and hardly could they part from each other and break up,

[30798] 5.38. soaked through with their good cheer. Then, they say, Arsaces said that, having spent the sweetest of days and having been in company in it with the most longed-for of all men, he could no longer endure of his own will to undergo the wretchedness of life,

[31061] 5.39. and so saying, he made away with himself with a knife which he chanced to have stolen on purpose at the feast, and thus he vanished from among men.

[31216] 5.40. Such, then, as concerning this Arsaces, the history of the Armenians says befell in the manner that has been told, and that then the law concerning the Castle of Oblivion was relaxed. But I must return to where I left off.

[31446] 6.1. While Cabades was shut up, his wife tended him, going in to him and bringing him his necessaries; and the keeper of the prison began to make trial of her, for she was exceedingly comely in appearance.

[31653] 6.2. When Cabades learned this from his wife, he bade her give herself to the man to use as he wished. So the keeper of the fortress, lying with the woman, fell in love with her with an extraordinary love,

[31860] 6.3. and from this he allowed her to make her entrances to her husband wherever she wished, and again to depart from there, with no one standing in the way. Now there was one of the men of note among the Persians, Seoses by name, very dear to Cabades,

[32113] 6.4. who kept himself near this fortress, watching for an opportunity, if somehow he might be able to take him out from within.

[32242] 6.5. And through the woman he signified to Cabades that horses and men were in readiness for him not far from the fortress, indicating to him a certain place.

[32402] 6.6. And once, when night had come on, Cabades persuaded his wife to give him her own dress, and herself, putting on his garments, to sit in the prison in his stead, where he had been sitting.

[32596] 6.7. Thus, then, Cabades departed from the prison. And those to whom this guard was assigned, seeing him, supposed that it was the woman; therefore they resolved neither to hinder nor otherwise to trouble him.

[32807] 6.8. And at daybreak, seeing the woman in the chamber in her husband's garments, and being far from the truth, they thought that Cabades was there. And this impression held strong for many days,

[33003] 6.9. until Cabades had got far along on his way. Now what befell the woman, when the plot came to light, and in what way they punished her, I cannot say exactly. For the Persians do not agree with one another; and therefore I leave it to be told.

[33251] 6.10. Cabades, unobserved by all, came with Seoses to the Ephthalite Huns, and the king gave him his own daughter to wife, and thus, as to a son-in-law, sent with him a considerable army against the Persians.

[33461] 6.11. This army the Persians by no means wished to oppose, but one in one direction, another in another, rushed to flight.

[33585] 6.12. And when Cabades came into the country where Gousanastades held command, he said to some of his intimates that he would make that man chanaranges who should that day be the first of the Persians to come into his presence and offer his service.

[33836] 6.13. But having said it he already repented of his words, for a law came into his mind which does not permit the Persians to confer offices on aliens, but on those to whom each honour belongs by birth.

[34040] 6.14. For he feared lest someone first come to him who was not a kinsman of the chanaranges, and he be compelled to break the law in order to keep his own word true.

[34207] 6.15. But while he had this in mind, some chance befell so that he need not, by being true to his word, dishonour the law. For Adergoudounbades happened to come to him first, a young man, kinsman of Gousanastades and exceedingly valiant in war.

[34453] 6.16. He addressed Cabades as master and was the first to do obeisance to him as king, and begged that he might be used by him as a slave in whatever way he wished.

[34619] 6.17. Cabades, then, coming with no trouble into the palace, and finding Blases bereft of defenders, blinded him in the manner in which the Persians are accustomed to blind malefactors—either boiling oil and pouring it, while at its hottest, into the eyes held open, or heating some iron pin red-hot and with it searing the inner parts of the eyes—and for the rest kept him in prison; he had ruled over the Persians for two years.

[35051] 6.18. And having killed Gousanastades, he set Adergoudounbades in his place over the office of chanaranges, and at once proclaimed Seoses adrastadaran salanes. This signifies the one set over all offices and over all soldiers together.

[35288] 6.19. This office Seoses alone, and first, held among the Persians; for it had belonged to no one either before or after. And Cabades secured the kingship and preserved it in safety. For he was quick-witted and effective, second to none.

[35527] 7.1. A little later Cabades owed money to the King of the Ephthalites, which, when he was unable to pay him, he asked the Roman Emperor Anastasius to lend him this money. And he, consulting with some of his intimates, inquired whether he ought to do this.

[35784] 7.2. And they would not allow him to make the contract. For they declared it inexpedient to make for the enemy a more secure friendship with the Ephthalites by means of their own money—men whom it was far better for them to set against one another as much as possible.

[36054] 7.3. Therefore Cabades resolved, from no cause whatever, to make an expedition against the Romans. And first he came of his own accord upon the country of the Armenians, and after plundering most of it in a raid he came suddenly to the city of Amida, lying in Mesopotamia, which in the season of winter he set about besieging.

[36382] 7.4. The Amidenes, though no soldiers were with them—since they were in peace and good fortune—and being otherwise altogether unprepared, nevertheless by no means wished to yield to the enemy, but held out against the dangers and the hardship beyond expectation.

[36646] 7.5. There was among the Syrians a righteous man, Jacob by name, who had practised the things of God with exactness. This man, in a place called Endielon, distant from Amida a day's journey, had for many years before shut himself up, that he might be able the more securely to meditate upon piety.

[36945] 7.6. And the people there, serving his purpose, set about him certain palisades, not joined together, however, but fixed apart from one another, so that those approaching could see him and converse with him.

[37154] 7.7. And they built him a small roof above, just enough to ward off rains and snowfalls. There from of old this man sat, yielding least of all to either heat or cold, and living on certain seeds, on which he was wont to feed not daily, but at long intervals.

[37414] 7.8. This Jacob, then, some of the Ephthalites, while overrunning the country there, saw, and with great haste they bent their bows and wished to shoot him. But the hands of them all became motionless and could by no means make the bows work.

[37658] 7.9. When this, carried about through the camp, came to Cabades, he wished to become an eyewitness of the thing, and on seeing it he fell into great amazement together with the Persians present, and entreated Jacob to remit to the barbarians their offence. And he remitted it with a single word, and the terror was loosed for the men.

[37994] 7.10. Cabades, then, bade the man ask whatever he wished, thinking that he would ask great riches, and even boasting that he should fail of nothing from him.

[38153] 7.11. But he begged him to grant him the men—as many as in this war should come to him as refugees. This request Cabades fulfilled, and he gave letters as a pledge of their safety. Many, therefore, streaming together from every side, found refuge there; for the deed became famous. So much for that.

[38454] 7.12. Cabades, besieging Amida, brought up the engine called the ram against the wall on every side. And the Amidenes always checked the battering with certain crossbeams; but he did not desist, until he learned that by this means the wall was not to be taken.

[38716] 7.13. For though he often struck it, he was by no means able to bring down any part of the wall or to shake it, so securely had the building been wrought by those who built it of old.

[38901] 7.14. Having failed in this, Cabades made an artificial mound as a siege-work against the city, far overtopping the height of the wall. And the besieged, beginning within the wall, dug a tunnel as far as the mound, and secretly carrying out the earth from there they hollowed out the inner part of the mound for a great extent. The outside, however, remained in the shape it had been given, affording no one any perception of what was being done.

[39349] 7.15. Many Persians, then, climbing up as upon something secure, got onto the height and planned to shoot down from there upon those within the wall. But as the throng streamed up at a run, the mound suddenly collapsed and killed very nearly all of them.

[39605] 7.16. Cabades, at a loss in his present circumstances, resolved to break off the siege, and on the next day gave orders to the army to withdraw.

[39751] 7.17. Then the besieged, as though now careless of the danger, mocked the barbarians much with laughter from the wall.

[39871] 7.18. And certain courtesans, lifting up their dress with no decorum, displayed to Cabades, who chanced to be standing near, those parts of women which it is not lawful to be seen naked by men.

[40066] 7.19. The Magi, seeing this, came into the king's presence and prevented the withdrawal, declaring, in interpretation of what had happened, that the Amidenes would before long show Cabades all their secret and hidden things. Thus, then, the camp of the Persians remained where it was.

[40352] 7.20. A few days later one of the Persians saw, near a certain tower, the outlet of an old conduit, not securely covered, but only with small pebbles and not very many.

[40522] 7.21. And coming there alone by night and making trial of the entrance, he got within the wall. And at daybreak he reported the whole matter to Cabades. And he, on the following night, having ladders prepared, came there with a few men. And a favourable chance fell to him in the following way.

[40818] 7.22. The tower which chanced to be nearest the conduit had been allotted to be guarded by the most temperate of the Christians, whom they have been accustomed to call monks. These happened on that day to be keeping a certain yearly festival to God.

[41069] 7.23. And when night came on, all of them, since they had passed it in much toil because of the celebration, and rather more had come to satiety of food and drink than was their wont, were sleeping a sweet and gentle sleep, and from this perceived nothing at all of what was being done.

[41357] 7.24. The Persians, then, coming by the conduit within the wall in small numbers, climbed up into the tower, and finding the monks still sleeping, killed them all.

[41522] 7.25. When Cabades learned this, he brought up the ladders to the wall very near this tower.

[41616] 7.26. It was already day. And of the Amidenes who were keeping guard in the adjoining tower, perceiving the disaster, hastened in haste to the rescue there.

[41774] 7.27. And both sides used much pushing for a long time against each other, and the Amidenes, already getting the upper hand, killed many of those who had climbed up and thrust back those on the ladders, and were not far from repelling the danger.

[42022] 7.28. But Cabades himself, drawing his scimitar and continually threatening with it, did not let the Persians cease rushing at the ladders, and death was the penalty for those who dared to turn back from there.

[42234] 7.29. Therefore the Persians, by their great multitude becoming superior to their adversaries, conquered them in the battle, and the city was taken by storm on the eightieth day from the beginning of the siege.

[42446] 7.30. And a great slaughter of the Amidenes took place, until, as Cabades was riding into the city, one of the Amidenes, an old man and a priest, came up and said that it was not kingly to murder those who had been captured.

[42672] 7.31. Cabades, then, still held by his anger, answered, "Why, then, did you resolve to make war on me?" And he, taking up the word, at once said, "Because God wished to deliver Amida to you, not by our judgement, but by your

[42898] 7.32. valour." Pleased by this saying, Cabades permitted no one to be killed for the future, but bade the Persians plunder the goods and reckon the survivors as slaves, and charged that all the notable men among them be set apart for himself.

[43142] 7.33. A little later, leaving there a thousand men for the garrison and setting over them as commander Glones, a Persian, and a few wretched men of the Amidenes who were to serve the Persians in their daily living, he himself, with all the rest of the army and with the captives, marched homeward.

[43441] 7.34. Toward these captives he used a humanity befitting a king; for after a short time he allowed them all to go to their own homes,

[43576] 7.35. and in word they had escaped from him; and the Roman Emperor Anastasius performed deeds toward them worthy of virtue. For he remitted to the city all the yearly taxes for seven years, and presented them, both collectively and each individually, with many good things, so that much forgetfulness of what had happened came upon them. But these things took place at a later time.

[43960] 8.1. At that time the Emperor Anastasius, learning that Amida was besieged, sent in haste a sufficient army. The commanders were several, according to the divisions of each, but four generals were set over all: Areobindus, son-in-law of Olybrius, who had been emperor in the West a little before,

[44258] 8.2. and was at that time General of the East; and Celer, commander of the palace regiments (the office the Romans have been accustomed to call magister); and besides these the commanders of the soldiers in Byzantium, Patricius the Phrygian and Hypatius the nephew of the emperor: these were the four generals.

[44570] 8.3. With them also was Justin, who later, on the death of Anastasius, became emperor; and Patriciolus with his son Vitalian, who, taking up arms against the Emperor Anastasius, not long afterward set up a tyranny; and Pharesmanes, by race a Colchian, but exceedingly valiant in war; and Godidisclus and Bessas, Gothic men, of the Goths who had not followed Theoderic when he went from Thrace into Italy, both prodigiously noble and experienced in the affairs of war; and many others, the best, accompanied them.

[45084] 8.4. For such an army, they say, was never gathered against the Persians by the Romans, either before or after. These, however, did not go all assembled into one body, nor making one army, but each himself led the soldiers under him against the enemy.

[45337] 8.5. As paymaster for the expense of the camp Apion the Egyptian was sent, a man eminent among the patricians and exceedingly effective; and the emperor proclaimed him in writing a partner of the empire, so that he might have authority to dispose of matters concerning the expenditure as he wished.

[45637] 8.6. This army, then, was gathered slowly and proceeded somewhat leisurely. Therefore they did not find the barbarians in the land of the Romans, since the Persians, having made their inroad in a raid, at once withdrew to their native land with all the booty.

[45898] 8.7. None of the generals at present wished to undertake the siege of those left in Amida; for they learned that they had brought in much provision; but they were eager to make an invasion into the country of the enemy.

[46119] 8.8. Yet they did not go against the barbarians jointly, but encamping separately from one another they proceeded. Learning this, Cabades (for he chanced to be quite near) came quickly to the Roman borders and met them.

[46340] 8.9. The Romans, however, had not yet learned that Cabades was coming against them with his whole army, but supposed that some small force of the Persians was there.

[46507] 8.10. Those, then, under Areobindus encamped at a place called Arzamon, distant two days' journey from the city of Constantina; and those under Patricius and Hypatius at a place called Siphrios, which is distant from the city of Amida not less than three hundred and fifty stades. For Celer had not yet arrived there.

[46826] 8.11. When Areobindus learned that Cabades was coming against them with all his army, he abandoned the camp and with all his followers rushed to flight, and went at a run to Constantina.

[47014] 8.12. The enemy, coming up a little later, took the camp empty of men, together with the goods. From there they went quickly against the rest of the Roman army.

[47176] 8.13. Those under Patricius and Hypatius, falling in with eight hundred Ephthalites who were going in front of the Persian army, killed very nearly all of them.

[47338] 8.14. And learning nothing about Cabades and the Persian host, as though they had won a victory, they used their daily living more carelessly. So, laying down their arms, they prepared a meal for themselves. For already the time of day brought them to it.

[47595] 8.15. Now a brook flowed in this place, where the Romans began to clean the meat on which they were going to feed.

[47711] 8.16. And some, oppressed by the heat, thought fit to bathe, and the water of the brook, being thus disturbed, went forward. Cabades, learning what had happened to the Ephthalites, went quickly against the enemy;

[47925] 8.17. and seeing the water of the brook fouled, and inferring what was being done, he perceived that his adversaries were unprepared, and gave orders to drive against them with all force. At once they fell upon them as they were feasting and unarmed.

[48177] 8.18. The Romans, not enduring the onset, looked not at all to defence, but fled each as best he could; and of them some, being overtaken, perished, while others, going up onto the mountain that rises there, threw themselves down the precipice with much fear and tumult.

[48449] 8.19. Whence, they say, no one was saved; but Patricius and Hypatius, at the beginning of the onset, were able to escape. Then Cabades, the hostile Huns having broken into his own land, withdrew with all his army homeward, and waged a long war against this nation in the northern parts of the country.

[48752] 8.20. Meanwhile the rest of the Roman army also came, but accomplished nothing worthy of mention, because no one was set as supreme commander of the war, but the generals, being equal to one another, opposed one another in their counsels and were by no means willing to be in one place.

[49040] 8.21. Celer, with his followers, crossing the Nymphius river, made an invasion into Arzanene.

[49135] 8.22. This river is very near Martyropolis, and distant about three hundred stades from Amida. They, having plundered the country there, returned not long after. And in a short time this inroad was completed.

[49345] 9.1. After this Areobindus went to Byzantium, summoned by the emperor, while the rest, coming to Amida in the season of winter, undertook the siege. And by force, although they made many attempts, they were unable to take the place, but they were going to do this by famine; for all provisions had failed the besieged.

[49665] 9.2. But the generals, knowing nothing of the enemy's want, since they saw the soldiers oppressed by the siege and the winter, and at the same time suspected that a Persian army would come against them before long, were eager to depart from there in whatever way.

[49930] 9.3. And the Persians, not knowing how they might fare in these straits, concealed their want of necessaries exactly, giving the impression that they had abundance of all provisions, and wished to withdraw to their own homes under a specious pretext.

[50182] 9.4. There came about, then, on both sides a parley, on the terms that the Persians, receiving a thousand pounds of gold, should give back the city to the Romans. And both sides gladly fulfilled the agreement; and the son of Glones, receiving the money, handed Amida over to the Romans. For Glones had already died in the following way.

[50520] 9.5. While the Romans had not yet encamped there, but were not far from the city of Amida, one of the country folk—who was accustomed to enter the city secretly and to sell to this Glones fowls and bread and many seasonable things for great sums of money—came into the presence of the general Patricius and promised to deliver Glones into his hands together with two hundred Persians, if he might receive from him some hope of recompense.

[50960] 9.6. And he, having promised that all should be as he wished, dismissed the man. And he, rending his garments terribly and looking like one who had wept, entered the city.

[51133] 9.7. And coming to Glones, tearing his hair, he said, "I chanced, O master, to be bringing you all the good things from the country, but soldiers of the Romans, falling upon me (for they go about somewhere in these districts in small bands and do violence to the wretched country folk), inflicted on me blows past bearing, and the robbers went off having taken everything; men whose custom from of old it is both to fear the Persians and to do violence to the husbandmen.

[51606] 9.8. But come, O master, defend yourself and us and the Persians. For if you go out to hunt in the suburbs of the city, you will have no mean quarry. For the accursed wretches go about in fives or fours and rob travellers." Thus

[51836] 9.9. he spoke. And Glones, persuaded, asked the man how many Persians he supposed would suffice for the business.

[51951] 9.10. And he said that fifty would suffice him well; for he could never fall in with more than five of them going along the road; yet, that nothing unexpected might befall them, it was none the worse to take even a hundred to the work; and if even double these, all the better. For no harm could come to a man from the surplus.

[52280] 9.11. Glones, then, choosing out two hundred horsemen, bade the man lead the way for them.

[52372] 9.12. But he insisted that it was better for himself to be sent first on a reconnaissance, and that if, seeing the Romans still going about in the same places, he should report it, then the Persians should in good season make the sally. Glones thought he spoke well, and at his bidding he set out.

[52671] 9.13. And coming to the general Patricius, he told everything; and he sent with him two of his own bodyguards and a thousand soldiers.

[52807] 9.14. These he hid near a village called Thilasamon, distant forty stades from Amida, in glens and wooded places, and charged them to remain there in these ambushes, and himself went at a run to the city.

[53013] 9.15. And telling Glones that the quarry was ready, he led him and the two hundred to the ambush of the enemy. And when they had passed the place where the Romans sat in wait, having escaped the notice of Glones and all the Persians, he both roused the Romans from the ambush and pointed out to them the enemy.

[53325] 9.16. When the Persians saw them coming against them, they were astounded at the unexpectedness and held in great helplessness. For they could neither ride back, since the enemy were at their rear, nor flee anywhere else in a hostile land.

[53566] 9.17. From the present situation, drawing themselves up as for battle, they defended themselves against the assailants; but, being far inferior in number, they were defeated and perished all together with Glones.

[53780] 9.18. When the son of Glones learned this, in his great grief and seething with anger that he could not avenge his father, he burned the church of Symeon, a holy man, where Glones had lodged.

[53973] 9.19. And yet no other building did either Glones or Cabades, nor indeed any other of the Persians, resolve either to pull down or to destroy in any other way in Amida, except this. But I return to my former narrative.

[54193] 9.20. Thus the Romans, by giving the money, recovered Amida two years after it had been taken by the enemy. And when they had come into it, both their own carelessness and the endurance of the Persians' way of life became known.

[54423] 9.21. For reckoning the measure of the food left there and the throng of barbarians who had gone out, they found that provision for about seven days had been left in the city, although Glones and his son had given out the food to the Persians more sparingly than the need required for a long time.

[54722] 9.22. For to the Romans in the city, as I have already shown, who had remained with them, they resolved to furnish nothing at all from the time the enemy began the siege; and these, coming first to unaccustomed foods and laying hands on all things unlawful,

[54981] 9.23. at last in the end even tasted of one another. Therefore the generals perceived that they had been deceived by the barbarians, and reproached the soldiers with their lack of self-control, in that, by showing themselves more disobedient, when it had been possible to take captive so great a multitude of Persians and the son of Glones together with the city, they had instead transferred the Romans' money to the enemy, and so had fastened on themselves a great shame, and had received Amida bought with silver from the Persians.

[55517] 9.24. Later the Persians, as their war against the Huns was prolonged, came to terms with the Romans, which were made for seven years, Celer the Roman and Aspebedes the Persian making them; and both withdrew homeward and remained quiet.

[55755] 9.25. Thus, then, as has been said, the war which had begun between the Romans and the Persians came to this end. But I now go on to relate what befell concerning the Caspian Gates.

[55938] 10.1. The Cilician mountain, the Taurus, passes first by the Cappadocians and the Armenians and the land of those called the Persarmenians, and further by the Albanians and the Iberians, and all the other nations, both independent and subject to the Persians, that dwell there.

[56217] 10.2. For it extends over a great country, and as one advances this mountain reaches an immense thing in breadth and height.

[56343] 10.3. But to one who has crossed the borders of the Iberians there is a certain path in much narrowness, reaching fifty stades.

[56472] 10.4. And this path ends at a precipitous and altogether impassable place. For no passage at all appears for the rest, except that, as it were, nature has there discovered some artificial gate, which from of old was called the Caspian.

[56709] 10.5. And from there on are plains fit for horsemen, full simply of much water, and a wide country, fit for pasturing horses and otherwise level.

[56856] 10.6. Here nearly all the Hunnic nations are settled, reaching as far as the Maeotic Lake.

[56948] 10.7. If these go through the gate which I just now mentioned into the country of the Persians and Romans, they come with fresh horses, using no roundabout way at all, nor meeting any precipitous places, except those fifty stades reaching to the borders of the Iberians, as has been said.

[57238] 10.8. But going by certain other passes they arrive with much toil and can no longer use the same horses. For they must necessarily make many circuits, and these precipitous.

[57414] 10.9. When Alexander, son of Philip, perceived this, he built gates at the place mentioned and established a guard-post. This many others in the course of time held, and among them Ambazouces, a Hun by race, but a friend of the Romans and of the Emperor Anastasius.

[57681] 10.10. This Ambazouces, when he had come to deep old age and was about to die, sent to Anastasius and asked that money be given him, on condition that he should hand over both the guard-post and the Caspian Gates to the Romans.

[57910] 10.11. But the Emperor Anastasius (for he neither knew how nor was wont to act without consideration), reckoning that it was impossible to support soldiers there in a place destitute of all good things and having no nation subject to the Romans in the neighbourhood, expressed much thanks to the man for his goodwill toward him, but by no means accepted this proposal.

[58280] 10.12. Ambazouces, then, not long after died of disease, and Cabades, forcing his sons, took the gates.

[58385] 10.13. And the Emperor Anastasius, when the truce with Cabades had been made, built a city in a place called Daras, exceedingly strong and worthy of account, named after the emperor himself.

[58577] 10.14. This is distant from the city of Nisibis ninety-eight stades, and from the boundary which divides the lands of the Romans and the Persians about twenty-eight.

[58744] 10.15. And the Persians, though eager to hinder the building, were by no means able, being pressed by their occupation with the Hunnic war.

[58885] 10.16. But as soon as Cabades had brought it to an end, he sent to the Romans and complained that they were building a city very near their own borders, this being forbidden by the things agreed of old between the Medes and the Romans.

[59122] 10.17. At that time, then, Anastasius, partly threatening, partly proffering his friendship and gifting him with no mean sums of money, wished to put him off and dissolve the charge.

[59306] 10.18. And this emperor built another city like it in Armenia, very near the borders of Persarmenia, which from of old happened to be a village, but having received the dignity of a city as far as the name from the Emperor Theodosius had become named after him.

[59569] 10.19. But Anastasius, surrounding it with a most strong wall, caused the Persians no less trouble than through the other; for both became siege-works against their country.

[59744] 11.1. When Anastasius died a little later, Justin received the kingship, all the kinsmen of Anastasius being driven from it, although they were many and very illustrious.

[59916] 11.2. Then a certain anxiety came upon Cabades, lest the Persians might attempt some revolution against his house as soon as he himself should end his life, since he was not going to be able to pass on the rule to any of his sons without dispute.

[60164] 11.3. For of his sons the eldest, Kaoses, the law called to the kingship on account of his age, but by no means pleased Cabades. And the father's judgement strove against both nature and law.

[60357] 11.4. And Zames, who held the second place, the law excluded, since one of his eyes had been struck out. For one-eyed, or held by any other maiming, it is not lawful for the Persians to set up as king.

[60560] 11.5. But Chosroes, who had been born to him from the sister of Aspebedes, the father loved exceedingly; yet, seeing nearly all the Persians, so to speak, struck with wonder at the manliness of Zames (for he was valiant in war) and revering his other virtue, he feared lest, rising against Chosroes, they might work irreparable deeds upon his line and the kingship.

[60927] 11.6. It seemed to him, then, best to settle both the war and the causes of the war with the Romans, on condition that Chosroes should be made adopted son of the Emperor Justin; for thus alone, he thought, could he preserve security for the rule. Therefore he sent envoys and a letter about these things to Byzantium to the Emperor Justin.

[61268] 11.7. And the writing declared as follows: "That we have suffered unjust things at the hands of the Romans you yourself also know; but I have resolved to remit to you all the charges altogether, knowing this, that those men would most of all among men be victorious who, having justice on their side, then, being worsted of their own free will, are overcome by their friends.

[61645] 11.8. A favour, however, I ask of you in return for these things, which would be able to bind not us alone, but the whole subject population of each, both into kinship and into the goodwill that naturally springs from it, and to set us, surely, in full enjoyment of the goods of peace.

[61932] 11.9. I mean, that you make my Chosroes, who shall be my successor in the kingship, your adopted son."

[62036] 11.10. When the Emperor Justin saw this brought to him, he himself was overjoyed, and so too was Justinian, the emperor's nephew, who was reckoned likely to succeed to the kingship.

[62219] 11.11. And in haste they hastened to set the adoption in writing, in the way that is the law for the Romans, had not Proclus prevented it, who then sat in counsel beside the emperor, holding the office of the so-called quaestor, a man just and conspicuously most incorruptible in money.

[62507] 11.12. And so he neither readily drew up any law nor wished to disturb any of the established things, who then too, in opposition, spoke as follows:

[62657] 11.13. "To attempt innovations is not my custom, and besides I fear them most of all, knowing well that in innovation safety is by no means preserved.

[62809] 11.14. And it seems to me that, even if one were exceedingly bold about such things, he would shrink from this affair and dread the surge that comes of it.

[62966] 11.15. For I think there is nothing else at present under our deliberation than how we may hand over the affairs of the Romans to the Persians under a specious pretext; men who, not concealing it nor using any veils, but openly avowing their design, thus shamelessly claim to take from us the kingship—putting forward as the manifest part of their deceit a candour, but with a shameless word holding out a wish to avoid trouble.

[63396] 11.16. And yet each of you ought to repel this attempt of the barbarians with all your might: you, O Emperor, that you may not be the last emperor of the Romans; and you, O General, that you may not become an obstacle to yourself in the way of the kingship.

[63655] 11.17. For all other sophisms of speech, cloaked for the most part in dignity, might perhaps need an interpreter for the many; but this embassy plainly, at once from its very preamble, wishes to make this Chosroes, whoever he is, heir to the Roman Emperor.

[63913] 11.18. For reason thus with yourselves about these things: by nature the goods of fathers are owed to children, and the laws, which in all men ever conflict with one another in everything, here, among both the Romans and all barbarians, come together and agree with one another in declaring that children are the masters of the father's estate. So that, having chosen the first step, it will remain for you to concede all the rest."

[64347] 11.19. So much Proclus said. And the emperor and the emperor's nephew received his words and took counsel about what should be done.

[64481] 11.20. In the meanwhile Cabades sent another letter to the Emperor Justin, asking him to send him men of repute, on condition that they should make the peace with him, and to indicate by letter the manner in which he wished the adoption of his son to be settled.

[64745] 11.21. And then Proclus, still more than before, disparaged the Persians' attempt, and insisted that they were concerned how they might most securely appropriate the Roman power to themselves.

[64939] 11.22. And he expressed the opinion that the peace should at once be concluded with them, but that the foremost men should be sent by the emperor for it, who, when Cabades inquired in what way the adoption of Chosroes ought to be made, should answer plainly that it must be as befits a barbarian, signifying that the barbarians make their children adopted not by writings, but by the panoply of arms.

[65341] 11.23. So, then, the Emperor Justin dismissed the envoys, promising that the best of the Romans should follow them before long, who should arrange both the matters concerning the peace and Chosroes in the best way.

[65557] 11.24. And he replied to Cabades by letter to the same effect. There were sent, then, from the Romans Hypatius, nephew of Anastasius who had been emperor before, a patrician and holding the office of General of the East, and Rufinus the son of Silvanus, a man of repute among the patricians and known to Cabades from their fathers' time;

[65896] 11.25. and from the Persians a most powerful man, enjoying great authority, by name Seoses, and of the dignity of adrastadaran salanes, and Mebodes,

[66046] 11.26. holding the office of magister. These, coming together to a certain place which divides the land of the Romans and the Persians, and conferring with one another, set about both to dissolve their differences and to arrange well the matters concerning the peace.

[66315] 11.27. And Chosroes came to the Tigris river, which is distant from the city of Nisibis about two days' journey, in order that, whenever the matters concerning the peace should seem to both sides to be in the best state, he might himself set out for Byzantium.

[66577] 11.28. Many other arguments, then, were spoken by both parties about their differences, and Seoses said that the land of Colchis, which is now called Lazica, having been from of old subject to the Persians, the Romans held by no right, having seized it by force.

[66841] 11.29. The Romans, hearing this, thought it monstrous, that Lazica too should be disputed by the Persians. And when they also declared that the adoption of Chosroes ought to take place as befits a barbarian, it seemed to the Persians not to be endured.

[67095] 11.30. Both sides, then, parted and withdrew homeward, and Chosroes departed to his father unsuccessful, exceedingly grieved at what had befallen and praying to take vengeance on the Romans for the insult done to him.

[67314] 11.31. After this Mebodes slandered Seoses to Cabades, alleging that on purpose, when it had not been enjoined on him by his master, he had put forward the argument about Lazica, frustrating the peace, having first consulted with Hypatius, who, being by no means well-disposed toward his own emperor, would not allow the peace and the adoption of Chosroes to be brought to effect; and bringing many other accusations besides, the enemies of Seoses called him to trial.

[67784] 11.32. The whole council of the Persians, then, sat in judgement, gathered together rather by envy than by law. For they were not unreasonably vexed at his office, unaccustomed to themselves, and were hard upon the man's character.

[68017] 11.33. For Seoses was most incorruptible in money and a most exact guardian of justice, but held by the disease of arrogance in no way like the rest of mankind. For this seems to be inborn in the rulers of the Persians; but in Seoses they themselves also thought that the malady had come to its very height.

[68326] 11.34. And the accusers said both these things which I have already shown, and that the man was by no means willing to live in the established manner or to keep the customs of the Persians.

[68517] 11.35. For he both worshipped strange divinities and had lately buried his wife when she died, it being forbidden by the laws of the Persians ever to hide in the earth the bodies of the dead.

[68710] 11.36. The judges, then, condemned the man to death, and Cabades, though he seemed to grieve with Seoses as with a friend, by no means wished to deliver him.

[68869] 11.37. Yet he did not declare that he held him in anger, but he did not wish to break the laws of the Persians by his word, although he owed the man his life, since Seoses had been the foremost cause of his living and being king. Thus Seoses, being condemned, was made to vanish from among men.

[69165] 11.38. And the office, beginning with him, ended with him. For no other became adrastadaran salanes. And Rufinus too slandered Hypatius to the emperor.

[69318] 11.39. Therefore the emperor deprived him of his office and, having most cruelly tortured some of his intimates, found nothing sound at all in this slander; yet he did Hypatius no other harm.

[69511] 12.1. At once Cabades, although eager to make an invasion into the land of the Romans, was by no means able, since the following hindrance befell him.

[69663] 12.2. The Iberians, who dwell in Asia, are settled close to the Caspian Gates, which are to them toward the north wind. And to their left, lying toward the setting of the sun, is Lazica; on the right, toward the rising sun, are the Persian nations.

[69913] 12.3. This people are Christians and guard the rites of this faith more than all men whom we know, but they have been from of old subject to the King of the Persians.

[70081] 12.4. At that time Cabades wished to force them to the rites of his own faith. And he charged their king Gourgenes both to do the other things as the Persians hold custom, and least of all to hide the dead in the earth, but to cast them all to birds and dogs.

[70342] 12.5. Therefore Gourgenes wished to go over to the Emperor Justin and asked to receive pledges that the Romans would never abandon the Iberians to the Persians.

[70504] 12.6. And he both gave him these with much eagerness, and sent Probus, nephew of the Anastasius who had been emperor, a patrician, with much money to Bosporus, in order that, having persuaded an army of Huns with money, he might send them to the Iberians for alliance.

[70774] 12.7. Now Bosporus is a city on the sea, on the left as one sails into the so-called Euxine Sea, distant from the city of Cherson, which is the farthest of the land of the Romans, twenty days' journey. And all the country between these the Huns hold.

[71026] 12.8. The Bosporites of old dwelt independent, but had lately resolved to go over to the Emperor Justin.

[71132] 12.9. But when Probus returned from there unsuccessful, the emperor sent the general Peter with some Huns to Lazica to fight as ally to Gourgenes with all his power.

[71299] 12.10. Meanwhile Cabades sent a considerable army against Gourgenes and the Iberians, and as general a Persian man, of the dignity of varizes, Boes by name.

[71457] 12.11. And Gourgenes, appearing too weak to bear the onset of the Persians, since the help from the Romans was not sufficient for him, fled with all the notables of the Iberians to Lazica, taking with him his wife and children and his brothers, of whom Peranius was the eldest.

[71736] 12.12. And coming to the borders of Lazica they remained, and fencing themselves with the difficult ground, they withstood the enemy.

[71871] 12.13. But the Persians, following them, accomplished nothing worthy of mention, the matter standing against them because of the difficult ground.

[72019] 12.14. After this both the Iberians came to Byzantium and Peter came summoned to the emperor; and for the rest the emperor asked the Lazi, who were unwilling, to keep guard over the country together with him, sending both an army and Irenaeus as commander.

[72277] 12.15. Now there are in Lazica two fortresses straightway as one enters from the borders of Iberia, of which the guarding was from of old a care to the natives, though held by them with much hardship, since neither grain nor wine nor any other good thing is produced there.

[72552] 12.16. Nor indeed is it possible to bring anything in from elsewhere because of the narrowness,

[72649] 12.17. except by men carrying it. The Lazi, however, by means of certain millets that grow there, which had become customary to them, were able to live.

[72803] 12.18. The emperor, removing these guards from there, ordered Roman soldiers to be set over the guarding of the fortresses.

[72928] 12.19. To these at first the Lazi with difficulty brought provisions, but later both they themselves declined the service and the Romans abandoned these fortresses, and the Persians with no trouble took them. These things took place in Lazica.

[73173] 12.20. But the Romans, with Sittas and Belisarius leading them, invading Persarmenia, which is subject to the Persians, both plundered much country and, having enslaved a very great multitude of Armenians, withdrew.

[73390] 12.21. Now these two men were young and just bearded for the first time, and bodyguards of the general Justinian, who at a later time held the kingship together with Justin his uncle. And another invasion of the Romans into Armenia having taken place, Narses and Aratius unexpectedly opposed them and came to blows.

[73707] 12.22. These not long after came as deserters to the Romans and made an expedition with Belisarius into Italy; but at that time, joining battle with those under Sittas and Belisarius, they got the better.

[73913] 12.23. Another Roman army also invaded near the city of Nisibis, of which Libelarius from Thrace was commander. These fled and made their withdrawal straight back, although no one was pursuing them.

[74113] 12.24. Therefore the emperor deprived Libelarius of his office, and set up Belisarius as commander of the troops at Daras. Then it was that Procopius, who wrote this history, was chosen as his counsellor.

[74480] 13.2. This Justinian bade Belisarius build a fortress in a place called Mindous, which is at the very borders of the Persians,

[74608] 13.3. on the left as one goes to Nisibis. He, then, with great eagerness carried out what seemed good to the emperor, and the stronghold was already being raised to a height by the multitude of workmen.

[74812] 13.4. But the Persians forbade them to build anything further, not by words alone, but threatening that they would not long after be hinderers in deed also.

[74970] 13.5. When the emperor heard this (for Belisarius was not able to repel the Persians from there with his present army), he ordered another army to go to that place, and Coutzes and Bouzes, who then commanded the soldiers in Lebanon. These were brothers from Thrace, both young and coming to blows with the enemy not with safety.

[75300] 13.6. Both sides, then, having gathered, came in a body against the building, the Persians to hinder it with all their might, the Romans to defend those who were constructing.

[75477] 13.7. A fierce battle taking place, the Romans were defeated, and a great slaughter of them occurred, and some the enemy took alive.

[75611] 13.8. Among these was Coutzes. These the Persians, leading all to their native homes, bound and kept shut up the whole time in a cave, and pulled down the building of the fortress to the ground, no one any longer defending it.

[75839] 13.9. Later the Emperor Justinian, having appointed Belisarius General of the East, ordered him to make an expedition against the Persians. And he, gathering a considerable army, came to Daras.

[76034] 13.10. And Hermogenes came to him from the emperor to help arrange the army, holding the dignity of magister, who had sat in counsel beside Vitalian before, when he was an enemy to the Emperor Anastasius.

[76240] 13.11. And the emperor sent Rufinus as an envoy, whom he ordered to remain in Hierapolis, which is by the Euphrates river, until he himself should give the signal. For already many parleys were taking place on both sides about peace.

[76475] 13.12. But suddenly someone reported to Belisarius and Hermogenes that the Persians were reckoned likely to invade the land of the Romans, being eager to take the city of Daras.

[76654] 13.13. And they, hearing this, prepared the matters concerning the battle-line as follows. At the gate which lies opposite the city of Nisibis, not far off, but about a stone's throw, they dug a certain deep trench, having many cross-passages. Not in a straight line, however, was this trench dug, but in the following manner.

[76982] 13.14. Along the middle a short straight stretch had been made, and on either side of it two straight arms were made, and at the ends of the two straight arms they again led out the trenches in straight lines for a great length.

[77212] 13.15. The Persians, then, not long after came with a great army, and in a place called Ammodios, distant from the city of Daras a measure of twenty stades,

[77370] 13.16. they all encamped. There were several commanders, and among them Pityaxes and Baresmanas the one-eyed. And one general was set over all, a Persian man, of the dignity of mirranes (for so the Persians call this office), Perozes by name.

[77614] 13.17. He at once sent to Belisarius and bade him have the bath prepared; for he wished to bathe there on the morrow.

[77733] 13.18. Therefore the Romans most vigorously prepared the matters concerning the engagement, intending to fight on the following day.

[77867] 13.19. And at sunrise, seeing the enemy advancing against them, they drew up as follows. The far end of the left straight stretch, which was below the straight arm, as far as the hill that rises there, Bouzes held with many horsemen, and Pharas the Herul with three hundred of his own kind;

[78159] 13.20. and on their right, outside the trench, at the angle which the straight arm and the straight line from there made, were Sunicas and Aïgan, Massagetae by race, with six hundred horsemen, so that, if those under Bouzes and Pharas should be put to flight, they themselves, going quickly aslant and getting behind the enemy's rear, might easily defend the Romans there. On the other side they were drawn up in the same manner;

[78590] 13.21. for the far end of the straight line many horsemen held, of whom John the son of Nicetas was commander, and Cyril and Marcellus; and with them were Germanus and Dorotheus; and at the angle on the right six hundred horsemen were drawn up, of whom Simmas and Ascan the Massagetae were commanders, in order that, as has been said, if those under John should be put to flight, should it so chance, they themselves rising up from there might come upon the rear of the Persians.

[79071] 13.22. Everywhere along the trench both the squadrons of the horsemen and the foot army stood. Behind these those under Belisarius and Hermogenes stood at the centre.

[79239] 13.23. Thus the Romans, coming together to twenty-five thousand, drew up; but the army of the Persians was four myriads of horse and foot, and they stood all in succession in line, intending to make the front of the phalanx very deep.

[79475] 13.24. For a long time, then, neither side began the battle against the other, but the Persians seemed to marvel at the good order of the Romans and to be at a loss how they should deal with the present situation.

[79690] 13.25. And toward late afternoon of the day a certain portion of the horsemen who held the right wing, breaking off from the rest of the army, came against those under Bouzes and Pharas.

[79878] 13.26. And they withdrew a little to the rear. But the Persians did not pursue them, but remained where they were, fearing, I suppose, some encircling movement by the enemy. After this the Romans who had fled suddenly rushed against them.

[80118] 13.27. And they, not withstanding the onset, rode back to the phalanx, and again those under Bouzes and Pharas drew up in their own place.

[80258] 13.28. In this affair seven of the Persians fell, of whose bodies the Romans took possession, and for the rest both sides remained quietly in their order.

[80414] 13.29. But one Persian man, a young man, driving his horse very near the Roman army, challenged all, if anyone wished to come to blows with him.

[80560] 13.30. And of the rest no one dared to undertake the danger, but there was a certain Andreas among the household of Bouzes, no soldier nor one who had ever practised anything pertaining to war, but a trainer of youths and set over a certain wrestling-school in Byzantium.

[80833] 13.31. Therefore he also followed the army, attending to the body of Bouzes in the bath, and was by race a Byzantine. This man alone took courage—neither Bouzes nor any other whomsoever having given the order—of his own accord to go out to single combat against the man. And anticipating the barbarian while he was still looking about where to charge, he smote him by the right breast with his spear.

[81235] 13.32. And he, not enduring the blow of an exceedingly strong man, falls from his horse to the ground. And Andreas with a certain short knife slew him, lying on his back like a sacrificial victim, and an extraordinary shout was raised both from the wall and from the Roman camp.

[81515] 13.33. The Persians, exceedingly grieved at what had happened, sent forth another horseman for the same business, brave and well-grown in stature, but no young man—rather grizzled in some of the hair on his head.

[81729] 13.34. He, coming up to the army of the enemy, and continually shaking the whip with which he was wont to strike his horse, called the Romans, whoever wished, to battle.

[81900] 13.35. And as no one came out against him, Andreas again, unobserved by all, came into the middle, although it had been forbidden him by Hermogenes.

[82050] 13.36. Both, then, with their spears, holding to one another with much passion, rushed together, and the spears, driven against the corselets, were terribly turned aside, and the horses, dashing their heads together, fell themselves and threw off their riders.

[82312] 13.37. And these two men, having fallen very near one another, hastened both with great eagerness to rise; but the Persian could not do this easily, his size being against him, while Andreas, getting ahead (for this his exercise in the wrestling-school taught him), struck him with his knee as he was rising, and again, when he had fallen to the ground, killed him.

[82679] 13.38. And a shout from the wall and from the Roman army was raised no less, if not more; and the Persians, breaking up the phalanx, withdrew to Ammodios, while the Romans, raising the paean, got within the wall.

[82893] 13.39. For already it was growing dark. And thus both sides passed that night in their bivouac.

[82990] 14.1. On the next day there came to the Persians ten thousand soldiers summoned from the city of Nisibis, and Belisarius and Hermogenes wrote to the mirranes as follows: "That peace is the first good has been agreed by all men who have even a

[83234] 14.2. little share of reason. So that whoever should become a destroyer of it would be the foremost cause of evils, not to his neighbours only, but also to his own kindred. That general, then, is best who is able to dispose peace out of war.

[83477] 14.3. But you, when affairs are well established for the Romans and the Persians, have resolved to bring upon us, who give no cause, a war, though each emperor is deliberating peaceful measures, and envoys of ours are already at hand in the neighbourhood, who will not long after dissolve the differences by their converse with one another, unless something irreparable, befalling from your onset, should be able to check this hope for us.

[83918] 14.4. But lead off your army as quickly as possible to the native land of the Persians, and stand not in the way of the greatest goods, lest, as is likely, you become to the Persians the cause of the dread things that will come to pass."

[84528] 14.6. We, then, having refused your deceit, have been forced to come against you in arms; and you, O dear Romans, henceforth think nothing else than that you must make war on the Persians. [87491] 14.18. Such, then, are the affairs of the enemy. But let the judgement of the King of Kings come upon you, O Persian men.

[87614] 14.19. For if in the present case you do not play the man worthily of the valour of the Persians, a punishment not glorious will surround you."

[87944] 14.21. "That the Persians are not altogether invincible, nor mightier than you know how to die, you surely know, judging from the former battle; but that, surpassing them in courage and bodily strength, you are worsted in this alone, in being more disobedient to your commanders,

[88225] 14.22. no one would deny. This you can correct with no trouble. For the adversities of fortune cannot by zeal at all be corrected, but resolve might easily become for a man a healer of his own evils;

[89056] 14.26. Therefore weapons with which they might trouble their adversaries they by no means have, but they hold before them shields of such size only that they themselves may be least struck by the enemy.

[91640] 14.37. But when all the arrows of both sides had now failed, they used their spears against each other, and the battle became still more at close quarters. And the left wing of the Romans was especially distressed.

[91856] 14.38. For the Cadiseni, who fought there with Pityaxes, having come up in great numbers, suddenly routed the enemy and, pressing hard on them as they fled, killed many.

[92027] 14.39. When those under Sunicas and Aïgan saw this, they went against them at a great run. And first the three hundred Heruls under Pharas, coming from the height upon the rear of the enemy, performed marvellous deeds both against the rest and against the Cadiseni.

[92294] 14.40. And these, when they saw those under Sunicas already coming up aslant against them, rushed to flight.

[92404] 14.41. The rout becoming brilliant, when the Romans there joined with one another, a great slaughter of the barbarians took place.

[92536] 14.42. And of them on the right wing not fewer than three thousand died in this toil, while the rest, hardly fleeing to the phalanx, were saved.

[92682] 14.43. And the Romans no longer pursued, but both sides stood in their line facing one another. So these things were carried on here.

[92817] 14.44. But the mirranes secretly sent many others and all the so-called Immortals to the left part. When Belisarius and Hermogenes perceived these, they bade the six hundred under Sunicas and Aïgan go to the angle on the right, where those under Simmas and Ascan stood, and behind them they set many of those who followed Belisarius.

[93152] 14.45. The Persians, then, who held the left wing, Baresmanas leading them with the Immortals, charged at a run against the Romans opposite them. And these, not withstanding the onset, rushed to flight.

[93356] 14.46. Then the Romans in the angle and as many as were behind them went with great eagerness against the pursuers.

[93473] 14.47. And going aslant the barbarians, they divided their army in two, and held the most of them on the right, but some, left behind, they made to be on the left. Among these it happened that the bearer of the standard of Baresmanas was, whom Sunicas, coming up, smites with his spear.

[93761] 14.48. And now the Persians in the foremost ranks of the pursuers, perceiving the evil they were in, turned their backs and, stopping the pursuit, went against them, and from this they became caught between the enemy.

[93980] 14.49. For perceiving what was being done, those who had fled turned back again. And the other Persians and the company of the Immortals, seeing the standard inclined and let down to the ground, rushed with Baresmanas against the Romans there.

[94225] 14.50. And the Romans met them. And Sunicas first killed Baresmanas and threw him from his horse to the ground. And from this the barbarians, falling into great fear, no longer remembered defence, but, held by much disorder, fled.

[94457] 14.51. And the Romans, making a certain encircling movement, killed about five thousand of them. So both armies were altogether set in motion, the Persians to retreat, the Romans to pursuit.

[94649] 14.52. In this toil all the foot in the Persian army, throwing away their shields and being overtaken, were killed in no order by the enemy. The pursuit of the Romans, however, was for a short space.

[94850] 14.53. For Belisarius and Hermogenes by no means allowed them to go further, fearing lest by some necessity the Persians, turning about, might rout them as they pursued without any order, and it seemed to them sufficient to preserve the victory unbroken.

[95106] 14.54. For after a long time the Persians were that day defeated in that battle by the Romans. Thus the two sides parted from one another.

[95246] 14.55. And the Persians no longer wished to carry on an open battle against the Romans. There were, however, on both sides certain raiding inroads, in which the Romans had no less the advantage. So the armies in Mesopotamia fared thus.

[95483] 15.1. Cabades sent another army against Armenia, which is subject to the Romans. This army was of Persarmenians and Sunitae, who are neighbours to the Alani. And three thousand Huns, called Sabiri, were with them, a most warlike nation.

[95721] 15.2. As general, Mermeroes, a Persian man, was set over all. And these, when they were three days' journey distant from Theodosiopolis, encamped and remained in the country of the Persarmenians and prepared the matters concerning the invasion.

[95967] 15.3. The general of Armenia happened to be Dorotheus, a man intelligent and experienced in many wars. And Sittas held the office of General in Byzantium, but was set over the whole army in Armenia.

[96167] 15.4. These, learning that an army of the enemy was gathering in Persarmenia, at once sent two bodyguards to spy out and report to them the whole force of the enemy.

[96334] 15.5. Both came into the camp of the barbarians and, having observed everything exactly, were departing.

[96440] 15.6. And as they went along the road into one of the places there, they fell in unexpectedly with hostile Huns. By these one of them, Dagaris by name, was bound and taken captive, but the other was able to flee and reported the whole matter to the generals.

[96700] 15.7. And they, arming the whole army, suddenly came upon the camp of the enemy.

[96782] 15.8. And the barbarians, struck with consternation at the unexpectedness, no longer looked to defence, but fled each as best he could. There the Romans, killing many and plundering the camp, at once rode back.

[96994] 15.9. And Mermeroes, gathering his whole army, not long after invaded the land of the Romans, and they came upon the enemy near the city of Satala. There, encamping at a place called Octave, they remained quiet, which is distant from the city fifty-six stades.

[97256] 15.10. Sittas, then, taking with him a thousand men, hid behind one of the hills, of which many encircle the city of Satala, lying in a plain.

[97400] 15.11. And Dorotheus with the rest of the army he bade remain within the wall, since they thought they could by no means withstand the enemy in the open, being not fewer than thirty thousand, while they themselves scarcely came to half.

[97638] 15.12. On the following day the barbarians, coming very near the wall, were eager to make a certain encircling of it. But suddenly perceiving those under Sittas already coming down from the height against them, and being by no means able to measure their number, since much dust lay heavy in the summer season, they thought them far more numerous, and abandoning the encircling at once, hastened to draw themselves together into some small space.

[98086] 15.13. But the Romans, anticipating them and dividing themselves into two bands, fell upon them as they withdrew from the wall. When the whole Roman army saw this, they took courage and at a great run, streaming out from the wall, went against the adversaries.

[98348] 15.14. And setting them in the middle, they turned them to flight. By their multitude, however, as has been said, the barbarians, surpassing the enemy, still held out, and the battle became fierce and at close quarters.

[98569] 15.15. And each side made quick-turning pursuits against the other, since all were horsemen. There Florentius the Thracian, commander of a cavalry squadron, charging into the middle of the enemy and seizing their general's standard and inclining it as much as possible, rode back.

[98851] 15.16. And he himself, being overtaken and cut to pieces, fell there, but he became the foremost cause of the victory for the Romans. For when the barbarians no longer saw the standard, falling into much disorder and dread, they withdrew and, coming into their camp, remained quiet,

[99135] 15.17. having lost many in the battle. And on the next day all withdrew homeward, no one pursuing them, since it seemed a great thing and worthy of much account to the Roman army that barbarians so great in number should in their own country have suffered what I have just related, and, having broken into hostile country, should depart unsuccessful and thus worsted by the fewer.

[99517] 15.18. Then too the Romans got Persian places in Persarmenia, both the fortress of Bolon and the so-called Pharangium, from which the Persians, digging out gold, bring it to the king.

[99702] 15.19. And it chanced that a little before they had subdued the Tzanic nation, who from of old dwelt independent in the land of the Romans; how this was done shall at once be told.

[99884] 15.20. To one going from the regions of Armenia into Persarmenia, on the right is the Taurus, reaching to Iberia and the nations there, as I have said a little before; on the left the road becomes ever more downward for a great distance, and mountains hang exceedingly precipitous, covered with clouds and snows through all time,

[100215] 15.21. whence, issuing forth, the river Phasis is borne to the land of Colchis. Here from the beginning barbarians, the Tzanic nation, dwelt subject to no one, called Sani in former times, using robbery against the Romans round about them, and having an exceedingly hard way of life, and ever living on their plunder; for the land bore them nothing good for food.

[100580] 15.22. Therefore the Emperor of the Romans sent them a fixed sum of gold every year, on condition that they should never plunder the country there.

[100729] 15.23. But they, swearing the oaths customary among them about these things, and holding what they had sworn of no account, falling unexpectedly upon them, for the most part did mischief not only to the Armenians, but also to the Romans adjoining them as far as the sea, and in a short time, having made their inroad, at once carried themselves off home.

[101085] 15.24. And if they happened to fall in with a Roman army, they were defeated in battle, but were by no means to be captured, by the strength of their places. Sittas, then, having conquered them in battle before this war, and having said and done many things to win them over,

[101362] 15.25. was able to win them over entirely. For having changed their way of life to the milder, they enrolled themselves in Roman registers, and for the rest go out against the enemy with the rest of the Roman army. And they changed their faith to the more pious, all having become Christians. So the affairs concerning the Tzani fared thus.

[101704] 15.26. To one who has crossed their borders there is a glen deep and exceedingly precipitous, reaching to the Caucasus mountains. There are very populous places, and the vine and other fruit grow abundantly.

[101913] 15.27. And for about a three days' journey this glen is tributary to the Romans, but from there on the borders of the Persarmenians take over, where also is the mine of gold, which, Cabades having given it, one of the natives, Symeon by name, administered.

[102171] 15.28. This Symeon, when he saw both sides at the height of war, resolved to deprive Cabades of the revenue of the money.

[102294] 15.29. Therefore, giving over both himself and the Pharangium to the Romans, he thought fit to bring the gold from the mine to neither side.

[102436] 15.30. For the Romans did nothing, thinking it enough for themselves that the revenue from there had been lost to the enemy; and the Persians were unable, against the will of the Romans, to constrain those dwelling there, the difficult ground standing against them.

[102703] 15.31. About the same time Narses and Aratius, who at the beginning of this war came to blows with Belisarius and Sittas in the country of the Persarmenians, as has been shown before, came as deserters with their mother to the Romans, and Narses the emperor's treasurer received them (for he too happened to be by race a Persarmenian) and gifted them with great sums of money.

[103081] 15.32. When Isaaces, the youngest of their brothers, learned this, coming secretly to a parley with the Romans, he handed over to them the fortress of Bolon, which is very near the borders of Theodosiopolis.

[103290] 15.33. For he charged soldiers to hide nearby, whom he received into the fortress by night, secretly opening to them one little gate; and thus he himself also came to Byzantium.

[103469] 16.1. Thus stood the affairs of the Romans. But the Persians, defeated in battle by Belisarius at Daras, did not even so resolve to withdraw from there, until Rufinus, when he had come into the presence of Cabades, spoke as follows: "Your brother, O King, has sent me, making a just complaint, that the Persians from no cause came in arms into his land.

[103824] 16.2. And yet it would befit a great king, and one come to so great wisdom, rather to dispense peace out of war than, when affairs are well established, to inflict, against need, a disturbance upon himself and his neighbours.

[104051] 16.3. With these things I too, being of good hope, have come here, that for the future the goods of peace may be to both." So much Rufinus said.

[104197] 16.4. And Cabades replies as follows: "O son of Silvanus, by no means try to reverse the charges, knowing best of all that you Romans have been the foremost cause of all the disturbance. For the Caspian Gates we held for the good of both the Persians and the Romans, having constrained the barbarians there, since Anastasius, the Emperor of the Romans, as you yourself also know, when it was possible to buy them with money, was unwilling, lest, having an army there for all time, he should be forced to expend great sums on behalf of both.

[104739] 16.5. And from that time we, having stationed there an army so great in number, and supporting it down to the present, have granted to you, at least as concerns the barbarians there, to inhabit your country unravaged, holding your own affairs with much freedom from trouble.

[105015] 16.6. But as though this were not enough for you, you have also made a great city, Daras, as a siege-work against the Persians, although it was expressly forbidden in the treaty which Anatolius made with the Persians; and from this it is necessary for the affairs of the Persians to be afflicted with toils and expense for two armies, the one that the Massagetae may not be able to ravage and carry off the land of both with impunity, the other that we may check your raids.

[105491] 16.7. About which, when we lately complained and demanded that one of two things be done by you—either that the army to the Caspian Gates be sent by both, or that the city of Daras be dismantled—you would not accept the understanding of what was said, but resolved to strengthen the plot against the Persians with a greater evil, if we remember at all the building at Mindous. And now too for the Romans the goods of peace are to be chosen, or arms are to be chosen—either by setting just things toward us, or by going against them.

[106025] 16.8. For the Persians will not lay down their arms before the Romans either justly and rightly guard the gates together with them, or dismantle the city of Daras."

[106191] 16.9. So much having said, Cabades dismissed the envoy, intimating that he was willing both to receive money from the Romans and to put an end to the causes of the war.

[106361] 16.10. These things Rufinus, coming to Byzantium, reported to the emperor. Not long after, Hermogenes also arrived, and the winter ended, and the fourth year ended of the Emperor Justinian holding the supreme rule.

[106577] 17.1. As spring was beginning, an army of the Persians, with Azarethes leading them, invaded the land of the Romans. They were fifteen thousand, all horsemen. And with them was Alamoundaras, son of Saccice, having a very great throng of Saracens.

[106825] 17.2. This invasion of the Persians did not take place as was usual; for they did not invade Mesopotamia as before, but the region called of old Commagene, now Euphratesia, whence the Persians had never before, so far as we know, made an expedition against the Romans.

[107095] 17.3. And for what reason the country was called Mesopotamia, and why the Persians refrained from the invasion into it, I now go on to relate.

[107239] 17.4. There is in Armenia a mountain not very precipitous, distant forty-two stades from Theodosiopolis, turned toward the north wind of it. And two springs issue from there, making at once two rivers, the Euphrates on the right, and the other, Tigris by name.

[107501] 17.5. Of these one, the Tigris, using no circuits, and few waters joining it,

[107580] 17.6. goes straight down to the city of Amida. And passing it toward the north wind it goes into the land of the Assyrians. But the Euphrates is borne at first to some small space, and going straight on disappears—not, however, becoming underground, but something wonderful befalls it; for above the water a swamp arises, deep for a great extent,

[107928] 17.7. in length about fifty stades, in breadth twenty; and a great quantity of reeds grows in this mud.

[108033] 17.8. And so hard is the soil there that to those who come upon it nothing seems other than that it is dry land. So it happens that foot and horse pass over it fearing nothing.

[108211] 17.9. And indeed many wagons too go across from there each day, but they have no power at all to move anything or to bring the swamp to light.

[108355] 17.10. And the natives burn the reeds every year, that the roads may not be barred by them, and once, a violent wind falling there, it happened that the fire reached to the very ends of the roots, and the water appeared in a small space;

[108594] 17.11. but after no long time the soil, growing together again, gave back to the place the shape it had before. From there the river goes on to the country called Celesene, where was the temple of Artemis among the Taurians, whence they say Iphigenia daughter of Agamemnon fled with Orestes and Pylades, bearing the image of Artemis.

[108929] 17.12. For the other temple, which exists even to my time in the city Comana, is not the one among the Taurians. But how it came to be I shall show.

[109079] 17.13. When Orestes departed from the Taurians with his sister, it befell him to fall sick in body. And as he inquired about the disease, they say the oracle gave answer that the evil would not abate for him until he should build a temple to Artemis in such a place as the one among the Taurians chanced to be, and there cut off his own hair and call the city by the same name as her.

[109465] 17.14. Therefore Orestes, going about the places there, came into Pontus, and saw a mountain which hung precipitous there, and below, along the edges of the mountain, flowed the river Iris.

[109656] 17.15. Orestes, then, supposing that the oracle indicated this place to him, built there a city worthy of account and the temple of Artemis, and cutting off his hair he called the city by the same name as her, which is named Comana even to my time.

[109906] 17.16. But when these things had been wrought by Orestes, the disease was at its height no less, if not more. And the man, perceiving that he was not doing these things suitably to the oracle, again going about everything, examined and found a place among the Cappadocians most resembling the one among the Taurians.

[110224] 17.17. Which I too, having often seen, marvelled at exceedingly, and thought I was among the Taurians. For this mountain is simply like that one, since here too is the Taurus, and the river Saros is likened to the Euphrates there.

[110456] 17.18. There, then, Orestes built a city worth seeing and two temples, the one to Artemis, the other to his sister Iphigenia, which the Christians have made into churches for themselves, changing the building not at all.

[110678] 17.19. This is called even now the golden Comana, being named after the hair (kome) of Orestes, which they say he cut off there and escaped the malady.
