CHAPTER: 16-idyll-16



[0] 16. The Graces, or Hiero This is ever the care of the daughters of Zeus, and ever of singers:

[95] to hymn the immortals, to hymn the glorious deeds of good men.

[159] The Muses are goddesses, and goddesses sing of gods;

[213] but we are mortals here, and let mortals sing of mortals.

[272] For who of all who dwell beneath the gray dawn

[320] will open his door and gladly receive my Graces

[369] when they spread their wings, and not send them off again unrewarded?

[440] But they come back home in anger, on bare feet,

[489] reviling me much because they went a fruitless journey,

[546] and reluctant once more, in the bottom of an empty chest,

[605] they remain, their heads cast down upon their cold knees,

[664] where their seat is always, when they return unsuccessful.

[724] Who of men now is of such a kind? Who will love the one who speaks well of him?

[805] I do not know; for men no longer, as before, are eager

[861] to be praised for noble deeds, but are mastered by gain.

[919] Each, with his hands under his cloak, looks about for where he may get

[991] silver, and would not rub the rust off a coin to give to anyone,

[1057] but at once declares: The shin is nearer than the knee;

[1114] let me have something for myself; the gods honor singers.

[1173] And who would listen to another? Homer is enough for all.

[1232] He is the best of singers, who shall take nothing from me.

[1292] Foolish men, what gain is the countless gold

[1338] lying within? This is not what the wise count the use of wealth,

[1404] but to give some to one's own soul, and some to a singer,

[1463] and to do good service to many kinsmen, and to many other

[1522] men besides, and ever to offer altar-sacrifice to the gods,

[1583] and not to be a bad host, but having welcomed a guest at table

[1647] to send him off when he wishes to depart,

[1690] and above all to honor the holy interpreters of the Muses,

[1750] so that even when hidden in Hades you may be heard of as noble,

[1815] and not lament without glory by cold Acheron,

[1862] like some man with hands callused inside by the mattock,

[1920] bewailing the bare poverty he has inherited from his fathers.

[1983] Many in the halls of Antiochus and of lord Aleuas

[2034] received their monthly measure of food, the bondsmen;

[2089] and many calves, driven to the pens of the Scopadae,

[2143] lowed along with the horned cattle,

[2180] and over the Crannonian plain countless

[2221] choice sheep the shepherds folded for the hospitable Creondae;

[2285] but no joy of these was theirs, when they had poured out their sweet

[2355] life into the wide raft of hateful Acheron,

[2400] and, forgotten, leaving behind all those many blessings,

[2458] among the wretched dead they lay through long ages,

[2511] had not that singer of Ceos, with his varied voice,

[2564] set them, named, on the many-stringed lyre among men

[2618] of later days; and even the swift horses won honor,

[2671] that came back garlanded for them from the holy games.

[2727] And who would ever have known the chiefs of the Lycians, who the long-haired

[2805] sons of Priam, or Cycnus pale as a woman in his skin,

[2860] had not singers hymned the battles of men of old?

[2911] Nor would Odysseus, who wandered a hundred and twenty months

[2973] among all peoples, and went living to the farthest Hades,

[3032] and escaped the cave of the deadly Cyclops,

[3077] have had long-lasting fame; and silent would Eumaeus

[3131] the swineherd have been, and Philoetius, busy

[3178] with the herded cattle, and great-hearted Laertes himself,

[3238] had not the songs of the Ionian man profited them.

[3290] From the Muses good fame comes to men,

[3330] but the living waste the wealth of the dead.

[3376] Yet equal is the labor of measuring the waves upon the shore,

[3439] as many as the wind drives landward from the gray sea,

[3495] or of washing clean a brick of mud with clear water,

[3549] and of trying to win over a man corrupted by love of gain.

[3609] Farewell to such a man; let him have countless

[3657] silver, and let longing for more ever possess him.

[3709] But I would choose honor and the friendship of men

[3761] before many mules and horses.

[3792] I seek to whom of mortals I may come welcome

[3838] with the Muses; for hard are the roads for singers

[3890] without the daughters of Zeus, who counsels greatly.

[3944] Not yet has the sky grown weary of leading round the months and the years;

[4020] many horses shall yet turn the chariot-wheel;

[4067] there shall be a man who will have need of me as his singer,

[4129] having done as great deeds as great Achilles or heavy Ajax

[4189] in the plain of Simois, where lies the tomb of Phrygian Ilus.

[4252] Already now the Phoenicians who dwell beneath the setting sun

[4315] on the farthest verge of Libya are shuddering with dread.

[4374] Already the Syracusans grip their spears by the middle,

[4431] their arms weighed down with shields of willow;

[4480] and among them Hiero, equal to the heroes of old,

[4531] girds himself, and horsehair plumes shade his helmet.

[4586] Would, O most glorious Zeus, father, and lady Athena,

[4641] and the Maiden, who with your mother have for your portion the great city

[4716] of the rich Ephyraeans by the waters of Lysimeleia,

[4769] that an evil necessity might send our enemies out of the island

[4834] over the Sardinian wave, bearing word of the death of dear ones

[4899] to children and to wives, few to be counted out of many;

[4957] and that the cities might again be dwelt in by their former citizens,

[5028] all that the enemies' hands ravaged to the ground;

[5080] and that men might work the flourishing fields; and that the countless

[5152] thousands of sheep, fattened on the pasture,

[5198] might bleat over the plain, and that the cattle, coming in herds

[5264] to the steading, might hasten the homing wayfarer at dusk;

[5324] and that the fallow lands might be worked toward the sowing, when the cicada,

[5403] watching the shepherds at noon, sheltered within the trees,

[5464] sings in the boughs; and that the spiders might spread their fine

[5531] webs over the armor, and that of the war-cry there might not be even a name.

[5609] And may singers carry the high fame of Hiero

[5655] both beyond the Scythian sea and to where the broad wall

[5713] that Semiramis bound with bitumen reigned over the land.

[5771] I am but one, and the daughters of Zeus love many

[5822] others too; and may they all care to hymn Sicilian Arethusa

[5883] with her peoples, and the spearman Hiero.

[5926] O goddesses, daughters of Eteocles, you who love

[5976] Minyan Orchomenus, once hateful to Thebes,

[6020] unbidden indeed would I stay at home, but to those who call

[6081] I would come boldly, with my Muses.

[6118] Nor will I leave you behind; for what is dear to men

[6172] apart from the Graces? May I be ever with the Graces.
