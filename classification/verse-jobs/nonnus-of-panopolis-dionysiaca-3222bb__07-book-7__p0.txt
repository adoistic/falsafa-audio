CHAPTER: 07-book-7



[0] 7. The seventh sings the white-haired suppliant prayer of Aion, and Semele, and the love of Zeus, and the stolen marriage-bed.

[128] Now, with the renewing fruit of everlasting life,

[179] mingling the male, the seed of begetting, in the female furrow,

[244] Eros plowed the unsown world, the husbandman of love;

[299] and Nature took root, the nurse of all that is born,

[353] and, blending fire with earth and water entwined with air,

[413] she shaped the human race in a four-yoked bond.

[462] But a sorrow of changing kinds held the life of mortals,

[520] beginning in toil and never ceasing from care.

[568] And to Zeus who rules over all, Aion his companion

[620] showed the race of suffering men, with no portion of gladness;

[684] for not yet, loosing the swaddling threads of childbirth,

[743] had the father shot forth Bacchus from his pregnant thigh,

[803] the rest from human care; not yet did the libation,

[856] steeped in wine, make drunk the airy pathways with fragrant smoke;

[924] and the daughters of the year, the joyless Seasons,

[977] wove for the gods their garlands out of meadow-grass.

[1032] For there was need of wine; and the grace of a dance without Bacchus

[1102] was half-complete and useless; it charmed only

[1150] the eyes of the gathered crowd, when in his whirling sweeps

[1211] the dancer, turning many a circle, spun in a storm of feet,

[1272] his gestures speech, his hand a mouth, his fingers a voice.

[1333] But Aion of changing shapes, spreading on the knees of Zeus

[1394] his white hair, holding the key of all that is born,

[1448] stretched out the slackened tress of his beard in supplication,

[1513] and made his prayer; with his head bowed down to the floor

[1573] the curving neck pressed his back, drawn out at length;

[1630] and stretching out an endless hand from his bending knee,

[1689] the old shepherd of everlasting life spoke out:

[1738] Lord Zeus, look you also upon the griefs of a downcast world.

[1801] Do you not see how War has goaded all the earth,

[1851] reaping in season the corn of swiftly-perishing youth?

[1907] Not yet have those relics passed away, since you washed

[1964] all the nations of men with the flood, and the stream of the rain from the sky,

[2045] surging through the air, splashed up against neighbouring Selene.

[2112] Farewell to the life of short-lived mortals, over whose doom

[2174] I refuse the heavenly tiller; no longer will I steer

[2228] the cable of the world; let some other, better, of the blessed

[2292] receive the rudder of life that comes round again;

[2344] let another keep the running course of my years; for in pity

[2406] of their bitter affliction I have toiled over the much-enduring race of men.

[2484] Old age is no remedy, which withers youth

[2527] and makes a man slow with his head bowed down,

[2575] when, bent, with trembling, over-many-footed steps,

[2628] heavy-kneed he leans upon the familiar staff that tends old age;

[2694] death is no remedy, that often has hidden in Oblivion

[2749] the bridegroom newly come from the dance, the partner of his bride of equal age,

[2831] loosing the life-giving cables of a union that cannot be loosed.

[2897] I know indeed that marriage is full of charm, where there sounds

[2963] the flute of Athena, blending its voice with the pipe of Pan;

[3026] yet what profit is it, when beside the wedded chamber

[3081] the upright echo of the seven-stringed lyre rings out?

[3137] The harps do not loose our cares; nay, Eros himself,

[3191] dance-less at the wedding, has shaken off the bridal torch,

[3252] looking on the marriage-songs of those who lack delight.

[3310] But the life-saving remedy that brings forgetfulness of grief

[3373] took no root among much-toiling mortals; for that heavenly

[3433] lid of the jar Pandora should never have opened

[3482] for men, that sweet evil. Nay, even Prometheus himself

[3538] is to blame for the wickedness of mankind,

[3582] who cares for wretched mortals; for in place of fire,

[3637] the beginning of woe, the sweet nectar that gladdens the mind of the blessed,

[3716] he ought rather to have stolen, and granted as a gift to men,

[3779] so as to scatter with your draught the cares of the world.

[3839] But leaving the cares of clamorous life,

[3881] look upon your own downcast rites; does the wandering,

[3937] empty vapour of unceasing sacrifices charm you?

[3986] So spoke the old man; and for a while, in thoughtful silence,

[4049] Zeus the counsellor stirred his own boundless wisdom;

[4104] and he loosed the reins of his mind; with crowding counsels

[4165] the purposes of his teeming brain were set whirling.

[4219] And the son of Cronos raised to Aion a god-proclaiming voice,

[4282] declaring the higher oracles of the prophetic axle:

[4335] O father, self-sown shepherd of the everlasting years,

[4391] be not wroth; for mortal nature, untimely, never ceases

[4448] waxing and waning, the imitation of the moon.

[4495] Leave nectar to the blessed; and to men, a defence against grief,

[4562] I will give sweet wine, self-poured, the likeness of nectar;

[4624] another drink, fitted for mortals; but the primal world

[4681] still grieves, until I bring one child to birth.

[4731] I myself, the father, give birth, and in my male thigh I will endure

[4801] a woman's pangs, that I may save the offspring from its pangs.

[4865] Yesterday, at the nod of my Deo of the wide threshing-floors,

[4928] the earth, scored by the iron that woos the corn-ears,

[4984] bore the dry fruit of the sheaf-bearing ploughland.

[5037] Now my glorious-gifted child shall fix in the ground

[5091] the moist, fragrant fruit of pain-soothing autumn,

[5143] Dionysus the griefless, rearing the grief-dispelling cluster,

[5206] rival of Demeter; and you will praise me when you behold

[5264] the vine reddening with its wine-bearing dews,

[5312] the herald of gladness, and the country-folk beside the wine-press

[5380] treading the harvest with their heavy-laden feet,

[5431] and the phalanx of the Bassarids, lovers of the cry of Euoi,

[5493] shaking their loosened, frenzied hair into the air above their shoulders;

[5568] and, made frantic in mind by cup after cup,

[5613] all will raise the shout of Euoi at the loud-resounding table,

[5677] hailing Dionysus, the defender of the human race.

[5728] Him, when he has fought his contest, after the earth—companion of the stars,

[5806] after the war with the Giants, after the battle with the Indians—

[5873] the shifting heaven will receive, flashing his lightning beside Zeus.

[5944] And the god of the garden-vines, twining about his locks

[6002] the creeping serpent set upon the wine-dark ivy as a crown . . .

[6068] shall wear the serpent-headband, the sign of his new godhead;

[6131] and equal in honour with the blessed, named among men

[6186] shall be Dionysus of the vine, like Hermes of the golden wand,

[6250] like brazen Ares, like far-shooting Apollo.

[6295] So spoke the father; and the Fates consented; and around his word

[6362] the swift-footed Seasons, the heralds of things to come, sneezed an omen.

[6437] And so, when they had spoken, the two parted, the one coming

[6499] to the house of Harmonia, the other to the embroidered hall of Hera.

[6569] And Eros, self-taught and wise, who shepherds eternity,

[6626] striking on the dark gates of first-born Chaos,

[6675] brought out a god-driven quiver, in which alone

[6724] were kept, for Zeus, the fire-nourished arrows

[6772] that should turn him, fickle, to the desire of earthly marriages—

[6839] twelve of them; and a golden verse, measured out for each,

[6899] he had written on the middle back of the love-shooting quiver:

[6963] The first leads the son of Cronos to the bed of cow-eyed Io;

[7025] the second woos Europa with the ravishing bull;

[7074] the third leads the lord of Olympus to wed Plouto;

[7126] the fourth calls him to Danae as a golden bridegroom;

[7181] the fifth makes ready the fiery marriage for Semele;

[7235] the sixth grants the eagle to Aegina, the chief of the air;

[7296] the seventh joins Antiope to the crafty Satyr;

[7344] the eighth brings the wise swan to naked-bodied Leda;

[7399] the ninth bears the horse-bed to Dia of Perrhaebia;

[7452] the tenth charms Alcmene's three-moon husband;

[7500] the eleventh brings about the wedding of Laodameia;

[7553] the twelfth draws the thrice-coiling consort of Olympias.

[7612] But when Eros had looked over them all, handling them in order,

[7677] he let the other fire-pointed arrows lie,

[7720] and with his hand raised up the fifth and fitted it to the blazing string,

[7796] casting ivy upon the tip of the winged arrow,

[7843] that it might be a fitting crown for the vine-god,

[7895] dipping the whole shaft in the moisture of the nectar-bowl,

[7956] that Bacchus might make the nectared vintage grow.

[8008] While Eros was lifted to the house of Zeus on beating wings,

[8070] meanwhile Semele also, keeping pace with the rosy dawn,

[8127] was driving her mules, stretching the sound of her silver whip

[8191] through the city, and the slender, upright track of the well-wheeled car

[8265] scored the surface of the dust;

[8298] for, plucking from her eyes the Lethean feather of Sleep,

[8357] a wandering dream escorted her bewildered heart,

[8407] embroidering oracles, and with fresh-grown clusters

[8460] she thought she saw within the garden a tree of fair leaves,

[8522] green, weighed down with the swelling fruit of the unripe grape,

[8588] drenched with the growth-giving dews of the son of Cronos;

[8648] and suddenly a heavenly flame, falling through the air,

[8705] laid the whole tree low, yet did not touch its fruit;

[8760] but a wandering, long-winged bird snatched it up,

[8811] half-formed and lacking the birth that brings it to fulfilment,

[8876] and held it out to the son of Cronos; and the father in his sweet bosom

[8949] received and took it, and stitched it into his thigh; and in place of the fruit,

[9031] a bull-natured man, shaped with a horned form,

[9079] sprang up self-perfected above the loins of his sire.

[9134] And Semele was the tree; and the maiden, shuddering,

[9188] leapt up from her bed and startled her father,

[9236] telling of the gleaming vapour rising from the fair leaves.

[9297] And Cadmus the king was shaken, hearing that the tree of Semele

[9362] was burnt by fire; and calling his son, the god-voiced offspring of Chariclo,

[9441] early in the morning he told the smouldering dreams of his child.

[9508] And then, having received the inspired voice of Teiresias,

[9568] the father sent his daughter to the wonted temple of Athena

[9629] to make sacrifice to Zeus, the hurler of the thunderbolt—

[9688] a bull, the very image of the horned form of Lyaeus,

[9742] and a goat, the cutter of vines, the foe of the vintage to come.

[9808] From there she went out before the city, to kindle an altar to Zeus,

[9878] lord of the lightning; and standing beside the sacrifice

[9936] she steeped her bosom in blood, and the maiden was sprinkled with the slaughter;

[10018] and unsparing streams of blood wetted her tresses,

[10070] and her garments were dyed purple with the runlets from the ox;

[10135] and guiding her course beside the deep-rushy grass

[10187] she went to the ancestral water of neighbouring Asopus,

[10244] the maiden of the gleaming robe, to wash in the streams

[10301] her spotted garments, soaked with the many-spattering gore.

[10362] And looking on Semele bathing beside the streams of Asopus,

[10423] the Fury that roams in the air laughed,

[10464] remembering the son of Cronos, since with a common doom

[10521] he was to strike them both with the flaming thunderbolt.

[10579] There she cleansed her body, and naked, with her handmaids,

[10640] oaring her hands through the water, the maiden ran;

[10693] and with skilful art she held her head undrenched,

[10745] raised high above the swell, soaked to her hair,

[10795] and, laying her breast upon the stream,

[10836] with alternate feet she pushed the water behind her.

[10890] And she took up another offering, and high upon the neighbouring bank,

[10962] beside the eastern edge, to Dionysus the averter of ill,

[11020] into the stream, into the winds she shook off the fear of her dreams.

[11091] Not without the gods did she go to the streams; rather the Seasons,

[11160] prophetic, led her to the outpourings of that river;

[11214] nor did she escape the all-seeing eye of Zeus; and around the maiden

[11284] he turned, high-shining, the endless circle of his gaze.

[11342] And drawing in the air his bow, the helper of life,

[11395] Eros stood facing the father who was watching,

[11443] the unerring archer; and on the flower-tressed arrow

[11497] the bowstring flashed, and as the back-bent bow

[11546] was drawn, the echo of Euoi whistled forth the cunning shaft.

[11609] And Zeus the father, mighty as he was, was the target; to a worthless

[11680] Eros he bowed his neck; and like the track of a star

[11734] the bolt of the Loves, stirred with the wedding-whistle,

[11792] came to the heart of Zeus, swerving with thoughtful throb,

[11852] scoring with its topmost notches the fold of his thigh,

[11909] the herald of the birth to come. Then the son of Cronos,

[11967] bearing a restless eye, by the channelling constraint of earth,

[12032] was scourged toward the love of the maiden by the girdle of desire;

[12101] and gazing on Semele he leapt up, lest near the bank

[12155] he should see Europa again; and in his heart

[12201] he laboured once more, bearing a Phoenician longing; for of that same

[12272] beauty she bore the imprint, and ever about her face

[12326] shone the kindred radiance of his father's sister.

[12378] And Zeus the father changed his crafty form,

[12424] and for love of Semele, untimely, he flew as an eagle

[12479] high above Asopus, the daughter-begetting river,

[12529] bearing, like the prophet of Aegina's well-winged marriage,

[12590] the keen-eyed semblance of a bird's gaze;

[12633] and he left the upper air, and close beside the nearby bank

[12694] he measured the naked body of the fair-tressed maiden;

[12750] for he did not wish to see her from afar, but to gaze

[12805] close at hand on the all-white whole body of the bride,

[12862] because, sending everywhere his boundless eye,

[12910] so great and of such kind, the surveyor of the whole world,

[12971] it seemed to him not enough to see one unwedded maiden.

[13028] And the dark water was reddened by her rosy limbs,

[13080] and the river's stream became a lovely meadow,

[13128] flashing with the Graces; and gazing through the maiden

[13185] the naiad, unveiled, gave up a voice of wonder:

[13234] Surely it was not after the elder Cypris that Cronos, snatching away

[13304] their wedlock, cut the loins of his father, until again the wise foam,

[13376] bringing to a self-accomplished birth the shaping water,

[13434] brought to labour the younger sea-born Aphrodite?

[13485] Surely it is not that the river, after the sea, with like jealous births,

[13560] whirling the birth-bearing wave of the self-born swell,

[13617] bore another Cypris, and did not yield to the sea?

[13669] Surely it is not that one of the Muses entered my ancestral water

[13736] from neighbouring Helicon, and left for some spring

[13789] the horse-spring of Pegasus, the honey-dropping water,

[13845] or the stream of Olmeius? Stretched out in the streams

[13901] I behold a silver-footed maiden within the river;

[13952] I am persuaded that, wishing to come to the bed of Latmos,

[14012] to the couch of Endymion, the sleepless herdsman,

[14063] Selene is bathing in the Aonian outpourings;

[14109] but if she has cleansed her body for the grace of her sweet herdsman,

[14180] what need has she of Asopus, after the stream of Ocean?

[14237] And if she keeps her snowy, heavenly form,

[14281] what sign of the moon has she? For the yoke-straps of the unbridled

[14350] mules and the silver-wheeled car

[14384] are beside the shore, and Selene, the driver of oxen,

[14439] knows not how to yoke mules to the harness.

[14484] But if some heavenly goddess has come—for of the maiden

[14541] I see the grey, calm flashing of her eyes—

[14585] then perhaps, after the old quarrel of Teiresias,

[14636] grey-eyed Athena has bathed again, casting off her garment.

[14697] The rosy-armed maiden has a godlike form;

[14740] but if an earthly womb, glory-laden, bore her,

[14788] she is worthy of the heavenly bed of the son of Cronos.

[14845] Such words the voice from beneath the water spoke among the waves;

[14913] but Zeus, stirred by the fire-pointed goad of desire,

[14968] gazed on the rosy fingers of the swimming maiden;

[15019] and he turned the wandering circle of his unsteady eye,

[15076] now gazing on the spark of her rosy face,

[15119] now on the gleam of her ox-eyed lids, now on her hair

[15174] wandering in the winds, and, as her tresses were drawn aside,

[15237] he beheld the free, uncovered neck of the maiden;

[15288] and yet more he gazed upon her breasts, and, naked before the son of Cronos,

[15366] her bosom was armed, the archers of the Loves;

[15414] and he gazed on all her body; only the unseen, sacred

[15469] mystery of her lap his reverent eyes passed by.

[15518] And the mind of heavenly Zeus, creeping as a wanderer,

[15574] swam together with swimming Semele; and, charmed,

[15625] receiving the sweet-maddening spark in his accustomed heart,

[15687] the father yielded to the child; and with his feeblest dart

[15748] little Eros set the hurler of the thunderbolt aflame;

[15803] neither the showering of snow, nor against the fiery bearer

[15864] did the lightning avail; and even she herself was vanquished,

[15927] that so great a heavenly flame, by the little fire

[15979] of unwarlike Paphia; and little Eros with his shaggy quiver

[16040] vied with the aegis, the girdle with the shield, and to the love-breeding quiver

[16122] the heavy-thundering crash of the thunder was made a slave.

[16183] And Semele was stirred by the heart-charming sting of desire,

[16246] holding amazement; for love is the neighbour of friendly wonder.

[16312] And hardly did wily, high-ruling Zeus come back to the pole of heaven,

[16384] wearing again the inspired, recovered shape of his form.

[16442] And wishing to mount the nightly bed of Semele

[16490] he stretched his eye toward the west, for when sweet Hesperus should come;

[16566] and he blamed the long evening hour of Phaethon,

[16616] and with friendly lips he uttered a love-sick voice:

[16670] Tell me, lingering Night, when does grudging Dawn go down?

[16730] But you, lift up the torch, the forerunner of the Loves of Zeus,

[16796] prophesying the night-wandering lamp of Lyaeus.

[16845] Jealous Phaethon constrains me; can it be that he too

[16900] desires Semele, and grudges me in my longing?

[16947] O Sun, you trouble me, even if you have learned the goad of the Loves;

[17019] with sparing lash why do you whip your slow horse?

[17071] I know also another, swifter setting; if I wish,

[17121] I will hide both you and the dawn-goddess in my clouds,

[17178] and, with you hidden, day-time Night shall appear

[17229] as the bride-bringer of eager Zeus, so that there may shine

[17290] the stars at midday, and so that I may make the wonted escort of the Loves,

[17367] Hesperus, rise and never set.

[17398] But, drawing your forerunner the Dawn-star to the west,

[17455] grant a favour to you and to me in my longing, and all night long

[17522] have the joy of your Clymene, and let me come swiftly to Semele.

[17588] Yoke your chariot for me, light-bearing Selene, you also,

[17647] sending forth your plant-fostering radiance, since this marriage

[17713] ordains the birth of plant-rearing Dionysus;

[17759] and, rising over the lovely halls of Semele,

[17805] shine for me in my longing, together with the star of the Cyprian,

[17873] and lengthen the sweet darkness that ministers to the bed of Zeus.

[17941] Such words the father spoke, the things that desire knows how to bid.

[18012] But when, as he hastened, the earth-born leap stretching upward,

[18078] the topmost wide cone of mist ran up,

[18117] bringing the moist gloom of setting, the shadow opposite to Dawn,

[18184] then airy Zeus passed by the starry house

[18227] to the marriage of Semele, and with untraceable sandal,

[18284] leaping his first bound, measured with his sole the whole

[18343] airy path; and with the second he reached Thebes

[18393] like a wing or a thought; and as he rushed within,

[18445] the bars of the gateway opened of themselves.

[18492] And he embraced Semele with the loving clasp of his hand,

[18551] now over the bed lowing with the bellow of an ox,

[18602] bearing a horned aspect on his human limbs,

[18647] the very likeness, equal in growth, of bull-horned Dionysus,

[18709] now he put on the thick-haired form of a lion,

[18757] at another time he was a leopard, as begetting a bold son,

[18817] the driver of leopards and the charioteer of lions;

[18870] at another time the bridegroom, his hair coiled

[18919] under the spires of serpents, was bound with a vine-wreath,

[18980] whirling the wine-dark, twisting ivy of his locks,

[19032] the woven ornament of Bacchus; and a curving serpent, creeping,

[19097] licked the rosy neck of the trembling bride

[19142] with gentle lips, and, going down over her bosom,

[19193] it wreathed the rounded circle of her steadfast breasts,

[19251] hissing the marriage-song, and pouring out the sweet honey

[19311] of the well-hived bee, not the deadly venom of the viper.

[19370] And Zeus tarried at the marriage, and as beside a neighbouring wine-press

[19445] he thundered the cry of Euoi, begetting the son who loves the cry of Euoi;

[19521] and he set mouth upon mouth in love-madness, and, gushing forth

[19586] the lovely nectar, the consort made Semele drunk,

[19637] that she might bear a child, the sceptred lord of the vintage,

[19701] raising the care-banishing cluster, the messenger of things to come,

[19771] leaning his weighted arm upon the fire-bearing fennel-rod;

[19831] at another time he raised the thyrsus, much-entwined with wine-dark ivy,

[19905] bearing the skin of a deer; and on the woman-maddened bearer's

[19969] left arm shook the dappled fawnskin.

[20007] And all the earth laughed, and with self-grown leaves

[20062] a vine-bearing orchard ran round the bed of Semele,

[20115] and the walls budded with flowers from the dewy meadow

[20171] about the birth of Bromios; and on the cloudless bed,

[20226] with thundering crashes, Zeus within the chamber clashed

[20284] the drums, prophesying nocturnal Dionysus.

[20328] And after the bed he embraced Semele with loving speech,

[20386] consoling his bride with hopes of things to come:

[20437] I am, woman, the son of Cronos, your bridegroom; lift up

[20495] your proud neck on high, joined to a heavenly consort,

[20551] and do not seek a measure greater than mortal birth.

[20605] The marriage of Danae does not rival yours; nay, the wedlock

[20667] of the cattle-Loves even of his own father's sister

[20720] you have hidden; for Europa, glorying in the bed of Zeus,

[20779] went to Crete, but Semele comes to Olympus.

[20824] What more did you wish, beyond the heaven and the pole of stars?

[20890] And one day someone will say that the son of Cronos gave honour

[20955] to Minos below and to Dionysus in heaven.

[20998] But after the mortal son of Autonoe and the offspring of Ino—

[21061] the one fallen by his own hounds, the other doomed

[21113] to die at the winged arrow of his child-slaying sire,

[21168] and after the short-lived son of raving Agave—

[21216] you shall bring to birth an undying son, and I will call you immortal;

[21288] blessed are you, that for gods and men you shall bring forth a joy,

[21357] bearing a son who makes mortals forget their grief.
