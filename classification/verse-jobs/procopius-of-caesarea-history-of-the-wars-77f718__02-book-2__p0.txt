[0] 1.1. Not long afterward Chosroes, learning that Belisarius had begun to win Italy too for the Emperor Justinian, was no longer able to restrain his mind, but wished to devise pretexts by which he might break the treaty under some specious word.

[246] 1.2. Consulting about these things with Alamoundaras, he bade him provide causes of war.

[336] 1.3. And he, charging Arethas that he was using violence against him over the boundaries of the land, came to blows with him in time of peace, and on this pretext began to overrun the land of the Romans.

[541] 1.4. And he declared that he himself was not breaking the treaty of the Persians and Romans, since neither party had enrolled him in it.

[679] 1.5. And this was so. For no account had ever been made of the Saracens in the treaties, since they are comprehended under the name of the Persians and the Romans.

[844] 1.6. This country, which then was disputed by the Saracens of both sides, is called Strata, and is turned toward the south wind of the city of Palmyra, bearing neither tree nor any of the good things of the cornfields (for it is exceedingly burnt by the sun), but set apart from of old for the pasturing of certain flocks.

[1168] 1.7. Arethas, then, insisted that the place was the Romans', inferring it from the name, which it chanced to have from of old among all (for Strata means the paved road in the Latin tongue), and using the testimonies of most ancient men.

[1407] 1.8. But Alamoundaras by no means thought fit to contend over the name, but said that the owners of the flocks had from of old paid him rent for the pasture there.

[1572] 1.9. Therefore the Emperor Justinian entrusted the arbitration of the disputed points to Strategius, a patrician and commander of the royal treasuries, and otherwise intelligent and well-born, and besides to Summus, who had been commander of the soldiers in Palestine.

[1842] 1.10. This Summus was the brother of Julian, who a little before had gone on an embassy to the Ethiopians and the Homerites.

[1968] 1.11. And of these two, the one, Summus, thought fit that the Romans should not abandon the country, while Strategius begged the emperor not, for the sake of a country small and of no account at all, but barren and altogether fruitless, to grant to the Persians, who desired war, pretexts for the war. The Emperor Justinian, then, took counsel about these things, and much time was spent on this arbitration.

[2378] 1.12. But Chosroes, the King of the Persians, declared that the treaty had been broken by Justinian, who had lately displayed a great plot against his house, in that he had attempted to make Alamoundaras his ally in time of peace.

[2610] 1.13. For he said that Summus, lately coming to him ostensibly for the arbitration, had got round him by promises of great sums of money, on condition that he should go over to the Romans, and he produced letters which the Emperor Justinian had written to Alamoundaras about these things.

[2900] 1.14. And he insisted that he had also sent a letter to some of the Huns, urging them to invade the land of the Persians and ravage the places there as much as possible. This letter he said the Huns themselves had handed to him, coming into his presence.

[3156] 1.15. These were the charges Chosroes brought against the Romans, purposing to break the treaty. Whether, however, in saying these things he chanced to be telling the truth, I cannot say.

[3345] 2.1. Meanwhile Vittigis, the leader of the Goths, already worn down by the war, sent two envoys to him to persuade him to make an expedition against the Romans—not Goths, however, lest by being recognized at once they should confound what was being done, but Ligurian priests, led to this deed by ample sums of money.

[3664] 2.2. Of these the one who seemed the worthier was set in the embassy, clothed with the appearance and name of a bishop, though it in no way belonged to him; and the other followed serving him.

[3858] 2.3. And going by the road to the regions of Thrace, they made companion of a man from there who should be for them an interpreter of the Syrian and the Greek tongue, and escaping the notice of all the Romans they reached the country of the Persians. For, being established in time of peace, the Romans did not keep guard there exactly.

[4196] 2.4. And coming into the presence of Chosroes they spoke as follows: "All other envoys, O King, for the most part chance to be set in the embassy for their own advantage, but us Vittigis, King of the Goths and the Italians, has sent to make the case on behalf of your empire; and consider that he himself, present, now speaks these things to you.

[4544] 2.5. If anyone, O King, summing it up, should say that you have given over both your kingship and all men to Justinian, he would speak rightly.

[4689] 2.6. For he, being by nature a lover of innovation and enamoured of what in no way belongs to him, and unable to remain within the established order, has desired to seize all the earth, and has been eager to encompass every realm.

[4921] 2.7. Then (for he was not able to attack the Persians alone, nor, while the Persians opposed him, was he able to go against others) he resolved to deceive you with the veil of peace, and by forcing the rest to make great powers his allies against your empire.

[5182] 2.8. Having already destroyed the kingdom of the Vandals and subdued the Moors, the Goths standing out of his way under the name of friendship, he comes against us with great sums of money and many men.

[5386] 2.9. And it is clear that, if he should be able to destroy the Goths altogether, he will make an expedition against the Persians together with us and those already enslaved, neither having in mind the name of friendship nor blushing at any of his oaths.

[5641] 2.10. While, then, some hope of safety is still left you, do us no further harm, nor suffer it yourself, but look in our evils at what a little later will befall the Persians, and consider that the Romans would never be well-disposed toward your kingship, but, becoming superior in power, will not delay to display their hatred toward the Persians.

[5991] 2.11. Use, then, your power in season; do not seek it when it has ceased. For the height of opportunities, when relaxed, is by no means wont to return. And it is better, having anticipated, to be in safety, than, having come too late for the opportunities, to suffer the most shameful of all things at the hands of the enemy."

[6319] 2.12. When Chosroes heard this, it seemed to him that Vittigis advised him well, and he was still more eager to break the treaty. For, held by envy toward the Emperor Justinian, he by no means resolved to reckon that these words came to him from men most hostile to the Emperor Justinian.

[6609] 2.13. But by his wishing it, he came of his own accord to be persuaded. Which indeed he did a little later in the case of the arguments of the Armenians and the Lazi, as shall presently be told.

[6805] 2.14. And yet they brought such charges against Justinian as might fitly be praises for a noble king: that he was eager to make his own kingship greater and far more illustrious.

[6985] 2.15. For these things one might bring also against Cyrus the King of the Persians and Alexander the Macedonian. But justice is by no means wont to dwell with envy. For these reasons Chosroes purposed to break the treaty.

[7208] 3.1. Meanwhile another thing befell, of the following kind. That Symeon, who had given over the Pharangium to the Romans, persuaded the Emperor Justinian, while the war was still at its height, to gift him with certain villages of Armenian men.

[7454] 3.2. And having become master of the places, he was killed by a plot at the hands of those who had owned them of old.

[7573] 3.3. The evil being accomplished, the perpetrators of the murder fled to the country of the Persians. They were two brothers, sons of Perozes. And the emperor, hearing this, gives the villages to Amazaspes, the nephew of Symeon, and set him up as commander over the Armenians.

[7851] 3.4. This Amazaspes, as time went on, one of his intimates, Acacius by name, slandered to the Emperor Justinian, saying that he was doing mischief to the Armenians and wished to hand over to the Persians both Theodosiopolis and certain other towns.

[8101] 3.5. Having said this, Acacius, by the emperor's purpose, killed Amazaspes by treachery, and held the command of Armenia himself, the emperor having given it.

[8261] 3.6. Being wicked by nature, he had whereby he might display the dispositions of his soul. He became, then, the most savage of all men toward his subjects.

[8418] 3.7. For he plundered their money with no account, and appointed for them a payment of tribute, never before existing, of four centenaria. And the Armenians (for they were no longer able to bear him), taking counsel together, killed Acacius and fled to the Pharangium.

[8688] 3.8. Therefore the emperor sent Sittas against them from Byzantium. For Sittas was passing his time there, since the treaty with the Persians had been made for the Romans.

[8861] 3.9. And he, coming to the Armenians, at first went slowly into the war, but hastened to tame them and to set the men back into their former dispositions, promising to persuade the emperor to remit to them the new payment of tribute.

[9096] 3.10. But when the emperor reproached him much for his delay and reviled him, led by the slanders of Adolius the son of Acacius, then at last Sittas prepared the matters concerning the engagement.

[9294] 3.11. First, then, by promises of many good things he attempted to persuade and win over some of them, that the mastery over the rest might become for him easier and less toilsome.

[9476] 3.12. And the race of those called the Aspetiani, being great and populous,

[9553] 3.13. wished to go over to him. And sending to Sittas in writing, they begged him to give them pledges that, if, in the engagement, abandoning their kinsmen, they should come into the Roman battle-line, they would remain altogether unharmed, holding their own.

[9815] 3.14. And he, gladly writing in a little book the pledges as they asked, gave them, and having sealed the writing sent it to them.

[9947] 3.15. Taking courage that through them he would master the war without battle, he went with his whole army to a place called Oenochalakon, where it chanced that the Armenians were encamped.

[10138] 3.16. But by some chance those who had the book, going by another road, were by no means able to meet the Aspetiani.

[10256] 3.17. A portion, however, of the Roman army, meeting a few of them and not knowing the agreement, treated them as enemies.

[10380] 3.18. And Sittas himself, taking their children and women somewhere in a cave, killed them, either not understanding what had happened or holding the Aspetiani in anger because they did not come over to him as had been agreed.

[10608] 3.19. And they, now held by anger, drew themselves up for battle together with all the rest. But since both sides were in difficult and precipitous ground, they did not fight in one place, but scattered among the foothills and glens. It chanced, then, that a few of the Armenians and Sittas, having not many followers, came very near one another, a glen being between them.

[10983] 3.20. And both were horsemen. Sittas, then, with a few following him, crossing the glen against his adversaries, drove on, but the Armenians, withdrawing back, stood; and Sittas no longer pursued, but remained where he was.

[11208] 3.21. And suddenly one of the Roman army, a Herul by race, who had made a pursuit against the enemy and was riding back from there in anger, came to those under Sittas. And it chanced that Sittas had planted his spear in the ground; this the horse of the Herul, falling upon it with much force, broke.

[11511] 3.22. This grieved the general exceedingly, and one of the Armenians, seeing him, recognized him and insisted to all the rest that this was Sittas. For it happened that he had no helmet on his head. Therefore he did not escape the notice of the enemy, that he had come there with a few men.

[11803] 3.23. Sittas, then, when he heard the Armenian saying this, and his spear, as has been said, broken off lay on the ground, drawing his sword, at once attempted to cross the glen.

[11983] 3.24. But the enemy with much haste drove against him, and one, overtaking him in the glen, struck him on the top of the head with his sword, a crosswise blow. And he took off the whole crown of the head,

[12189] 3.25. but the iron in no way touched the bone. And Sittas drove on still more than before; but Artabanes, the son of John, an Arsacid, falling upon him from behind and striking him with his spear, killed him.

[12399] 3.26. And thus Sittas vanished from among men in no account, unworthily of his valour and of his deeds ever done against the enemy, a man exceedingly fair in body and valiant in war, and the best general of his time, second to none.

[12633] 3.27. But some say that Sittas perished not at the hands of Artabanes, but that Solomon, a very obscure man among the Armenians, made away with the man.

[12787] 3.28. On the death of Sittas the emperor bade Bouzes go against the Armenians; and he, when he came very near, sent to them, promising to reconcile all the Armenians to the emperor and asking some of the men of repute to come to a parley with him about these things.

[13055] 3.29. The rest, then, could neither trust Bouzes nor were willing to accept his words. But there was a man most friendly to him, an Arsacid, John by name, the father of Artabanes, who then, trusting Bouzes as a friend, came to him together with Bassaces his son-in-law and a few others; and these, when they had come to a place and bivouacked where they were to meet Bouzes on the morrow, perceived that they had come into an encircling by the Roman army.

[13512] 3.30. Bassaces, then, the son-in-law, much entreated John to take to flight. But when he could not persuade him, leaving him alone there, together with all the rest, escaping the notice of the Romans, he rode back again by the same road.

[13751] 3.31. And Bouzes, finding John alone, killed him; and from this the Armenians, having no longer any hope toward the Romans concerning an agreement, nor being able to overcome the emperor in war, came to the King of the Persians, Bassaces leading them, an energetic man.

[14022] 3.32. Of these the foremost, then coming into the presence of Chosroes, spoke as follows: "There are many of us Arsacids, O master, descendants of that Arsaces who chanced to be no stranger to the kingship of the Parthians, when the affairs of the Persians lay under the Parthians, and who became an illustrious king, second to none of his time.

[14369] 3.33. We are now present before you, all having become slaves and fugitives, not willingly, however, but constrained as much as possible, in appearance by the Roman power, but in true word by your

[14567] 3.34. judgement, O King; if indeed he who gives strength to those who wish to do wrong might justly bear also the blame of the deeds. And it shall be told a little further back, so that you may be able to follow all that has been done.

[14804] 3.35. For Arsaces, the last king of our ancestors, ceded his rule of his own free will to Theodosius the Emperor of the Romans, on condition that all those who by descent should belong to him for all time should both live otherwise in freedom and never be subject to tribute.

[15081] 3.36. And we preserved the agreement until you made these famous treaties, which, calling them a common ruin, one would not, we think, err.

[15222] 3.37. For thenceforth, taking no thought of friends and enemies, your friend in word, O King, but in deed your foe, confounded and threw into confusion all human affairs.

[15394] 3.38. Which you yourself also will not long after know, as soon as he is able to subdue the men of the West altogether. For what of the things forbidden before did he not do? Or what of the things well established did he not disturb?

[15629] 3.39. Did he not appoint for us a payment of tribute not before existing, and enslave the Tzani, who were our neighbours and independent, and set a Roman commander over the king of the wretched Lazi?—a thing agreeing neither with the nature of things nor easy to explain in word.

[15910] 3.40. Did he not send generals to the Bosporites, who were subject to the Huns, and appropriate the city, though it in no way belonged to him, and make alliance with the rulers of the Ethiopians, of whom the Romans had not even heard at all?

[16153] 3.41. But he has also encompassed the Homerites and the Red Sea, and adds the Palm-grove to the Roman realm.

[16263] 3.42. For we forbear to speak of the sufferings of the Libyans and the Italians. The whole earth does not contain the man; it is a small thing for him to rule all men together.

[16441] 3.43. And he scans the heavens too and searches out the recesses beyond the ocean, wishing to acquire for himself some other inhabited world.

[16584] 3.44. Why, then, O King, do you still delay? Why are you ashamed of a peace most wretchedly to perish, that he may make you the last morsel of all the rest?

[16742] 3.45. If you wish to learn what sort of man Justinian would be toward those who yield to him, you have the example near at hand, from ourselves and from the wretched Lazi;

[16915] 3.46. and if you wish to learn how he is wont to deal with those who are unknown and have done him no wrong at all, consider the Vandals and the Goths and the Moors.

[17082] 3.47. The chief point has not yet been told. Has he not made it his work in time of peace to get round Alamoundaras, your slave, O most mighty King, by deceit and to detach him from your kingship, and has he not been eager lately to win over the Huns, in no way known to him, against your affairs? And yet no deed more monstrous than this has happened in all time.

[17448] 3.48. For when he perceived, I think, that the subjugation of the West would all but reach its completion for him, he has now attempted to assail you of the East, since the power of the Persians alone is left him for a contest.

[17677] 3.49. The peace, then, on his part is already broken for you, and he himself has put an end to the endless treaty.

[17793] 3.50. For those break the peace not who first take up arms, but who are caught plotting against their neighbours in time of treaty.

[17926] 3.51. For the charge has been committed by him who attempts it, even if success be absent. But how the war will turn out is plain to everyone, I suppose. For not those who furnish the causes of war, but those who defend themselves against those who furnish them, are ever wont to master the enemy.

[18225] 3.52. Nor indeed will the contest be for us on equal terms of power. For it chances that the Romans have most of their soldiers at the extremities of the inhabited world, and of two generals, who were their best, we come having killed the one, Sittas, while Justinian will never again see Belisarius. For, neglecting him, he has remained in the West,

[18577] 3.53. himself holding the Italian dominion. So that, as you go against the enemy, none of all will meet you, and you will have us too, with goodwill, as is likely, and with much experience of the places, leading the way for your army." When Chosroes heard this,

[18840] 3.54. he was pleased, and calling together whatever was pure among the Persians, he brought out to all both what Vittigis wrote and all that the Armenians said, and set the matter of what should be done before them for counsel.

[19069] 3.55. There, then, many opinions were spoken tending to either side, but in the end it seemed best that they should make war against the Romans at the beginning of spring.

[19242] 3.56. For it was the autumn of the year, the thirteenth year of the Emperor Justinian holding the supreme rule.

[19355] 3.57. The Romans, however, did not suspect this, nor did they think the Persians would ever break the so-called endless treaty, although they had heard that Chosroes both accused their own emperor of his successes in the West and brought against him these charges which I have just mentioned.

[19649] 4.1. Then too the comet star appeared, at first about as large as a very tall man, but later much greater. And its end was toward the setting sun, its beginning toward the rising,

[19830] 4.2. and it followed behind the sun itself. For the sun was in Capricorn, and it was in Sagittarius. And some called it the swordfish, because it was very long and had a very sharp point, others the bearded star; and it appeared for more than forty days.

[20086] 4.3. The wise men in these matters, in no way agreeing with one another, foretold each different things to be signified by this star; but I, writing what came to pass, give to each to infer from the outcomes as he wishes.

[20309] 4.4. At once a great Hunnic army, crossing the Ister river, fell upon all Europe, a thing that had happened often before, but never bringing so many evils in number or so great in magnitude to the men there. For from the Ionian Gulf these barbarians plundered all in succession as far as the suburbs of Byzantium.

[20624] 4.5. And they took thirty-two fortresses in Illyricum, and subdued by force the city of Cassandria (which the ancients called Potidaea, so far as we know), not having stormed a wall before.

[20815] 4.6. And holding the spoils and leading off twelve myriads of captives, they all withdrew home, no opposition meeting them.

[20940] 4.7. And at a later time, often coming there, they did irreparable dreadful things to the Romans.

[21039] 4.8. These also stormed the wall in the Chersonese, and forcing those who defended themselves from the wall, and passing over the rampart by way of the surf of the sea—the rampart which is by the so-called Black Gulf—and thus getting within the long walls and falling unexpectedly upon the Romans in the Chersonese, they killed many and enslaved nearly all.

[21398] 4.9. And a few even crossing the strait between Sestos and Abydos, and plundering the places in Asia, and again turning back to the Chersonese, were conveyed home with the rest of the army and all the booty.

[21607] 4.10. And in another invasion, plundering the Illyrians and the Thessalians, they attempted to storm the wall at Thermopylae, but the guards in the walls defending themselves most vigorously, searching out the circuits they found, beyond expectation, the path that leads to the mountain which rises there.

[21914] 4.11. And thus, having destroyed nearly all the Greeks except the Peloponnesians, they withdrew.

[22012] 4.12. And the Persians not long after, breaking the treaty, wrought upon the Romans of the East deeds which I shall presently relate.

[22147] 4.13. Belisarius, having destroyed Vittigis the King of the Goths and the Italians, brought him alive to Byzantium. But how the Persian army invaded the land of the Romans I now go on to relate.

[22343] 4.14. When the Emperor Justinian perceived that Chosroes desired war, he wished to make some exhortation and to draw him from the attempt.

[22483] 4.15. There chanced to be at Byzantium one come from the city of Daras, Anastasius by name, having repute for intelligence, who had also put down the tyranny lately arisen at Daras.

[22666] 4.16. This Anastasius, then, Justinian sent to Chosroes, having written a letter;

[22749] 4.17. and the writing declared as follows: "It is the part of intelligent men, and of those who have sufficiently practised the things of God, to cut off with all their might the springing causes of war, especially toward men who are most friends; but of unintelligent men, and of those who most readily make the things of God hostile to themselves, to contrive occasions of battle and disturbance that in no way exist.

[23170] 4.18. For to dissolve peace and go to war is no great matter, since the nature of things has held that the basest of practices are easy even for the most dishonoured of men.

[23345] 4.19. But for men who have ordered war as they purposed to go again to peace is, I think, not easy.

[23446] 4.20. And yet you charge us with letters not written for the purpose, and have been eager to interpret these now by your own self-willed judgement, not as we wrote them when we devised them, but as it seems to you, desiring to accomplish your designs not without some veil, will profit.

[23734] 4.21. But it is in our power to show that your Alamoundaras, lately overrunning our land, wrought in time of peace irreparable deeds—captures of places, plundering of money, murders and enslavements of men so great in number—for which it will be needful for you not to accuse us, but to defend yourself.

[24039] 4.22. For the deeds, not the intentions, declare to neighbours the charges of those who have done wrong. But though these things are so, we have resolved even so to hold to the peace, while we hear that you, desiring war against the Romans, are fashioning charges that in no way belong to us.

[24333] 4.23. Reasonably; for those who are eager to set right the present shake off even the charges against their friends, pressing as they are, while those whom the law of friendship does not please are eager to provide even pretexts that do not exist.

[24582] 4.24. But these things would seem to befit not even ordinary men, much less kings.

[24666] 4.25. But you, leaving these, consider the measure of those who will perish on both sides in the war, and who would justly bear the blame of what shall befall, and reckon the oaths, which, having sworn and received the money, then dishonouring against need, you could not get round by any arts or sophisms; for the divine is by nature mightier than to be deceived by

[25034] 4.26. all men." When Chosroes saw this brought to him, for the moment he neither answered anything nor dismissed Anastasius, but compelled him to remain there.

[25195] 5.1. But when the winter was now abating, and the thirteenth year was ending of the Emperor Justinian holding the supreme rule, Chosroes the son of Cabades invaded the land of the Romans with a great army at the beginning of spring, and brilliantly broke the so-called endless peace. And he went, not through the country between the rivers, but keeping the Euphrates on the right.

[25577] 5.2. On the other side of the river is the last Roman fortress, which is called Circesium, exceedingly strong, since the Aborrhas, a great river, having its outlet there, mingles with the Euphrates, and this fortress lies at the very angle which the meeting of the two rivers makes.

[25861] 5.3. And another long wall outside the fortress, taking in the land between each river, completes there about Circesium a triangular shape.

[26002] 5.4. Therefore Chosroes, neither wishing to make trial of so strong a fortress nor purposing to cross the Euphrates river, but to go against the Syrians and the Cilicians, without any delay drove his army forward, and having traversed a journey of about three days for an unencumbered man along the bank of the Euphrates, he came upon the city of Zenobia; which Zenobia once built and, as is likely, gave her name to the city.

[26430] 5.5. Now Zenobia was the wife of Odonathus, the ruler of the Saracens there, who from of old were in treaty with the Romans.

[26556] 5.6. This Odonathus recovered for the Romans the realm of the East, which had come under the Medes. But these things took place in former times.

[26702] 5.7. Chosroes then, coming very near Zenobia, when he learned that the place was not worth account and perceived the country to be uninhabited and destitute of all good things, fearing lest some time spent there should be a hindrance to his great undertakings for no result, made trial of taking the place by agreement. But when nothing went forward, he quickly drove his army on.

[27084] 5.8. And having again traversed so great a road, he came to the city of Sura, which is by the Euphrates river,

[27196] 5.9. and coming very near it he halted. There it chanced that the horse on which Chosroes sat both neighed and struck the ground with its foot. The Magi, inferring this, declared that the place would be taken.

[27407] 5.10. And he, encamping, led the army against the wall as though to storm it.

[27486] 5.11. There chanced to be a man, Arsaces by name, an Armenian by race, commander of the soldiers there, who, having got the soldiers up onto the battlements and fighting from there most vigorously and killing many of the enemy, was struck by an arrow and died.

[27748] 5.12. And the Persians then (for it was late in the day), as though they would storm the wall again on the morrow, withdrew to their camp; but the Romans, in despair, since their commander had died, purposed to become suppliants of Chosroes.

[27991] 5.13. On the following day, then, they sent the bishop of the city to entreat and to beg off the place, who, taking with him some of his attendants bearing fowls and wine and pure loaves, came to Chosroes, and let himself down to the ground and in tears besought him to spare wretched men and a city most dishonoured for the Romans, and held of no account by the Persians, neither in former times nor ever to be hereafter; and he promised that the Surenes would give him ransom worthily of themselves and of the city in which they dwelt.

[28530] 5.14. But Chosroes was harsh toward the Surenes, because, being the first of all the Romans to fall in with him, they neither received him willingly into the city, but, daring even to take up arms against him, killed a considerable throng of notable Persians.

[28791] 5.15. He did not, however, bring out his anger, but hid it exactly beneath his face, so that, by making the punishment upon the Surenes, he might render himself terrible and invincible to the Romans. For thus he supposed that those who should ever be in his path would go over to him with no trouble.

[29093] 5.16. Therefore with much friendliness he raised up the bishop, and receiving the gifts gave a certain impression that he would presently consult with the men of note among the Persians about the ransom of the Surenes and dispose the request well.

[29342] 5.17. And thus he dismissed the bishop with his attendants, having no perception of the plot, and sent with him some of the notable Persians as escorts, ostensibly in word.

[29516] 5.18. These he secretly bade go with him up to the wall, encouraging him and lifting him with good hopes, so that he and all those with him might be seen by those within rejoicing and fearing nothing.

[29718] 5.19. And when the guards, opening the postern, should be about to receive them into the city, throwing some stone or wood between the threshold and the door, they should not allow it to be set, but should themselves stand for some time in the way of those who wished to set it; for the army would not long after follow them.

[30045] 5.20. Having charged the men these things, Chosroes got his army in readiness, and bade them go at a run against the city whenever he himself should give the signal.

[30212] 5.21. [33663] 6.3. Hierapolites, he spoke as follows: "For those whose contest against the invaders is on equal terms of power, it is not at all unfitting to come to blows with the enemy straightway; but for those who chance to be far inferior to their adversaries by a great difference, it will be more profitable to get round the enemy by certain devices than, drawing up openly against them, to go into a foreseen danger.

[34999] 6.7. So much Bouzes said, and seemed to speak what was expedient, but did none of what was needful. For having chosen out whatever was notable in the Roman army,

[35352] 6.9. And the Emperor Justinian, learning of the Persian inroad, at once sent Germanus, his own nephew, with much tumult, having three hundred followers, and promised that a great army would not long after follow him.

[36034] 6.11. For there happens to be a certain rock there, sufficiently broad for a great extent, but in height a little less than the wall.

[44249] 7.25. For I, when I was sent to declare what you enjoined upon the Antiochenes, coming into your presence on the seventh day (than which what could be quicker?), found these things wrought by you upon my fatherland.

[44657] 7.27. For to pay something of what is not present would be for a man no device at all.

[44745] 7.28. And long ago the names of things have been well and rightly distinguished by men; among which is this also, that weakness is separated from ill-will.

[48650] 8.10. The Persians, then, pressing most vigorously, sent many arrows from all sides, and especially over the height of the rock.

[48780] 8.11. And the Romans defended themselves with all their might, not soldiers only, but also many most daring young men of the populace.

[57586] 9.10. For they say that he, groaning ostensibly in word and giving an impression that he was in tears to those then present, both others and Anastasius the envoy, prayed God to punish the author of the evils that had happened.

[57814] 9.11. And he wished to point obliquely at Justinian the Emperor of the Romans, knowing well that he himself was the most to blame of all.

[57953] 9.12. Using so great a perversity of nature, Chosroes became King of the Persians (the demon having maimed the eye of Zames, who by his age bore the chief claim to the kingship, after Caoses, whom Cabades hated for no reason), and with no trouble mastered those who rose against him, and easily wrought upon the Romans whatever evils he devised.

[58300] 9.13. For Fortune, wishing ever to make some man great, accomplishes at the fitting times what she has resolved, nothing opposing the rush of her will, neither considering the man's worth nor reckoning that anything unfitting may come to be, nor that many will blaspheme her for these things, mocking what has come to one beyond his desert who has obtained her favour, nor putting any other of all things in mind, provided only that what seems good to her be accomplished. But let these things be as is dear to God.

[59218] 9.16. And taking thence many marvellous marbles, he bade them be set outside the wall, that these too might be conveyed to the country of the Persians.

[59371] 9.17. Having accomplished these things, he charged the Persians to burn the whole city. And the envoys begged him to refrain from the church alone, of which he had received sufficient ransom.

[59564] 9.18. And he, having conceded this to the envoys, bade them burn all the rest, and leaving a few there to set fire to the city, himself with all the rest withdrew to the camp where they had bivouacked before.

[59774] 10.1. Some time before this disaster God, displaying a portent to those dwelling there, signified what was to be. For the standards of the soldiers, who are settled there from of old, before standing toward the setting sun, of their own accord turned and stood toward the rising sun, and again returned to their former position, no one touching them.

[62333] 10.11. For one might say that such a thing is nothing else than that the life of men has been changed into that of beasts.

[62457] 10.12. For in never making treaties, war will be left altogether endless, and war that has no end is by nature wont ever to remove from their nature those who use it.

[91115] 15.2. The Lazi at first dwelt in Colchis, being subject to the Romans, not, however, for the payment of tribute, nor obeying them in any other command, except that, whenever their king died, the Emperor of the Romans sent the tokens of rule to him who should succeed to the kingship.

[91400] 15.3. And he guarded the borders of the country together with his subjects exactly, lest the hostile Huns from the Caucasus, which is their neighbour, journeying through Lazica should invade the land of the Romans.

[91616] 15.4. And they kept guard neither receiving money or an army from the Romans nor making any expedition together with the Romans, but ever trading by sea with the Romans dwelling on the Pontus.

[91810] 15.5. For they themselves have neither salt nor grain nor any other good thing, but furnishing skins and hides and slaves, they got their necessaries.

[91962] 15.6. But when the affairs of Gourgenes the king of the Iberians came to be as I have said before, Roman soldiers began to sojourn among the Lazi, with whom these barbarians were vexed, and most of all with the general Peter, who was given to insulting those he met.

[92230] 15.7. This Peter came from Arzanene, which is beyond the Nymphius river, subject to the Persians from of old, and had been enslaved by the Emperor Justin while still a boy, when Justin, after the capture of Amida, invaded the land of the Persians with the army of Celer. His owner, using much humanity toward him, sent him to a schoolmaster.

[92573] 15.8. And at first he became secretary of Justin, and when, Anastasius having died, Justin received the kingship of the Romans, Peter, having become general, drove into love of money as no other did and used much folly toward all.

[92805] 15.9. Later the Emperor Justinian sent other commanders to Lazica, and John whom they called Tzibus, a man come from obscure and inglorious origins, who had risen to a generalship for no other reason than that he was the most wicked of all men and most able to find unjust ways of money. He confounded and threw into confusion all the affairs of the Romans and Lazi.

[93173] 15.10. He also persuaded the Emperor Justinian to build a city on the sea among the Lazi, Petra by name; and sitting there as in a citadel he plundered the affairs of the Lazi.

[93351] 15.11. For the salt and all the other cargoes that seemed necessary to the Lazi the merchants were no longer allowed to bring to Colchis, or to buy them elsewhere thence, but he, establishing at Petra the so-called monopoly, became himself both retailer and overseer of all the business about these things, buying and selling everything to the Colchians, not as was customary, but as he was permitted.

[93754] 15.12. And at the same time the barbarians were otherwise vexed that the Roman army, not wont before, was sojourning among them. Being no longer able to bear these things, they resolved to go over to the Persians and Chosroes, and at once secretly from the Romans sent envoys to them to accomplish this.

[94059] 15.13. To whom it had been said that, having received pledges from Chosroes that he would never hand over the Lazi unwilling to the Romans, they should thus bring him with the Persian army into the country.

[94267] 15.14. Coming, then, to the Persians and secretly to the presence of Chosroes, the envoys spoke as follows: "If ever any others in all time, having revolted in some way from their own people and gone over to wholly unknown men, Fortune, doing well, has led back beyond their hope most gladly to their former friends, consider, O greatest King,

[94612] 15.15. that the Lazi are some such. For the Colchians, being allies of the Persians from the beginning, both wrought them many good things and themselves suffered; of which we have many memorials in writings, and in your palaces they are preserved to the present.

[94877] 15.16. But at a later time it befell our ancestors, whether neglected by you or for some other reason (for we have nothing clear to know about these things), to become in treaty with the Romans.

[95073] 15.17. And now both we and the king of Lazica give to the Persians ourselves and our land to use as you wish.

[95184] 15.18. And we beg you to consider thus about us: if, having suffered no harm at the hands of the Romans, but held by ill-will, we have gone over to you, shake off at once this our supplication, thinking that the Colchians will never be faithful to you either (for the manner of friendship dissolved becomes a proof of that set up toward others after it);

[95540] 15.19. but if in word friends of the Romans, but in deed having become faithful slaves, we have suffered unholy deeds from those who have tyrannized over us, receive us, your former allies, and gain as slaves those whom you used as friends, and hate a tyranny so bitter raised up near us, doing things worthy of the justice which it is ever native to the Persians to maintain.

[95918] 15.20. For he who himself does no wrong is not just, unless he is also able, having it in his power, to rescue those wronged by others.

[96055] 15.21. And it is worth saying some of the things the accursed Romans have dared against us. For to our king they have left only the appearance of kingship, and have taken from him the authority over deeds; and the king sits in the station of an underling, fearing the general who gives the orders.

[96354] 15.22. And they have set over us a multitude of soldiery, not that they may guard the country from those who trouble it (for none of our neighbours except the Romans has troubled us), but that, shutting us up as in a prison, they may become masters of what is ours.

[96621] 15.23. And reckoning to make the taking away of what is ours quicker, see, O King,

[96705] 15.24. into what an idea they have come: of the provisions whatever chance to be superfluous among them, they compel the Lazi to buy against their will, and whatever Lazica is wont to bear most useful to them, these they think fit, ostensibly in word, to buy from us, the price being fixed on both sides by the purpose of the rulers.

[97040] 15.25. And thus, together with the necessaries, they take all the gold from us, using the specious name of trade, but in deed forcing us as much as possible. And there is set over us a huckster ruler, who has made our want a kind of business by the authority of his office.

[97315] 15.26. The cause of our revolt, then, being such, has justice on its side; and what will be expedient to you yourselves in receiving the supplication of the Lazi we will at once tell.

[97500] 15.27. To the Persian realm you will add a most ancient kingdom, and you will have from it the dignity of empire extended, and it will befall you to have a share of the Roman sea through our country, in which, O King, building ships, you will reach the palace at Byzantium with no trouble. For there is no obstacle between.

[97825] 15.28. And one might add that to plunder the neighbouring barbarians of the land of the Romans every year will lie in your power.

[97956] 15.29. For that the land of the Lazi has till now been a bulwark to the Caucasus mountains you too, I suppose, well know.

[98079] 15.30. Justice, then, leading, and advantage being present, we think it would be no good counsel not to accept these words." So much the envoys said.

[98230] 15.31. And Chosroes, pleased with the words, agreed to defend the Lazi, and inquired of the envoys whether it was possible for him to go to the land of Colchis with a great army.

[98410] 15.32. For from many reports, he said, he had heard before that the country was tolerably difficult of access even for an unencumbered man, being exceedingly precipitous and held together for a great extent by thick and dense trees.

[98644] 15.33. But they insisted to him that the way there would be easy for the whole Persian army, cutting the trees and throwing them into the difficult ground of the precipices.

[98819] 15.34. And they themselves agreed to be guides of the road and forerunners for the Persians in this work.

[98926] 15.35. Lifted by this counsel, Chosroes gathered a great army and prepared the matters of the inroad, neither bringing out the plan to the Persians, except to those alone with whom he was wont to consult about secret matters, and charging the envoys to tell what was being done to no one, but he set out, ostensibly in word, for Iberia, as though to settle the affairs there; for he gave out that a Hunnic nation had attacked the Persian realm there.

[99378] 16.1. Meanwhile Belisarius, being in Mesopotamia, gathered the army from all sides, and sent some to the country of the Persians on a reconnaissance.

[99529] 16.2. And he himself, wishing to meet the enemy there, should they make another inroad into the land of the Romans, ordered and arranged the soldiers, who were for the most part naked and unarmed, cowering at the name of the Persians.

[99765] 16.3. The scouts, returning, insisted that there would be no inroad of the enemy at present; for Chosroes had occupation with a Hunnic war elsewhere.

[99916] 16.4. Belisarius, hearing this, wished at once to invade the land of the enemy with his whole army.

[100017] 16.5. And Arethas came to him with a great army of Saracens, and the emperor, writing a letter, charged him to invade the enemy's land quickly.

[100162] 16.6. Having called together all the commanders at Daras, he spoke as follows: "All of you, fellow-commanders, I know to be experienced in many wars, and I have gathered you at present, not that, reminding you or making some exhortation, I may urge your mind against the enemy (for I think you need no word to lead you to boldness), but that, taking some counsel among ourselves, we may rather choose what may seem best and most excellent for the emperor's affairs.

[100629] 16.7. For war is wont to be set right by good counsel most of all. And those who enter into counsel must make their judgement altogether free of awe and fear.

[100789] 16.8. For fear, ever astounding those who fall into it, does not allow the mind to choose the better, and awe, casting a shadow over what seem to be the better courses, carries the judgement to the opposite knowledge.

[101008] 16.9. If, then, anything seems to have been resolved either by the great emperor or by me concerning the present, let this not come upon you.

[101151] 16.10. For he, being far removed from what is being done,

[101210] 16.11. cannot fit actions to the occasions; so that there is no fear for those who oppose him in working what will profit his affairs.

[101346] 16.12. And it is impossible for me, being a man and come here after a long time from the West, not to overlook something of what is needful.

[101488] 16.13. So that it befits you, awed in nothing by my judgement, to say plainly whatever shall profit ourselves and the emperor.

[101616] 16.14. For from the beginning we came here, fellow-commanders, to hinder the enemy from making some inroad into our land; but now, things having turned out for us better than according to hope, it is in our power to take counsel about his.

[101857] 16.15. For which, I think it just that you, having been gathered, say, hiding nothing, whatever may seem best and most expedient to each."

[101997] 16.16. So much Belisarius said. And Peter and Bouzes bade him lead the army with no delay against the enemy's land. And the whole assembly at once followed their opinion.

[102169] 16.17. [106440] 17.18. The city of Petra is in part inaccessible from the sea, in part from precipitous rocks which rise there on all sides; from which it has obtained this name.

[106604] 17.19. It has one entrance on the level ground, and this not very broad; for extraordinary precipices hang over it on either side.

[106736] 17.20. There, foreseeing this from the first, those who built the city, lest the part of the wall there be assailable for them, made long walls along each precipice of the entrance for a great extent.

[106938] 17.21. And of these walls they built two towers on either side, not as was wont, but in another manner.

[107043] 17.22. For they left no empty space at all in the middle of the building, but made the towers whole from the ground up to a great height with very large stones holding to one another, so that they might be least shaken by a ram or any other engine. So the wall of Petra stood.

[107524] 17.24. And the flame, rising little by little, broke through the strength of the stones, and shaking the whole tower brought it suddenly at once to the ground.

[107685] 17.25. And the Romans who were in the tower perceived what was being done before only so far as not to fall together with it to the ground, but, fleeing, to get within the wall of the city.
