[211163] 35.1. Belisarius, then, went toward Byzantium in no order, never having landed in the land of the Italians for five years, nor having been able to go anywhere there by the road, but gripped by hidden flight all this time. And always sailing from some fort by the sea to another fort along the coast continuously, 35.2. and from this it befell that the enemy more fearlessly both enslaved Rome and, to speak in a word, all the rest. And at that time too he left the city of Perusia, which happened to be the first in Tuscany, being most bitterly besieged—which, while he was still going on his way, was taken by storm. 35.3. And coming to Byzantium, he stayed there for the rest of the time, being girt with much abundance of wealth, and gazed upon for the successes which had befallen him before, which, before he campaigned in Libya, the Deity had foretold by no obscure sign. 35.4. And the sign came about thus. There was a certain estate of Belisarius in the suburb of Byzantium which is named Panteichion, and lies on the opposite mainland. There, a little before Belisarius was about to lead the Roman army against Gelimer and Libya, it befell that the vines flourished most abundantly. 35.5. And the servants, filling a great quantity of jars with the wine that came from there, and burying the lower parts of them and stopping up the upper parts exactly with clay, deposited them in the wine-cellar. 35.6. And eight months later the wine in certain jars, boiling up, both burst the clay with which each of them had been stopped, and overflowing and pouring out in quantity flooded so much of the neighbouring ground as even to make a great pool on this floor. 35.7. And when the servants saw this, falling into great amazement, they were able to fill many amphorae from there, and again, stopping up these jars with clay, kept the matter in silence. 35.8. But when they saw this happening often about the same time, they themselves brought the matter to the owner, and he, gathering many of his friends there, showed them what was being done; and they, judging by the sign, foretold that great good would come to this house. 35.9. So matters went for Belisarius thus. And Vigilius, the high priest of Rome, together with the Italians present there at that time, many and most notable, ceased not, but needed the emperor to lay claim to Italy with all his might. 35.10. And most of all Gothigus urged him, a patrician man, who had mounted into the chair of the consuls long before; since he too had happened to come to Byzantium for this very reason lately. 35.11. And the emperor announced that he himself would take forethought for Italy, but was for the most part occupied with the doctrines of the Christians, being eager and most exceedingly strenuous to settle well the matters disputed among them. 35.12. These things were being done in Byzantium. Now there happened to be a Lombard man fleeing to the Gepaedes from the following cause. 35.13. When Vaces commanded the Lombards, he had a nephew Risiulfus by name, whom the law, when Vaces should die, called to the leadership. 35.14. So Vaces, taking forethought that the rule should go to his own son, bringing a charge against Risiulfus that had no cause, punished the man with exile. 35.15. And he, setting out from his ancestral abodes with some few, immediately fled to the Varni, two children of his being left there. 35.16. And Vaces persuaded these barbarians with money to kill Risiulfus. And of the children of Risiulfus the one died of sickness, and the other, Ildiges by name, fled to the Sclaveni. 35.17. Not long afterward Vaces, having fallen sick, was removed from among men, and the rule of the Lombards came to Valdarus, the son of Vaces. And to this boy, being quite young, Auduin, having become guardian, managed the rule. 35.18. And using much power from this, he himself not long afterward held the rule, this boy having immediately been removed from among men by sickness. 35.19. So when war was set between the Gepaedes and the Lombards, as has been said by me, Ildiges straightway, bringing with him those of the Lombards who followed him and many of the Sclaveni, came to the Gepaedes, and the Gepaedes had hope of restoring him to the rule. 35.20. But when the present treaty was made with the Lombards, Auduin straightway demanded back Ildiges from the Gepaedes, as from friends; but they by no means resolved to give over the man, but ordered him, departing from there, to save himself wherever he wished. 35.21. And he, with no delay, came again to the Sclaveni with his followers and some Gepaedes who were willing. 35.22. And setting out from there, he went to Totila and the Goths, having with him an army of no fewer than six thousand; and coming to Venetia, and meeting some Romans, of whom Lazarus was the leader, he came to close quarters, and routing them killed many. Yet he did not join the Goths, but, crossing the river Ister, withdrew again to the Sclaveni. 35.23. While these things were being done thus as has been said by me, in the meantime one of the guards of Belisarius, Indulf by name, a barbarian by race, passionate and energetic, who had happened to be left in Italy, came over to Totila and the Goths for no reason. 35.24. And Totila straightway sent him with a large army and ships to the regions of Dalmatia. 35.25. And he, coming to a place called Mucurum, which is by the sea somewhere close to Salones, at first mingled with the people there as being a Roman and belonging to Belisarius, but afterward, himself drawing his sword and exhorting those who followed him, killed them all suddenly. 35.26. And having plundered all the goods, he went off from there, and fell upon another fort lying by the coast, which the Romans call Laureate. 35.27. And mounting it, he slew those who fell in his way. When Claudianus learned this, who then commanded Salones, he sent an army against him on the so-called warships. 35.28. And these, when they came to Laureate, came to close quarters with the enemy. And being defeated by far in the battle, they fled as it became possible for each, leaving the warships in the harbour. There also the other vessels happened to be full of grain and the other provisions. 35.29. All which Indulf and the Goths, having taken and killed all who fell in their way and plundered the goods, came to Totila. 35.30. And the winter was ending, and the fourteenth year of this war which Procopius has written was coming to a close.

[217638] 36.1. After this Totila led the whole army against Rome, and sitting down set himself to a siege. Now Belisarius had happened to select three thousand for their valour, whom, establishing for the garrison of Rome, he set Diogenes over as commander, one of his own guards, an exceedingly intelligent man and good in matters of war. 36.2. Wherefore a length of time was wasted in this siege. For the besieged, by abundance of valour, appeared a match for the whole army of the Goths, and Diogenes both used the guard exactly, so that no one might go to do mischief to the wall, and, having sown grain everywhere in the city within the wall, made the provisions by no means fail them. 36.3. And often the barbarians, undertaking to assault the wall and to make trial of it, were thrust back, the Romans driving them off from there by valour. Having mastered Portus, however, they besieged Rome by force. These things, then, were going on thus. 36.4. And the emperor Justinian, when he saw Belisarius come to Byzantium, purposed to send another commander with an army against the Goths and Totila. 36.5. And if he had carried out this thought, I think, since Rome was still under him, and the soldiers there had been saved and were able to mingle with those who had come to the aid from Byzantium, he would have prevailed over his opponents in the war. 36.6. But as it was, at first, selecting Liberius, a man of the patricians of Rome, he ordered him to be in readiness, but afterward, some other occupation perhaps coming upon him, he stopped his eagerness. 36.7. And as much time was wasted in the siege of Rome, some of the Isaurians who held guard about the gate which is named after Paul the apostle (charging at once that for many years nothing had been given them by the emperor, and at the same time seeing the Isaurians who had previously given over Rome to the Goths grown sleek in the bulk of certain great sums of money), coming most secretly to a parley with Totila, agreed to give over the city, 36.8. and a fixed day was agreed upon for the deed. And when the appointed day was present, Totila contrived the following. In the river Tiber, in the first watch of the night, he let down two long vessels, putting in them men who understood how to use trumpets. 36.9. Whom he instructed to go forward rowing through the Tiber, and, when they should come close to the wall, to sound their trumpets there with all their might. 36.10. And he himself held the army of the Goths in readiness close to the said gate, which is named after Paul the apostle, escaping the notice of the enemy. 36.11. And reckoning that, if any of the Roman army, escaping notice in the dark, should be able to flee from the city, they would go to Centumcellae, since no other fortress of the places there was left to them, he resolved to lay ambushes of warlike men beforehand on the road leading there, whom he instructed to destroy the fugitives. 36.12. So those in the vessels, when they came close to the city, now used the trumpets according to what had been enjoined them. 36.13. And the Romans, struck with consternation and falling into great fear and tumult, suddenly abandoning their own watch-stations for no reason, came to the aid there at a run, thinking that the plot was against the wall there. 36.14. And only the betraying Isaurians, remaining at their own guard, both opened the gates at their will and received the enemy into the city. 36.15. And there was much slaughter of those who fell in their way there, and many, fleeing, went off through other gates, while those who went toward Centumcellae, coming under the ambushers, were destroyed. Some few of them, however, barely escaped, among whom they say Diogenes too, being wounded, was saved. 36.16. Now there was a certain man in the Roman army, Paulus by name, a Cilician by race, who at first had been set over the household of Belisarius, but afterward, as commander of a regiment of horse, campaigned in Italy and had been ranged with Diogenes for the guard of Rome. 36.17. This Paulus, when the city was then being taken, with four hundred horsemen ran up to the tomb of Hadrian and held the bridge that leads to the church of Peter the apostle. 36.18. And when the army of the Goths, it being dawn and day about to break forth, came to close quarters with these men, withstanding the enemy there most stoutly, they had the advantage; and they killed many of the barbarians, inasmuch as there was a great multitude of them and a narrow space among them. 36.19. When Totila saw this, he straightway stopped the battle, and ordered the Goths to sit quiet facing the enemy, thinking that he would take the men by famine. 36.20. So this day Paulus and the four hundred passed without food, and the night they bivouacked thus; but on the following day they resolved to feed on some of the horses, but shrinking from the unfamiliarity of the food held them off until late evening, although exceedingly hard pressed by famine. 36.21. Then, after much deliberation among themselves, and exhorting one another to courage, they resolved that it was better for them to end their life immediately by a seemly death. 36.22. For they resolved to rush upon the enemy unexpectedly, and to kill as many of them as it should be possible for each, and thus all to meet their end bravely. 36.23. Suddenly, then, embracing one another and kissing their faces, they greeted one another for death, as all about to perish immediately. 36.24. Observing which, Totila feared lest men ready to die, and having no hope of safety for the future, should do irreparable deeds to the Goths. 36.25. So sending to them, he offered them a choice of two things, that either, leaving their horses there and laying down their arms and swearing no longer to campaign against the Goths, they should depart unharmed to Byzantium, or, keeping their own goods, should for the future campaign with the Goths on equal and like terms. 36.26. These words the Romans gladly heard. And at first they all chose the way to Byzantium, but afterward, being ashamed to make their withdrawal on foot and unarmed, and fearing lest, falling into some ambushes on the retreat, they should be destroyed, and at the same time charging that the Roman treasury owed them their allowances for a long time, they all of their own will mingled with the army of the Goths, except indeed that Paulus and one of the Isaurians, Mindes by name, coming into the sight of Totila, begged him to send them to Byzantium. 36.27. For they said that they had children and wives in their native land, without whom they were not able to live. 36.28. And Totila, receiving them as men who told the truth, and presenting them with travelling supplies and sending escorts with them, let them go. And others too of the Roman army, all who had taken refuge in the churches of the city, about three hundred, receiving pledges, came over to Totila. 36.29. And Totila for the future resolved neither to raze Rome nor to abandon it, but resolved to settle there together both Goths and the Romans of the senate and all the others, from the following cause.

[224769] 37.1. Totila not long before, sending to the ruler of the Franks, asked him to give his daughter to him as wedded wife. 37.2. But he thrust aside the request, saying that Totila neither was nor would ever be king of Italy, who, having taken Rome, was by no means able to hold it, but, having razed a part of it, let it go again to the enemy. 37.3. Wherefore for the present he was eager to bring provisions into it, and ordered all to be rebuilt as quickly as possible, all that he had himself happened to raze and burn when he first took Rome; and he summoned both the Romans of the senate and all the others whom he was guarding in Campania. 37.4. And there, watching the chariot-contest, he made the whole army ready, as though to campaign against Sicily. 37.5. And at the same time he made ready the four hundred long ships as if for a sea-fight, and a fleet of great and very many ships, which had been sent there from the East by the emperor, and which he had happened to capture, with all the men and cargoes, during all this time. 37.6. And he sent Stephanus, a Roman man, as envoy to the emperor, asking him to bring this war to an end, and to make the Goths his allies, on condition that they should fight with him against the other enemies. 37.7. But the emperor Justinian neither permitted the envoy to come into his sight nor made any account at all of what was said. 37.8. When Totila heard this, he again made ready for the war. And it seemed advantageous to him, having first made trial of Centumcellae, thus to go against Sicily. 37.9. Now Diogenes the guard of Belisarius then commanded the garrison there, having an adequate power with him. 37.10. So the army of the Goths, when they came to Centumcellae, encamping close to the wall, set themselves to a siege. 37.11. And Totila, sending envoys to Diogenes, challenged both him and the soldiers about him, if it was their wish to settle matters with them by battle, to set to work at once. 37.12. And he exhorted them to have no hope that any other power would come to them from the emperor; 37.13. for it was impossible for Justinian for the future to carry on this war against the Goths, if anyone is able to judge sufficiently by what had happened concerning Rome in so long a time. 37.14. He offered them, then, a choice to take whichever they wished, either to mingle with the army of the Goths on equal and like terms, or, departing from there unharmed, to be conveyed to Byzantium. 37.15. But the Romans and Diogenes said that it was neither their wish to settle matters by battle nor indeed to mingle with the army of the Goths, since without their own children and wives they could not live. 37.16. And the city of which they held the guard they had by no means any specious reason for giving over for the present, since they had no excuse, especially as they wished to send to the emperor; 37.17. but they begged him to put off the matter for some time, in order that they might in the meantime make known to the emperor their present situation, and, no help coming meanwhile from the emperor, they should thus depart, giving over the city to the Goths, but themselves making their withdrawal not without pretext. 37.18. When this pleased Totila, a fixed day was agreed upon, and thirty men were given on either side in the position of hostages on this agreement, and the Goths, breaking off the siege, went toward Sicily. 37.19. And when they came to Rhegium, they did not cross the strait there before they had made trial of the fort in Rhegium. 37.20. Now Thurimuth and Himerius commanded the garrison there, whom Belisarius had happened to establish there. 37.21. These, since they had many and excellent men with them, thrust back the enemy as they assaulted the wall, and sallying out had the advantage in the engagement. 37.22. Afterward, however, being inferior by far in the multitude of their opponents, being shut up within the wall, they remained quiet. 37.23. So Totila left a portion of the Gothic army there for the sake of a garrison, waiting to take the Romans there at a later time through the want of provisions, and sending an army to the people of Taras, won over the fort there with no toil; and the Goths whom he had left in the country of Picenum then took the city of Ariminum by treachery. 37.24. Hearing this, the emperor Justinian purposed to establish Germanus his cousin as supreme commander of the war against the Goths and Totila, and instructed him to be in readiness. And when the report of this came to Italy, the Goths fell into great anxiety; for the repute about Germanus among all men happened to be auspicious. 37.25. And the Romans, having all at once become hopeful, and those of the emperor's army held out against the danger and hardship much more than before. 37.26. But the emperor, I know not how, having changed his mind, resolved to establish for the deed Liberius, a Roman man whom I mentioned in the previous narrative, instead of Germanus. 37.27. And he, having prepared as quickly as possible, was expected to set sail immediately with an army. But when the emperor repented again, he too remained quiet. 37.28. Then indeed Verus, with the most warlike men whom he had happened to gather about himself, coming to close quarters with the Goths who were in Picenum not far from the city of Ravenna, both lost many of his followers and himself died, having become a brave man in this struggle.

[230194] 38.1. About this time an army of Sclaveni, gathered to no more than three thousand, crossed the river Ister, no one withstanding them, and with no toil immediately crossing the river Hebrus, divided into two. 38.2. And one company of them had eighteen hundred men, and the other the remainder. 38.3. With both of these, although they were separated from one another, the commanders of the Roman army among the Illyrians and Thracians came to close quarters, and were unexpectedly defeated, and some of them perished there, while others, fleeing in no order, were saved. 38.4. And when all the generals had thus departed from each of the two barbarian armies, although they were by far inferior, the other company of the enemy joined Asbadus. 38.5. Now this was a guard of the emperor Justinian, since he happened to be enrolled among the so-called Candidati, and commanded the regiments of horse who from of old are settled in Tzurulum, the fort in Thrace, 38.6. who were many and excellent. And the Sclaveni, routing them with no toil, killed very many fleeing most shamefully, but seizing Asbadus took him alive for the moment, but afterward burned him, throwing him into the flame of a fire, having first flayed strips from the man's back. 38.7. Having accomplished these things, they plundered all the regions, both of the Thracians and the Illyrians, more fearlessly, and both companies took many forts by siege, neither having besieged a wall before, nor having dared to come down into the plain, since these barbarians had never before undertaken to overrun the land of the Romans. 38.8. Nor indeed do they appear to have crossed the river Ister with an army in all time, except as has been said by me before. 38.9. And those who had conquered Asbadus, having plundered everything in order as far as the sea, took by assault a city by the sea, although it had a garrison of soldiers, Topherus by name; which is the first of the Thracian cities by the coast and is distant from Byzantium a journey of twelve days. 38.10. And they took it in the following manner. The most hid themselves in difficult places before the wall, but some few, coming about the gates that are toward the rising sun, troubled the Romans on the battlements. 38.11. And the soldiers who held the garrison there, suspecting that they were no more than as many as were seen, taking up their arms immediately, all went out against them. 38.12. And the barbarians turned back, giving the impression to those who came against them that, having become afraid, they were going off in withdrawal; and the Romans, rushing out into the pursuit, got somewhere far from the wall. 38.13. So those from the ambushes, rising and getting behind the pursuers, made it no longer possible for them to go into the city. 38.14. And those who seemed to flee, turning back, made the Romans now caught between two enemies. And the barbarians, having destroyed all of them, assaulted the wall. 38.15. And the inhabitants of the city, deprived of the strength of the soldiers, fell into much helplessness, and even so defended themselves against the assailants from what was at hand. 38.16. And at first, heating oil and pitch most thoroughly, they poured it down on those who were assaulting the wall, and using casts of stones against them in a body, came not far from thrusting off the danger. 38.17. But afterward the barbarians, forcing them with a multitude of missiles, compelled them to abandon the battlements, and setting ladders against the wall took the city by storm. 38.18. Now they straightway killed all the men, to fifteen thousand, and plundered all the goods, but the children and women they made into the position of slaves. 38.19. And yet at first they spared no age, but both they and the other company, from the time they fell upon the land of the Romans, killed all who fell in their way, even to manhood. So that all the land which is of the Illyrians and Thracians became for the most part full of unburied corpses. 38.20. And they killed those who fell in their way neither by sword nor spear nor in any other accustomed way, but, fixing stakes most firmly into the ground and making them exceedingly sharp, they seated the wretches upon these with much force, driving the point of the stake into the middle of the buttocks and pushing it as far as the men's vitals, and thus they saw fit to make away with them. 38.21. And these barbarians, having buried four thick stakes deep in the ground, and binding upon them the hands and feet of the captives, then striking them on the head most continually with clubs, destroyed them as though they were dogs or snakes or some other beast. 38.22. And others, together with the oxen and sheep which they were by no means able to bring to their native abodes, they shut up in the chambers and burned with no sparing. Thus the Sclaveni always destroyed those whom they met. 38.23. But now both they and those of the other company, as if drunk with the multitude of the blood, saw fit for the future to take alive some of those who fell in their way, and from this, bringing along myriads of captives more than could be numbered, all withdrew home.

[235364] 39.1. Afterward the Goths assaulted the fort of the people of Rhegium, and the besieged, defending themselves most stoutly, thrust them off, and Thurimuth always displayed deeds worthy of valour against them. 39.2. But Totila, learning that provisions were failing the besieged, left a portion of the army there for the sake of a garrison, in order that the enemy might bring nothing in for the future, but, through want of the necessities, give over both themselves and the fort to the Goths; while he himself with the rest of the army, crossing over to Sicily, assaulted the wall of the people of Messana. 39.3. And Domnentiolus the nephew of Buzes, who commanded the Romans there, meeting him before the wall and coming to close quarters, had not the worst of it. 39.4. But coming again into the city and attending to the guard there, he kept quiet. And the Goths, no one sallying out against them, plundered nearly the whole of Sicily. 39.5. And the Romans besieged in Rhegium, whom Thurimuth and Himerius commanded, as has been said by me—for the necessities had altogether failed them—gave over both themselves and the fort to the enemy by agreement. 39.6. When the emperor heard this, gathering a fleet of ships and putting into this fleet an army worthy of account from the regiments of foot, and setting Liberius over them as commander, he ordered him to sail quickly to Sicily and to save the island with all his might. 39.7. But as soon as he had established Liberius commander of the fleet he repented; for the man was exceedingly old and unpractised in deeds of war. 39.8. And remitting to Artabanes all the charges against him and establishing him general of the regiments of Thrace, he sent him immediately to Sicily, providing him with not a large army, but instructing him to take over the fleet with Liberius, since he summoned Liberius to Byzantium. 39.9. And he established Germanus his own cousin as supreme commander of the war against Totila and the Goths. To him he gave not a large army, but, providing him with money worthy of account, he instructed him, having gathered a most considerable army from the Thracians and Illyrians, thus to set out to Italy with much speed. 39.10. And he ordered him to bring with him to Italy both Philemuth the Erulian with his followers and John the son-in-law of Germanus himself and nephew of Vitalian (for, being general of the regiments in Illyricum, he had his stay there). 39.11. Then much ambition seized Germanus to win the mastery over the Goths, in order that it might be his to recover both Libya and Italy for the Roman empire. 39.12. For when Stotzas had tyrannized over Libya in earlier times and held the mastery of Libya most firmly, Germanus, sent by the emperor and conquering the rebels in battle contrary to expectation, both stopped the tyranny and recovered Libya again for the Roman empire, as has been said by me in the previous narrative. 39.13. And now, the affairs of Italy having come to such a fortune as has been related by me, he wished to gird himself with great fame from this, as having been able to recover it too for the emperor. 39.14. And first (for his wife Passara had died long before) he made Matasuntha his wedded wife, the daughter of Amalasuntha the daughter of Theoderic, Wittigis being already removed from among men. 39.15. For he hoped that, if the woman should be with him in the camp, the Goths, as is natural, would be ashamed to take up arms against her, calling to mind the rule of Theoderic and Athalaric. 39.16. After this, putting forth great sums of money, some from the emperor and more from his own household, with no sparing, he was easily able in a short time to gather a large army of most warlike men unexpectedly. 39.17. For Romans, men good in matters of war, and many of the commanders whose guards and shield-bearers they had been, who had been held in disregard, followed Germanus, both from Byzantium and from the regions of Thrace and the Illyrians none the less, Justinus and Justinian his sons displaying much eagerness about these things, 39.18. since he took them too and went off. And some he gathered also from the regiments of horse that were settled in Thrace, the emperor giving them. 39.19. And many barbarians who had their stay about the river Ister, coming for the fame of Germanus and having received much money, 39.20. mingled with the Roman army. And other barbarians, gathering from every land, flowed together. And the ruler of the Lombards, having made ready a thousand men-at-arms, promised to send them immediately. 39.21. As these things and more were reported in Italy—such as report, going forward, is wont to work upon human affairs—the Goths at once both feared and fell into perplexity, whether they ought to make war against the race of Theoderic. 39.22. And the Roman soldiers, all who happened to be campaigning unwillingly with the Goths, sending a messenger to Germanus, ordered him to signify that, as soon as they should see him present in Italy and his army encamping, they too, with no delay, would by all means range themselves with those men. 39.23. Encouraged by all these things, those of the emperor's army, both in Ravenna and wherever else any city happened to have been left to them, having become exceedingly hopeful, saw fit to guard the places exactly for the emperor. 39.24. But all who had previously come to close quarters either with Verus or with certain others of the enemy, and being defeated by their opponents in the engagement had fled and were going about scattered, as it had befallen each, when they heard that Germanus was on his way, gathering in a body in Istria, and there awaiting this army, remained quiet. 39.25. Then indeed Totila (for the day agreed upon by him and Diogenes about Centumcellae had arrived), sending to him, ordered him to give over the city according to what had been agreed. 39.26. But Diogenes said that he himself was no longer master of this; for he had heard that Germanus had been established supreme commander of this war and was not far off with his army. 39.27. And if it should be his wish, he was willing to take back his own hostages and to give back those given to them by the Goths. 39.28. And dismissing those sent to him, he attended to the guard of the city, awaiting Germanus and the army with him. 39.29. These things, then, were going on thus, and the winter was ending, and the fifteenth year of this war which Procopius has written was coming to a close.

[241859] 40.1. While Germanus was gathering and ordering his army in Sardica, the city of the Illyrians, and most strongly fitting out all the preparation for the war, a throng of Sclaveni, such as had never come before, came to the land of the Romans; and crossing the river Ister, they came about Naisus. 40.2. Some few of these, having been scattered from the camp and wandering and going about alone in the regions there, some of the Romans, seizing and binding, inquired for what reason this army of the Sclaveni and intending to accomplish what had crossed the river Ister. 40.3. And they affirmed that they had come to take Thessalonica itself and the cities about it by siege. When the emperor heard this, he was greatly thrown into confusion and immediately wrote to Germanus to put off for the present the journey to Italy, and to defend Thessalonica and the other cities, and to repel the assault of the Sclaveni with all his power. And Germanus was occupied with these things. 40.4. And the Sclaveni, learning expressly from the captives that Germanus was in Sardica, came into fear; 40.5. for Germanus had a great name among these barbarians from the following cause. When Justinian the uncle of Germanus held the empire, the Antae, who dwell close to the Sclaveni, crossing the river Ister, invaded the land of the Romans with a great army. 40.6. Now the emperor had happened to establish Germanus general of all Thrace not long before. He, coming to close quarters with the army of the enemy and conquering them by force in battle, killed nearly all of them, and won great fame from this deed among all men and especially among these barbarians. 40.7. So fearing him, as has been said by me, the Sclaveni, and at the same time thinking that he was bringing with him a most considerable power, since he was being sent by the emperor against Totila and the Goths, straightway abstained from the road to Thessalonica, and no longer dared to come down into the plain, but, traversing all the mountains of the Illyrians, came into Dalmatia. 40.8. Germanus, then, being relieved of these, instructed the whole army to make ready, as about, two days later, to begin the journey from there to Italy. 40.9. But a certain fortune befell him, that, falling sick suddenly, he ended his life. And Germanus was straightway removed from among men, a man brave and exceedingly energetic, in war both the best of generals and a skilful worker with his own hands, and in peace and good times most steadfast in guarding the laws and the order of the state; judging most uprightly of all men; lending great sums of money to all who needed and never receiving from them so much as the mention of interest; in the Palatium and the forum most weighty and exceedingly grave, but a host at home each day pleasant and liberal and gracious; allowing nothing to be done amiss in the Palatium contrary to custom, so far as he was able, and never having shared in the wish or the company of the factions in Byzantium, although many even of those in power had gone to such a degree of impropriety. These things, then, went thus. 40.10. And the emperor, being grievously distressed at what had befallen, ordered John the nephew of Vitalian and son-in-law of Germanus, together with Justinian, one of the two children of Germanus, to lead this army to Italy. 40.11. And they went toward Dalmatia, intending to winter in Salones, since they thought it impossible at that time of the year, going around the circuit of the gulf, to be conveyed to Italy; for to ferry across, since ships were not present for them, was impracticable. 40.12. And Liberius, not yet having learned anything of what the emperor repented concerning this fleet, put in at Syracuse, which was being besieged by the enemy. 40.13. And forcing the barbarians there, he both put in to the harbour and got with the whole fleet inside the wall. 40.14. And Artabanes not long afterward, coming to Cephalonia, when he learned that Liberius and his men had already set out from there and gone to Sicily, setting out from there immediately crossed the sea called the Adriatic. 40.15. And when he came near the Calabrians, a prodigious storm falling upon him, and the wind being exceedingly hard and going against them, it befell that all the ships were so scattered as to make it seem that the many, driven out to Calabria, had come under the enemy. 40.16. But it was not so, but, driven by the wind with much force, they turned back, being prodigiously forced, and came again to the Peloponnese. And the others, wherever they chanced, it befell either to be destroyed or to be saved. 40.17. But one ship, in which Artabanes himself was sailing, its mast having been broken off in this tossing, coming into such danger, both borne by the surge and followed by the wave, put in at the island of Melite. So it befell that Artabanes was saved unexpectedly. 40.18. And Liberius, being able neither to sally out against the besiegers nor to settle matters with them by battle, and the provisions, since they were many, in no way sufficing for a longer time, setting out from there with his followers and escaping the notice of the enemy, withdrew to Panormus. 40.19. And Totila and the Goths, having plundered nearly all the regions of Sicily, bringing along a great quantity of horses and other animals, and carrying off the grain and all the other crops from the island, and putting all the goods, which were quite great, into their vessels, suddenly abandoned the island and returned to Italy, set in motion by the following manner. 40.20. A certain Roman, Spinus by name, setting out from Spolitium, Totila had happened not long before to establish as his assessor. 40.21. This man had his stay in the city of Catana, which was unwalled. And a certain fortune befell him, to come under the enemy there. 40.22. Whom Totila, being eager to ransom, wished to release to the Romans in his stead a woman of certain notable men, who was a captive. 40.23. But the Romans by no means thought it just to exchange a woman for a man who held the office of the so-called quaestor. 40.24. So the man, fearing lest he be destroyed among the enemy, agreed with the Romans that he would immediately persuade Totila to set out from Sicily and to ferry the whole Gothic army to Italy. 40.25. And they, having bound him with oaths concerning this agreement, gave him back to the Goths, having received the woman in his stead. 40.26. And he, coming into the sight of Totila, said that it was not to their advantage that the Goths, having plundered nearly the whole of Sicily, should stay there for the sake of some few forts. 40.27. For he affirmed that he had lately heard, while he was among the enemy, that Germanus the cousin of the emperor had been removed from among men, but that John his son-in-law and Justinian his son were already in Dalmatia with the whole army that had been gathered by Germanus, and that, fitting themselves out, they would set out from there straight for Liguria immediately, in order that, by a sudden raid, they might enslave the children and wives of the Goths and plunder all the goods; whom it would be better for us to meet, wintering in safety with our own people. 40.28. For if we should prevail over them, it would be possible for us, at the beginning of spring, more fearlessly to set foot on Sicily again, having no enemy in mind. 40.29. Persuaded by this suggestion, Totila left guards in four strongholds, and himself, bringing along all the booty, ferried across to Italy with the whole rest of the army. These things, then, were going on thus. 40.30. And John and the emperor's army, coming to Dalmatia, resolved to winter in Salones, intending, after the winter season, to go from there straight to Ravenna by the road. 40.31. And the Sclaveni, both those who had previously come into the emperor's land, as has been related by me lately, and others not long afterward, crossing the river Ister and mingling with the former, overran the Roman empire with much license. 40.32. And some held it in suspicion that Totila, having persuaded these barbarians with much money, sent them against the Romans there, in order that it might be impossible for the emperor to manage well the war against the Goths, being occupied with these barbarians. 40.33. But whether the Sclaveni came there to gratify Totila or uninvited I cannot say. Dividing themselves, however, into three bodies, these barbarians wrought irreparable deeds in all Europe, not plundering the regions there by a sudden raid, but as though wintering in their own country and fearing no enemy. 40.34. And afterward the emperor Justinian sent a most considerable army against them, of which, among others, Constantianus and Aratius and Nazares were commanders, and Justinus the son of Germanus and John whom they called by the surname Phagas. 40.35. And he established Scholasticus, one of the eunuchs in the Palatium, as overseer over all. 40.36. This army caught a portion of the barbarians about Adrianople, which lies in the midland of Thrace, distant a journey of five days from Byzantium. 40.37. And the barbarians could no longer go forward; for they were bringing along booty of men and other animals and all goods beyond number. 40.38. And remaining there, they were eager to come to close quarters with the enemy, giving them by no means any perception of this. And the Sclaveni encamped on the mountain that rises there, while the Romans were in the plain not far off. 40.39. And as much time was wasted for them in this sitting, the soldiers were vexed and made a great matter of it, charging against the generals that they, having all the provisions in abundance as commanders of the Roman army, overlooked the soldiers being hard pressed by the want of the necessities, and were unwilling to come to close quarters with the enemy. 40.40. By these the generals, being compelled, joined battle with their opponents. And a fierce battle took place, but the Romans were defeated by force. 40.41. There many and excellent soldiers perished, and the generals, coming near to fall under the enemy, with the survivors barely escaping were saved, 40.42. as it became possible for each. And the barbarians took the standard of Constantianus, and, holding the Roman army in disregard, advanced forward. 40.43. And they plundered the country called Astice at their will, which had been unravaged from of old, and from this it befell them to find much booty there; and thus, plundering much country, they came as far as the Long Walls, which are distant a little more than a day's journey from Byzantium. 40.44. But not long afterward the Roman army, following these barbarians, and falling in with a portion of them and coming to close quarters suddenly, routed them. 40.45. And they killed many of the enemy, and saved a great quantity of the Roman captives, and finding the standard of Constantianus took it up. And the rest of the barbarians, with the other booty, withdrew home.
