CHAPTER: 03-book-3



[0] 1.1. The Median war, then, came for the Emperor Justinian to this end; but I now go on to tell what he wrought against the Vandals and the Moors. And it shall first be told whence the army of the Vandals attacked the land of the Romans.

[238] 1.2. When Theodosius, the Emperor of the Romans, had vanished from among men, a man who had been most just and good in war, both his sons succeeded to his kingship, Arcadius the elder to the eastern portion, and Honorius the younger to the western.

[488] 1.3. The Roman dominion had been divided thus from of old, from the time of Constantine and his sons, who, having transferred the kingship to Byzantium and made the city greater and far more illustrious, allowed it to be named after himself.

[731] 1.4. The earth is encompassed in a circle by the ocean, either the whole or the greater part (for we do not yet know anything clearly about this); and it is cleft in two into two continents by a certain outflow from it, which breaks in at the western part and makes this very sea, beginning from Gadeira and reaching to the Maeotic Lake itself.

[1077] 1.5. Of these two continents the one on the right as one sails into the sea up to the lake is called Asia, beginning from Gadeira and the other of the Pillars of Heracles.

[1250] 1.6. The natives call the fortress there Septem, since seven hills appear there; for septem means seven in the Latin tongue.

[1376] 1.7. And the whole continent opposite it has been called Europe. And the strait there divides each continent by about eighty-four stades, but from there on they are separated from one another by great seas as far as the Hellespont.

[1609] 1.8. For here they come together again about Sestos and Abydos, and again at Byzantium and Chalcedon as far as the rocks called of old the Cyaneae, where now is the place named Hieron. For in these places they are separated from one another by a measure of ten stades and less than this.

[1898] 1.9. From the one of the Pillars of Heracles to the other, going along the shore and not going round the Ionian Gulf and the so-called Euxine Sea, but crossing from Chalcedon to Byzantium and from Dryous to the continent opposite, the journey is two hundred and eighty-five days for an unencumbered man.

[2203] 1.10. For to reckon exactly all the parts about the Euxine Sea, which goes from Byzantium to the lake, was impossible, the barbarians beyond the river Ister, which they also call the Danube, making the shore there by no means passable for the Romans, except that from Byzantium to the mouths of the Ister is a journey of twenty-two days, which it befits one reckoning to place in Europe.

[2592] 1.11. And on the side of Asia, that is, from Chalcedon to the river Phasis, which flowing from the Colchians goes down to the Pontus, the journey is accomplished in forty days.

[2770] 1.12. So that the whole dominion of the Romans, at least by the journey along the sea, comes to a measure of three hundred and forty-seven days, if one, as has been said, crosses the Ionian Gulf, reaching to about eight hundred stades, from Dryous.

[3020] 1.13. For the passage of the gulf reaches a journey of not less than four days. So great was the Roman realm, at least in ancient time.

[3157] 1.14. And to the one holding the dominion of the West fell the greater part of Libya, reaching to a journey of ninety days; for so much is it from Gadeira to the borders of Tripolis in Libya; and in Europe he received a journey of seventy-five days;

[3408] 1.15. for so much chances to be from the other of the Pillars of Heracles to the Ionian Gulf. And one might add the circuit of the gulf too.

[3550] 1.16. And the emperor of the East was allotted a journey of a hundred and twenty days, from the borders of Cyrene in Libya as far as Epidamnus, which lies by the Ionian Gulf itself, now called Dyrrhachium, and all that is about the Euxine Sea, as said before, is under the Romans.

[3832] 1.17. And a journey of one day reaches two hundred and ten stades, as much as to go from Athens to Megara. Thus the Emperors of the Romans divided each continent between them.

[4009] 1.18. And of the islands, Britain, which is outside the Pillars of Heracles and by far the greatest of all islands, was assigned to the western portion, as was likely; and within them Ebusa, lying in the sea, as it were in a Propontis after the inflow of the ocean, reaching to about a seven days' journey, and two others near it, called in the native tongue Maiorica and Minorica.

[4392] 1.19. And of the islands in the sea, each fell to one of the two emperors, according as it chanced to lie within the borders of the one.

[4530] 2.1. And while Honorius held the kingship toward the setting sun, barbarians seized his land; who they were and in what manner shall be told.

[4673] 2.2. There were many other Gothic nations both before and now, but the greatest and most worthy of account of all are the Goths and the Vandals and the Visigoths and the Gepids. Of old, however, they were named Sauromatae and Melanchlaeni; and there are some who called these nations Getic.

[4965] 2.3. All these differ from one another in name, as has been said, but in nothing else at all.

[5060] 2.4. For they are all white in body and fair-haired, tall and good in countenance, and use the same laws,

[5167] 2.5. and have practised the things of God alike. For they are all of the Arian faith, and they have one tongue, called Gothic; and it seems to me that they were all of one nation in old time, but were later distinguished by the names of those who led each of them.

[5433] 2.6. This people dwelt of old beyond the river Ister. Afterward the Gepids held the places about Singidunum and Sirmium, both within and outside the river Ister, where indeed they are settled even to my time.

[5643] 2.7. Of the others, the Visigoths, rising up thence, at first came into alliance with the Emperor Arcadius, but at a later time (for the faith toward the Romans does not know how to dwell among barbarians), with Alaric leading them, they turned to plot against each emperor, and beginning from Thrace they treated all Europe as hostile.

[5981] 2.8. The Emperor Honorius before this sat in Rome, having nothing hostile in mind, but content, I think, if someone allowed him to remain quietly in his palace.

[6143] 2.9. But when the barbarians were reported to be, not far off, but somewhere among the Taulantii with a great army, leaving the palace in no order he fled to Ravenna, a strong city lying at the very end of the Ionian Gulf.

[6367] 2.10. There are some who say that he himself brought in the barbarians, a faction having arisen against him from his subjects; but to me they tell things not to be believed,

[6542] 2.11. so far as one may reckon from the man's character. The barbarians, since nothing hostile met them, became the most savage of all men. For the cities they took they so destroyed that no token of them is left to my time, especially within the Ionian Gulf, except that it chanced one tower or one gate or some such thing was left to them;

[6885] 2.12. and they killed all the men they met, alike old and young, sparing neither women nor children. Whence even to this day Italy chances to be sparsely peopled.

[7049] 2.13. And they plundered all the money from all Europe, and, the chief point, leaving nothing at all in Rome of either public or private property, they went on to Gaul. And in what manner Alaric took Rome, I shall show.

[7270] 2.14. When much time had been spent by him in the siege and he was able by neither force nor any other device to take the place, he devised the following.

[7426] 2.15. Of the young men in his army not yet bearded, but just come of age, he chose out three hundred, whom he knew to be well-born and to lay claim to valour beyond their age, and told them secretly that he was about to make a gift of them to some of the patricians in Rome, as though they were slaves, in word.

[7739] 2.16. And he charged them that, as soon as they were in those men's houses, displaying much gentleness and prudence, they should eagerly serve in all that was laid upon them by their owners;

[7931] 2.17. then not long after, on an appointed day, about midday, when all who had taken them would be taking, as is likely, sleep after their meal, they should all come to the gate called Salaria, and falling unexpectedly upon the guards kill them and open the gates as quickly as possible.

[8220] 2.18. Having charged the young men these things, Alaric at once sent envoys to the members of the senate, declaring that he admired them for their goodwill toward their own emperor, and would no longer trouble them for the future, on account of the valour and faith which they were manifestly so eager to claim, and that, in order that memorials of him might be preserved among fair and good men, he wished to gift each of them with some servants.

[8669] 2.19. Having signified this and sent the young men not long after, he bade the barbarians prepare for departure, and gave the Romans perception of this.

[8823] 2.20. And they gladly heard his words and, receiving the gifts, were in much good cheer, being very far from the barbarian's plot.

[8955] 2.21. For the young men, by being more obedient to their owners, turned away suspicion, and of the army some were already seen rising up and breaking up the siege, while others were reckoned likely to do this same thing very soon.

[9187] 2.22. And when the appointed day came, Alaric, having armed his whole army for the assault, held it in readiness very near the gate Salaria; for there he had chanced to be encamped at the beginning of the siege.

[9400] 2.23. And all the young men, coming to this gate at the agreed time of day, falling suddenly upon the guards killed them, and flinging open the gates received Alaric and the army into the city at their pleasure.

[9613] 2.24. And they set fire to the houses which were near the gate, among which was that of Sallust, who wrote the history for the Romans of old, of which the greater part stands half-burned even to my time; and having plundered the whole city and destroyed most of the Romans, they went on.

[9902] 2.25. Then they say that at Ravenna one of the eunuchs, evidently a keeper of fowls, announced to the Emperor Honorius that Rome had perished. And he, crying out, said, "And yet it has just eaten from my hands." For he had a very large cock,

[10145] 2.26. Rome by name; and the eunuch, understanding the word, said that the city of Rome had perished at the hands of Alaric, and the emperor, taking it up, replied, "But I, comrade, thought that Rome my fowl had perished." So great was the folly, they say, with which this emperor was held.

[10436] 2.27. But some say that Rome was not taken by Alaric thus, but that Proba, a woman most conspicuous in wealth and repute in the Roman senate, took pity on the Romans being destroyed by famine and other suffering, who were even already tasting of one another; and seeing that all good hope had failed them, the river and the harbour being held by the enemy, she bade her servants by night open the gates.

[10841] 2.28. When Alaric was about to depart from Rome, he proclaimed Attalus, one of the patricians, Emperor of the Romans, investing him with the diadem and the purple and whatever else pertains to imperial dignity. And he did this purposing to depose Honorius from the kingship and to hand over the whole western dominion to Attalus.

[11172] 2.29. With such a purpose Attalus and Alaric went against Ravenna with a great army. But this Attalus was neither able himself to understand anything nor to be persuaded by one who advised well.

[11368] 2.30. So when Alaric by no means approved, he sent commanders to Libya without an army. These things, then, were being done thus.

[11499] 2.31. And the island of Britain revolted from the Romans, and the soldiers there chose for themselves as emperor Constantine, a man not obscure. He at once, having gathered a fleet of ships and a considerable army, invaded Spain and Gaul with a great army as though to enslave them.

[11783] 2.32. And Honorius had ships in readiness, and awaited the fortunes from Libya, that, if those sent by Attalus should be repulsed, he himself might sail to Libya and hold some portion of his own kingship, but if affairs there should go against him, he might come to Theodosius and be with him.

[12078] 2.33. For Arcadius having already long before died, Theodosius his son, being still quite a child, held the dominion of the East.

[12209] 2.34. To Honorius, awaiting these things and carried in the third wave of fortune, marvellous strokes of good fortune befell.

[12336] 2.35. For God loves to help and assist those who are neither quick-witted nor able to contrive anything of themselves, if they are not wicked, when they are at the utmost loss; which indeed befell this emperor too.

[12552] 2.36. For from Libya it was suddenly announced that the commanders of Attalus had been destroyed, and a multitude of ships from Byzantium with as many soldiers as possible, come to his aid, were present to him though he had not expected them; and Alaric, having become at variance with Attalus, took from him the imperial appearance and held him, now reckoned in the rank of a private man, in custody.

[12955] 2.37. And after this Alaric died of disease, and the army of the Visigoths, with Adaulphus leading them, went on to Gaul, and Constantine, worsted in battle, died with his sons.

[13134] 2.38. The Romans, however, were no longer able to recover Britain, but it, being under tyrants, remained so from then on.

[13257] 2.39. And the Goths, having made the crossing of the Ister, at first held Pannonia, then, the emperor granting it, dwelt in the regions of Thrace.

[13405] 2.40. And there, having spent not much time, they mastered the West. But these things will be told in the books about the Goths.

[13535] 3.1. The Vandals, dwelling about the Maeotic Lake, when they were pressed by famine, went to the Germans, who are now called Franks, and the Rhine river,

[13690] 3.2. having made companions of the Alani, a Gothic nation. Then thence, with Godigisclus leading them, they settled in Spain, which is the first land of the Roman realm from the ocean. Then Honorius makes terms with Godigisclus, on condition that they should settle there not to the harm of the country.

[13995] 3.3. And there being a law among the Romans, that if any should hold their own property not by their own hands and a time should pass amounting to thirty years, these should no longer be empowered to proceed against those who had forced them, but a bar to entrance into the court should be set against them by prescription, the emperor wrote a law that the time of the Vandals, which they should spend in the Roman realm, should by no means count toward this thirty-year prescription.

[14481] 3.4. Honorius, the dominion of the West having been brought to this for him, died of disease. Constantius had chanced before to hold the kingship together with Honorius, the husband of Placidia, the sister of Arcadius and Honorius, who, surviving in his rule a few days and falling grievously sick, then died, Honorius being alive, having been able neither to say nor do anything worth account; for the time he lived in the kingship did not suffice him.

[14936] 3.5. The son of this Constantius, Valentinian, just weaned, was being reared in the palace of Theodosius, and those of the imperial court in Rome chose one of the soldiers there, John by name, as emperor.

[15142] 3.6. This man was gentle and well endowed with intelligence and able to lay claim to virtue.

[15236] 3.7. Holding the tyranny five years, then, he ruled moderately, and neither lent his ear to slanderers nor wrought any unjust murder of his own will nor set himself to the taking away of money; but against the barbarians he had been able to do nothing, since the things from Byzantium were hostile to him.

[15543] 3.8. Against this John, Theodosius the son of Arcadius, sending a great army and the generals Aspar and Ardaburius, the son of Aspar, both took from him the tyranny and handed over the kingship to Valentinian, who was still a child.

[15777] 3.9. And Valentinian, having taken John alive in the hippodrome of Aquileia, with one of his hands cut off, led him in and paraded him riding on an ass, and having suffered and heard many things from those on the stage there, killed him. Thus Valentinian received the dominion of the West.

[16068] 3.10. And Placidia his mother reared and educated this emperor with an effeminate upbringing and instruction, and from this he became full of vice from boyhood.

[16230] 3.11. For he consorted much with sorcerers and those curious about the stars, and being demonically eager for loves of others' wives he used much lawlessness in his way of life, although he dwelt with a wife exceedingly comely in appearance.

[16473] 3.12. Therefore he recovered for the kingship nothing of what had been taken before, but lost Libya in addition and was himself destroyed.

[16613] 3.13. And when he died, it befell his wife and children to become captives. And the disaster in Libya came about thus.

[16733] 3.14. There were two generals of the Romans, Aetius and Boniface, very mighty men and experienced in many wars, second to none at least of those of that time.

[16893] 3.15. These two men became at variance in their politics, but came to such a degree of magnanimity and other virtue that, if anyone should call each of them the last man of the Romans, he would not err; so the whole virtue of the Romans chanced to be distinguished into these two men.

[17179] 3.16. Of these the one, Boniface, Placidia appointed general of all Libya. This was not to the liking of Aetius, but he by no means showed that it did not please him. For their enmity had not yet come to light, but was hidden beneath the face of each.

[17432] 3.17. When Boniface had got out of his way, he slandered him to Placidia, saying that he was setting up a tyranny, depriving her and the emperor of all Libya, and he said it was easy for her to find out the truth; for if she should summon Boniface to Rome, he would never come.

[17711] 3.18. When the woman heard this, Aetius seemed to her to speak well, and she did accordingly. But Aetius, anticipating, wrote secretly to Boniface that the emperor's mother was plotting against him and wished to make away with him.

[17944] 3.19. And he foretold that there would be a great proof of the plot: for from no cause he would very soon be summoned. This the letter declared.

[18090] 3.20. And Boniface, not disregarding what was written, when those came who summoned him as emperor wished, refused to obey the emperor and his mother, declaring to no one the suggestion of Aetius.

[18288] 3.21. Placidia, then, when she heard this, thought Aetius exceedingly well-disposed toward the emperor's affairs, and took counsel about Boniface.

[18436] 3.22. And Boniface (for it seemed to him neither possible to oppose the emperor, nor was any safety apparent for one departing to Rome) took counsel how, if he could, there might be an alliance for him with the Vandals, who, as said before, had settled in Spain not far from Libya.

[18719] 3.23. There Godigisclus had died, and his sons succeeded to the rule, Gontharis, born to him of a wedded wife, and Gizeric, a bastard.

[18855] 3.24. But the one was still a child and not very effective, while Gizeric was most excellently trained in war and was the cleverest of all men.

[19000] 3.25. Boniface, then, sending to Spain his most intimate friends, won over each of the sons of Godigisclus on an equal and like footing, on condition that each of them, holding a third part of Libya, should rule those under him; and if anyone should go to make war on one of them,

[19282] 3.26. they should jointly defend themselves against the assailants. On these terms the Vandals, crossing the strait at Gadeira, came to Libya, and the Visigoths at a later time settled in Spain.

[19478] 3.27. In Rome the intimates of Boniface, considering the man's character and reckoning how great the unreasonableness was, marvelled greatly that Boniface should set up a tyranny, and some of them, at Placidia's bidding, went to Carthage.

[19718] 3.28. There, being with Boniface, they saw the letters of Aetius, and hearing the whole matter, turned back to Rome as quickly as they could and reported to her how Boniface stood.

[19900] 3.29. The woman, struck with consternation, did Aetius no harm and reproached him with nothing of what he had wrought against the imperial house, since he himself wielded great power and the affairs of the kingship were already in evil case; but to the friends of Boniface she told the suggestion of Aetius, and offering pledges and oaths begged of them that they should, if they could, persuade the man to return to his native land, not overlooking the Roman realm lying under barbarians.

[20391] 3.30. When Boniface heard this, he repented of the deed and of the agreement with the barbarians, and besought them, undertaking all things without measure, to depart from Libya.

[20571] 3.31. But they not accepting his words, but thinking themselves insulted, he was forced to come to blows with them, and worsted in battle withdrew to Hippo Regius, a strong city lying in Numidia by the sea.

[20779] 3.32. There the Vandals, encamping with Gizeric leading them, besieged it; for Gontharis had already died. And they say he perished at the hands of his brother.

[20941] 3.33. But the Vandals, not agreeing with this, say that Gontharis, seized in Spain by the Germans in battle, was impaled, and that Gizeric, being already sole ruler, led the Vandals to Libya.

[21134] 3.34. These things, then, I have heard thus from the Vandals. And when much time had passed, since they were able to subdue Hippo Regius neither by force nor by agreement and were pressed by famine, they broke up the siege.

[21359] 3.35. A little later Boniface and the Romans in Libya, since a great army had come to them from Rome and Byzantium and the general Aspar, thought fit to renew the battle, and a fierce battle taking place, being worsted by the enemy by far, they rushed to flight each as he could.

[21640] 3.36. And Aspar was conveyed home, and Boniface, coming to Placidia, dissolved the suspicion, that it had arisen against him from no true cause.

[21786] 4.1. Thus, then, the Vandals, having taken Libya from the Romans, held it. And the enemies whom they took alive, reckoning them in the rank of slaves, they kept in custody.

[21960] 4.2. Among these it chanced that Marcian too was, who later, on the death of Theodosius, received the kingship.

[22073] 4.3. At that time Gizeric bade the captives be present in the king's court, that, examining, he might be able to know to what master each of them might serve, not according to his worth.

[22261] 4.4. And when they were gathered in the open, about midday in the summer season, distressed by the sun, they sat. And among them Marcian too somewhere was sleeping neglected.

[22437] 4.5. And an eagle flew over him, spreading its wings, as they say, and ever remaining in the same place of the air overshadowed only Marcian.

[22580] 4.6. Gizeric, seeing what was being done from the upper rooms, being a most quick-witted man, suspected the thing to be divine, and summoning the man inquired of him who he might be.

[22764] 4.7. And he said that he was a sharer of the secrets of Aspar; the Romans call this man a domesticus in their own tongue.

[22887] 4.8. When Gizeric heard this and inferred the work of the bird, and had in mind the power which Aspar wielded in Byzantium, it became plain to him that the man was being led to the kingship.

[23079] 4.9. He by no means resolved to kill him, reckoning that, if he should make him vanish from among men, it would be clear that the work of the bird would be nothing (for it would not be paying court to a king with its shadow, one who was about presently to perish), and that he would kill him for no reason; but if it was destined that the man should reign at a later time, never could he be taken by death; for the things of God, coming to counsel, could not be hindered by a man's judgement.

[23573] 4.10. So he bound him with oaths that, if it should be in his power, he should never come in arms against the Vandals. Thus Marcian, being released, came to Byzantium, and when Theodosius died at a later time, received the kingship.

[23807] 4.11. And in all other respects he had become a good emperor, but the affairs of Libya he held of no account. But these things took place at a later time.

[23963] 4.12. And Gizeric then, having conquered Aspar and Boniface in battle and displayed a forethought worthy of recounting, secured his good fortune most firmly.

[24122] 4.13. For fearing lest, if again an army should go against him from Rome and Byzantium, the Vandals should not be able to use the same strength and fortune, since human affairs are wont to be tripped up by divine things and to be worsted in their bodies, not lifted up by what he had achieved, but moderate from what he had feared, he made a treaty with the Emperor Valentinian on condition that he should pay tribute to the emperor from Libya each year, and gave over one of his sons, Honoric, as a hostage on this agreement.

[24650] 4.14. Gizeric, then, both proved a brave man in the battle and most securely preserved the victory, and got back his son Honoric when their friendship advanced greatly.

[24820] 4.15. And in Rome Placidia died first, then Valentinian her son, childless of male offspring, though two daughters were born to him by Eudoxia the daughter of Theodosius. In what manner Valentinian died, I now go on to tell.

[25046] 4.16. There was a certain Maximus, a man of senatorial rank, a Roman, of the house of that Maximus whom the elder Theodosius, having put down his tyranny, killed; over whom indeed the Romans keep a yearly festival named after the defeat of Maximus.

[25296] 4.17. This younger Maximus dwelt with a wife prudent in character and exceedingly famous for beauty. Therefore a certain desire came to Valentinian to come to her bed.

[25465] 4.18. And when, wishing to be with her, it was impossible, he plotted unholy deeds and carried them out.

[25571] 4.19. For summoning Maximus to the palace he sat down with him to play dice, and a fixed sum of gold was appointed as the penalty for the loser;

[25717] 4.20. and the emperor, having won in this game and got the ring of Maximus as a pledge for the agreement, sends it to his house, having charged the bearer to say to the woman that Maximus bade her come to the palace as quickly as possible to greet the empress Eudoxia.

[25987] 4.21. And she, inferring from the ring that the word was from Maximus, getting into the litter was conveyed to the emperor's court.

[26120] 4.22. And those to whom this service from the emperor was laid, taking her, lead her into a chamber of the women's quarters far off, where Valentinian, meeting her, forced her against her will.

[26315] 4.23. And she, after the outrage, coming to her husband's house in tears and exceedingly grieved at the misfortune, laid many curses upon Maximus, as having furnished the cause of what was done.

[26511] 4.24. Maximus, then, exceedingly grieved at what had befallen, at once set himself to a plot against the emperor; but seeing that Aetius wielded great power, who had just conquered Attila when he invaded the Roman realm with a great army of Massagetae and the other Scythians, the thought came to him that Aetius would be a hindrance to his designs.

[26862] 4.25. And reckoning these things, it seemed better to him to make away with Aetius first, taking no account that on him rested the whole hope of the Romans.

[27020] 4.26. And the eunuchs about the emperor's service being well-disposed toward him, he persuaded the emperor by their devices that Aetius was attempting revolutionary measures.

[27196] 4.27. And Valentinian, judging the word sound by nothing other than the power and virtue of Aetius, killed the man.

[27313] 4.28. On which occasion a certain Roman won repute by a saying. For when the emperor asked him whether the death of Aetius had been well wrought, he answered, saying that he could not know whether it was well or otherwise wrought by him, but that he knew this most excellently, that he had cut off his own right hand with his other hand.

[27652] 4.29. So when Aetius had died, Attila, no one being his match, plundered all Europe with no trouble and held both kingdoms subject to the payment of tribute. For tribute was sent to him by the emperors every year.

[27867] 4.30. At that time, while Attila was besieging the city of Aquileia, great and exceedingly populous, on the seacoast, but outside the Ionian Gulf,

[28015] 4.31. they say a piece of good fortune of the following kind befell. For they say that he, since he was able neither by force nor in any other way to take the place, and despaired of the siege, now grown very long, bade his whole army at once prepare for withdrawal, so that on the morrow they should all rise up thence at sunrise.

[28348] 4.32. And on the following day about sunrise, the barbarians, having broken up the siege, were already holding to the departure, when a single stork, which had a nest on one of the towers of the city wall and was rearing its young, suddenly rose up thence with its offspring.

[28625] 4.33. And the father stork flew, but the young storks, as being not yet able to fly altogether, in part shared in his flight, in part were borne on the father's back, and thus flying off they got very far from the city.

[28846] 4.34. Which Attila, seeing (for he was most clever both at understanding and inferring everything), bade his army remain again in the same place, adding that the bird would never have flown off thence in vain with its young, unless it had divined that something untoward would befall the place not long after.

[29157] 4.35. Thus, they say, the army of the barbarians settled again to the siege, and a portion of the wall, that which held the nest of this bird, not long after, from no cause suddenly fell, and there was an entrance into the city for the enemy, and thus Aquileia was taken by storm. So the affairs about Aquileia stood thus.

[29481] 4.36. Later Maximus killed the emperor with no trouble and held the tyranny, and was with Eudoxia by force. For his wife, with whom he had dwelt, had died not long before. And once he addressed a word to her in bed, that for love of her all that he had wrought had been done.

[29758] 4.37. And Eudoxia, being vexed at Maximus before and desiring to punish him for his injustice to Valentinian, the word made her swell against him still more, and led her into the plot, when she heard Maximus saying that the misfortune to her husband befell for her sake.

[30030] 4.38. And as soon as day came, she sends to Carthage, begging Gizeric to avenge Valentinian, who had been destroyed by an unholy man, unworthily of himself and of the kingship, and to rescue her, who was suffering unholy things from the tyrant.

[30276] 4.39. And she charged that, since Gizeric was a friend and ally, with so great a suffering having befallen the imperial house, it was not holy for him not to become an avenger. For she thought no vengeance would come from Byzantium, Theodosius having already vanished from among men and Marcian having received the kingship.

[30602] 5.1. Gizeric, for no other reason than that he suspected great wealth would be his, sailed to Italy with a great fleet. And going up to Rome, since no one stood in his way, he mastered the palace.

[30800] 5.2. Maximus, then, fleeing, the Romans destroyed by stoning, and cutting off his head and each of his other limbs, divided them among themselves.

[30948] 5.3. And Gizeric took Eudoxia captive, together with Eudocia and Placidia, the daughters of her and Valentinian, and putting a great quantity of gold and the other imperial possessions in his ships sailed to Carthage, sparing neither bronze nor anything else in the palace.

[31223] 5.4. He also plundered the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus and took off the half part of its roof. This roof chanced to be of the best bronze, and with thick gold overlaid upon it it shone, exceedingly magnificent and worthy of much wonder.

[31463] 5.5. Of the ships with Gizeric, one, which bore the images, they say was lost, but with all the others the Vandals put in to the harbour of Carthage.

[31614] 5.6. Eudocia, then, Gizeric married to Honoric the elder of his sons, but the other (for she dwelt with a husband, Olybrius, the most notable in the senate of the Romans), together with her mother Eudoxia, the emperor having requested it, he sent to Byzantium.

[31876] 5.7. Already the dominion of the East had come round to Leo, Aspar having set him up to this, when Marcian had departed from among men.

[32013] 5.8. Later Gizeric devised the following. The walls of the cities in Libya, except Carthage, he pulled down, so that neither the Libyans themselves, having chosen the Roman cause, should be able to set out from a strong place and revolt, nor should those sent by the emperor have hope that they would seize a city and, making a garrison in it, cause the Vandals trouble.

[32385] 5.9. At that time, then, he seemed to have taken good counsel and to have preserved the Vandals' good fortune most securely, but at a later time, when, being without walls, these cities were taken by Belisarius more easily and with less toil, Gizeric incurred much laughter, and what seemed for the time to be his good counsel turned to folly for him.

[32738] 5.10. For men are wont ever, according to their fortunes, to change their opinions about their former counsels.

[32851] 5.11. And of the Libyans, whatever chanced to be notable and flourishing in wealth, together with the very lands and all their goods he handed over in the rank of slaves to his sons Honoric and Genzon. For Theodorus the youngest had already died, childless altogether of male and female offspring.

[33150] 5.12. And of the other Libyans he took away the lands, which were most and best, and divided them among the nation of the Vandals, and from this these lands are called the lots of the Vandals to this time.

[33357] 5.13. And to those who had owned these places of old it befell to be exceedingly poor, yet free; and it was in their power to depart wherever they wished.

[33513] 5.14. And all the lands which Gizeric had handed over to his sons and the other Vandals he ordered to be subject to no payment of tribute.

[33653] 5.15. But of the land which seemed to him not good, he left to those who had it before, having appointed so much to be paid thence to the treasury that nothing at all was left over to those who held their own places.

[33871] 5.16. And many fled and were killed. For many and grievous charges were brought against them;

[33966] 5.17. and of all one seemed to be the greatest, that a man, having his own money, concealed it. Thus every kind of calamity encompassed the Libyans.

[34116] 5.18. And the Vandals and Alani, having drawn them up into companies, he set over them company-commanders not fewer than eighty, whom he called chiliarchs, giving an impression that the host of his soldiers came to eight myriads.

[34347] 5.19. And yet the multitude of the Vandals and Alani, at least in the former time, was said to be no more than five myriads.

[34473] 5.20. Afterward, however, both by their own begetting and by making companions of other barbarians, they came to a great population.

[34607] 5.21. And the names of the Alani and the other barbarians, except the Moors, were all merged into that of the Vandals.

[34727] 5.22. At that time Gizeric, having won over the Moors, since Valentinian had died, made inroads each year at the beginning of spring into Sicily and Italy, and enslaving some of the cities, razing others to the ground, and plundering everything, since the country had become empty of men and money, invaded the dominion of the eastern emperor.

[35072] 5.23. He plundered the Illyrians and most of the Peloponnese and the rest of Greece and as many islands as lie near it. And again he landed in Sicily and Italy, and led and carried off everything in turn.

[35278] 5.24. And once, when he had got into his ship in the harbour of Carthage, the sails already being raised, they say the steersman asked him against what men in the world he bade them go.

[35465] 5.25. And he, answering, said, "Plainly against those with whom God is angry." So from no cause he made inroads against whomever it chanced.

[35607] 6.1. For these reasons the Emperor Leo, wishing to punish the Vandals, gathered an army against them; and the multitude of this army is said to have been about ten myriads. And having collected a fleet of ships from all the eastern sea, he displayed great magnanimity toward the soldiers and sailors, fearing lest from pettiness something should hinder him as he was eager to accomplish the chastisement upon the barbarians.

[36033] 6.2. They say, indeed, that thirteen hundred centenaria were spent by him for no result. But since the Vandals were not destined to perish by this fleet, he makes Basiliscus, the brother of his wife Verina and one extraordinarily in love with the kingship, supreme commander of the war—the kingship which he hoped would be his without a battle, if he should win the friendship of Aspar.

[36421] 6.3. For Aspar himself, laying claim to the Arian faith, and not purposing to change it, was not able to come to the kingship himself, but was easily able to set up another in it, and was already reckoned likely to plot, having quarrelled with the Emperor Leo.

[36683] 6.4. They say, then, that Aspar, fearing lest, the Vandals being worsted, Leo should most securely secure the kingship, much charged Basiliscus to spare the Vandals and Gizeric.

[36862] 6.5. And Leo had already before set up Anthemius, a man of senatorial rank, great in wealth and family, as emperor of the West, and sent him, that he might help him in the Vandalic war.

[37049] 6.6. And yet Gizeric begged and much entreated that the kingship be handed over to Olybrius, who dwelt with Placidia the daughter of Valentinian and was well-disposed toward him through the kinship; and when he failed of this, he was still more angry and plundered all the emperor's land.

[37339] 6.7. There was in Dalmatia a certain Marcellianus, one of the acquaintances of Aetius, a man of repute, who, when Aetius had died in the way described, no longer thought fit to yield to the emperor, but, revolting and making all the rest revolt, himself held the dominion of Dalmatia, no one daring to come to blows with him.

[37666] 6.8. This Marcellianus the Emperor Leo, taming him well, won over, and bade him go to the island of Sardo, which was subject to the Vandals. And he, driving out the Vandals, held it without difficulty.

[37869] 6.9. And Heraclius, sent from Byzantium to Tripolis in Libya, having conquered in battle the Vandals there, easily took the cities, and leaving the ships there, led the army on foot to Carthage. So the preludes of the war were carried on thus.

[38114] 6.10. And Basiliscus with the whole fleet sailed to a town distant from Carthage not less than two hundred and eighty stades (a temple of Hermes had chanced to be there from of old, from which the place was called Mercurium; for so the Romans call Hermes), and had he not, playing the coward, delayed, but at once attempted to go to Carthage, he would have taken it at the first shout and enslaved the Vandals, who turned to no defence;

[38552] 6.11. so greatly did Gizeric dread Leo as an invincible emperor, since Sardo and Tripolis were reported to him to be taken, and he saw the fleet of Basiliscus such as no one was ever said to have belonged to the Romans before. But now the delay of the general prevented this, occurring either through cowardice or through treachery.

[38886] 6.12. And Gizeric, profiting by the negligence of Basiliscus, did as follows. Having armed all his subjects as best he could, he filled his ships, and held in readiness others empty of men and sailing as quickly as possible.

[39112] 6.13. And sending envoys to Basiliscus he begged that the war be deferred for a space of five days, in order that in the meanwhile, having deliberated, he might do those things which would be most pleasing to the emperor.

[39335] 6.14. They say, too, that he, secretly sending a great quantity of gold to the army of Basiliscus, bought this armistice.

[39458] 6.15. And he did this thinking, which happened, that a favourable wind would arise for him in this time.

[39564] 6.16. And Basiliscus, either gratifying Aspar as he had undertaken, or selling the opportunity for money, or even thinking it better, did what was asked and remained quiet in the camp, awaiting a good opportunity against the enemy.

[39797] 6.17. And the Vandals, as soon as the wind had come to them, awaiting which they had been sitting, raising their sails and dragging the vessels empty of men, which, as I said before, had been prepared, sailed against the enemy.

[40026] 6.18. And when they came near, putting fire in the vessels which they themselves were dragging and towing, their sails being filled, they let them go against the camp of the Romans.

[40209] 6.19. And, there being a multitude of ships there, wherever these vessels fell, they burned easily, and those they met were readily destroyed together with them.

[40372] 6.20. And as the fire was thus borne on, tumult, as is likely, held the Roman fleet, and a great shout, the wind and the roar of the flame crashing against it; and the soldiers together with the sailors urging one another on and thrusting away with their poles both the fire-bearing vessels and their own ships, which were being destroyed by one another in no order.

[40740] 6.21. And already the Vandals too were present, ramming and sinking, and plundering the soldiers who escaped, arms and all.

[40865] 6.22. There were, however, some brave men of the Romans in this toil, and most of all John, who was sub-general of Basiliscus and had shared in no way at all in his treachery.

[41042] 6.23. For a great throng having surrounded his ship, he killed all about him from the deck a great multitude of the enemy, but when he perceived that the ship was being taken, he leaped with all his panoply of arms from the deck into the sea.

[41286] 6.24. And Genzon the son of Gizeric much entreated him, offering pledges and holding out safety, but he none the less let his body down into the sea, uttering only this, that John would never come under the hands of dogs.

[41509] 6.25. This war, then, came to this end, and Heraclius was conveyed home; for Marcellianus had perished by treachery at the hands of one of his fellow-commanders.

[41672] 6.26. And Basiliscus, coming to Byzantium, sat as a suppliant in the temple of Christ the great God (the Byzantines call the temple Sophia, thinking this name most befits God), and Verina the empress having begged him off, he escaped this danger, but was not able then to come to the kingship, for which all had been wrought by him.

[42006] 6.27. For the Emperor Leo not long after destroyed Aspar and Ardaburius in the palace, since he suspected them of plotting his death. So these things were done thus.

[42173] 7.1. And Anthemius, the emperor of the West, perished, destroyed by his son-in-law Recimer, and Olybrius, receiving the kingship, a little later fulfilled the same destiny.

[42347] 7.2. And Leo too having died in Byzantium, the kingship was received by Leo, son of Zeno and of Ariadne the daughter of Leo, being still of an age of a few days.

[42510] 7.3. And when his father had been chosen co-emperor with him, very soon the child vanished from among men.

[42618] 7.4. And it is worth mentioning Majorinus too, who before held the dominion of the West. For this Majorinus, surpassing in all virtue all who had ever reigned over the Romans, did not bear the suffering of Libya gently, but, having gathered a most considerable army against the Vandals, came to Liguria, purposing himself to lead the army against the enemy.

[42977] 7.5. For Majorinus was quite without hesitation toward toils and not least toward dangers.

[43069] 7.6. And thinking it not inexpedient for him first to investigate the power of the Vandals and the character of Gizeric, and where in the world the Moors and the Libyans stood in goodwill or hatred toward them, he resolved to trust such matters to no other than his own eyes.

[43346] 7.7. So as an envoy, ostensibly from the emperor, he was sent to Gizeric, bringing another name fabricated for himself. And fearing lest, having become recognized, he should both suffer some harm himself and hinder his designs, he devised the following.

[43601] 7.8. The hairs of his head (for they were famous among all men, since they were so fair as to be likened to pure gold), anointing them with a certain dye invented for this very purpose, he was able for the occasion to change altogether to dark.

[43847] 7.9. And when he came into the presence of Gizeric, Gizeric set about frightening him in various ways, and as though leading him on as a friend went to the building where all the arms were stored, being many and exceedingly worthy of account.

[44091] 7.10. There, they say, the arms, moving of their own accord, gave off a clatter neither moderate nor ordinary, and then it seemed to Gizeric that there had been an earthquake; but when he had gone out and inquired about the earthquake, since none of the rest agreed, a great wonder fell upon him, yet he was not able to infer what had happened.

[44437] 7.11. Majorinus, then, having accomplished what he wished, was conveyed back to Liguria, and leading the army on foot went to the Pillars of Heracles, purposing to cross the strait there and to go for the rest by the road to Carthage.

[44673] 7.12. When Gizeric perceived this, and that he had been deceived by Majorinus in the embassy, he fell into dread and prepared the matters of war.

[44820] 7.13. And the Romans, inferring from the virtue of Majorinus, were already of good hope to recover Libya for the realm.

[44941] 7.14. But in the meanwhile Majorinus, seized by a disease of dysentery, was destroyed, a man moderate toward his subjects but terrible toward his enemies.

[45097] 7.15. And Nepos, having received the kingship and surviving a few days, died of disease, and Glycerius after him, advancing to this rule, fulfilled the same fortune. After whom Augustus received the supreme rule.

[45311] 7.16. There were, however, other emperors before in the West, whose names, though I know them, I shall by no means mention.

[45436] 7.17. For it chanced that they survived in their rule but a short time and from this did nothing worthy of account. These things took place in the West.

[45590] 7.18. And in Byzantium, Basiliscus (for he was no longer able to force the love of the kingship) set himself to a tyranny and mastered it with no trouble, Zeno together with his wife having fled to Isauria, from which he had come.

[45822] 7.19. And while he held the tyranny a year and eight months, all the rest, so to speak, and the soldiers of the court were vexed by the magnitude of his love of money.

[45991] 7.20. When Zeno perceived this, having gathered an army he went against him. And Basiliscus sent an army and the general Harmatus to oppose Zeno.

[46138] 7.21. But when they encamped near one another, Harmatus handed over his army to Zeno, on condition that he should appoint Basiliscus his son, who was quite a young child, Caesar and leave him at his death as successor to the kingship.

[46374] 7.22. And Basiliscus, having become bereft of all, fled to the temple where he had before. And Acacius, the priest of the city, gave him into the hands of Zeno, charging him with impiety and that he had much confounded and innovated upon the doctrine of the Christians, inclining to the heresy of Eutyches. And so it was.

[46697] 7.23. And Zeno, having again received the kingship and disavowing his faith toward Harmatus, having appointed Basiliscus his son Caesar, not long after took away the honour from him and killed Harmatus.

[46901] 7.24. And Basiliscus, together with his sons and his wife, he sent to Cappadocia in the winter season, and bade them be without food and clothing and other care.

[47064] 7.25. There, pressed by cold and hunger, they took refuge with one another, and embracing their dearest persons perished. This requital overtook Basiliscus for his deeds. But these things took place at a later time.

[47281] 7.26. And Gizeric then, having got round by deceit and driven out by force, as said before, the enemy, none the less, if not more, led and carried off all the Romans' goods, until the Emperor Zeno came to an agreement with him, and an endless treaty was concluded between them, that neither should the Vandals do anything hostile to the Romans for all time, nor should anything befall them from these. This treaty Zeno himself preserved, and he who received the kingship after him, Anastasius.

[47776] 7.27. And they remained even to the Emperor Justin. And Justinian, being nephew of this Justin, succeeded to the kingship.

[47900] 7.28. In the reign of this Justinian this war arose, in the manner that shall be told in the books to follow.

[48011] 7.29. And Gizeric, surviving a short time, died, having come already far on in age, having made a will in which he charged the Vandals with many things, and that the kingship of the Vandals should ever come to him who, being related by descent to Gizeric himself by male offspring, should chance to be first of all his kinsmen in age.

[48347] 7.30. Gizeric, then, having ruled the Vandals from the time he mastered Carthage thirty-nine years, died, as has been said.

[48472] 8.1. And Honoric, the eldest of his sons, succeeded to the rule, Genzon having already vanished from among men. In the reign of this Honoric over the Vandals, no war befell them with any men, except against the Moors.

[48691] 8.2. For the Moors, quiet before through fear of Gizeric, as soon as he was out of their way, both wrought many evils upon the Vandals and themselves suffered.

[48852] 8.3. And Honoric became toward the Christians in Libya the most savage and most unjust of all men.

[48952] 8.4. For forcing them to change to the Arian faith, as many as he caught not readily yielding to him, he burned and destroyed by other forms of death, and of many he cut off the tongues from the very throat, who, surviving even to my time in Byzantium, used pure speech, in no way perceiving this punishment; of whom two, when they resolved to consort with prostitute women,

[49328] 8.5. were no longer able for the rest to speak. And having ruled the Vandals eight years, he died of disease, the Moors dwelling in the Aurasium mountain having already revolted from the Vandals and being independent (the Aurasium is in Numidia, distant about thirteen days' journey from Carthage and turned toward the south), who were no longer under the Vandals, the Vandals being unable in a mountain difficult of access and very steep to carry on war against the Moors.

[49803] 8.6. When Honoric died, the dominion of the Vandals came to Gundamundus, son of Genzon, son of Gizeric. For to him the time brought the first place of Gizeric's family.

[49973] 8.7. This Gundamundus fought against the Moors in more engagements, and subjecting the Christians to greater sufferings, died of disease, already about the middle of the twelfth year of his rule.

[50170] 8.8. And his brother Trasamundus received the kingship, being exceedingly well endowed with appearance and intelligence and magnanimity.

[50308] 8.9. The Christians, however, he forced to change their ancestral faith, not torturing their bodies like the former ones, but pursuing them with honours and offices and gifting them with great wealth, and taking least account of those who disobeyed, of whatever sort they were.

[50587] 8.10. And if he caught any guilty of great offences or held by birth, to these, changing their faith, he set forth as a reward not to pay the penalty of what they had done wrong.

[50767] 8.11. And when his wife died, having become mother of neither male nor female offspring, wishing to secure his kingship most excellently, he sent to Theoderic the king of the Goths and asked him to give him as wife his sister Amalafrida, whose husband had just died.

[51035] 8.12. And he both sent his sister and a thousand notable Goths in the rank of bodyguards, whom a throng of attendants followed, amounting to about five thousand warlike men.

[51210] 8.13. And Theoderic gifted his sister with one of the three promontories of Sicily, which they call Lilybaeum, and from this Trasamundus seemed both to be mightier and the most powerful of all who had led the Vandals.

[51429] 8.14. And he became a very great friend of the Emperor Anastasius too. In his reign it befell the Vandals to suffer a thing from the Moors such as had never before come to pass.

[51608] 8.15. There was a certain Cabaon, ruler of the Moors about Tripolis, experienced in many wars and exceedingly quick-witted. This Cabaon, when he learned that the Vandals were marching against him, did as follows.

[51822] 8.16. First he charged his subjects to abstain from all injustice and from food leading to luxury and most of all from intercourse with women; and pitching two stockades, in the one he himself encamped with all the men, and in the other he shut up the women, and threatened that death would be the penalty if anyone should go to the stockade of the women.

[52179] 8.17. After this, sending scouts to Carthage, he charged them as follows: whenever the Vandals, marching to the campaign, should outrage any temple which the Christians worship, they should look on at what was being done; but when the Vandals should leave the place, they should do everything the opposite to the sanctuary of what those men had done before they departed.

[52552] 8.18. And they say he added this too, that he did not know the God whom the Christians worship, but it was likely that He, if He is strong, as is said, would both punish those who outrage and defend those who serve Him.

[52773] 8.19. The scouts, then, coming to Carthage, remained quiet, watching the preparation of the Vandals; but when the army went toward Tripolis, clad in lowly garb they followed.

[52949] 8.20. And the Vandals, when on the first day they bivouacked, leading their horses and other animals into the temples of the Christians, refrained from no outrage and used their own licentiousness, and the priests they caught they beat and, scourging them many times over the back, bade them serve them in whatever the most dishonoured of servants were wont to attend to.

[53322] 8.21. And as soon as they departed thence, the scouts of Cabaon did what had been enjoined upon them; for they at once cleansed the sanctuaries, taking away with much care the dung and whatever else lay there unholily, and lit all the lamps, and did obeisance to the priests with much reverence and greeted them with all other friendliness;

[53664] 8.22. and giving silver to the poor who sat about these sanctuaries, thus they followed the army of the Vandals.

[53778] 8.23. And from this, along the whole road, the Vandals did the same wrong and the scouts performed the same service.

[53896] 8.24. And when they were about to be near, the scouts, going ahead, announced to Cabaon all that had been wrought by the Vandals and by themselves toward the sanctuaries of the Christians, and that the enemy were somewhere near.

[54126] 8.25. And he, hearing this, set himself to the engagement as follows. Marking off a circle in the plain where he was about to make his stockade, he set the camels crosswise as a defence to the camp in a circle, making the front about twelve camels deep.

[54381] 8.26. The children and women, then, and whatever was unfit for fighting among them, together with the goods, he placed in the middle, and bade the warlike host be in the middle of the feet of those animals, fenced with their shields.

[54616] 8.27. The phalanx of the Moors being thus, the Vandals were at a loss how to deal with the present. For they were neither good javelin-throwers nor archers, nor knew how to go into battle on foot, but were all horsemen and used for the most part spears and swords, and from this were able to do the enemy no harm at a distance, and their horses, distressed at the sight of the camels, were by no means led against the enemy.

[55042] 8.28. And since the enemy, throwing javelins thickly at them from safety, killed both the horses and the men, being a multitude, without difficulty, they fled, and when the Moors came out against them, the most were destroyed, and there are some who came under the enemy, and only quite a few from this army were conveyed home.

[55371] 8.29. These things it befell Trasamundus to suffer from the Moors. And he died at a later time, having ruled the Vandals twenty-seven years.

[55513] 9.1. And Ilderic, son of Honoric, son of Gizeric, received the kingship, who toward his subjects was both approachable and altogether gentle, and had become harsh neither to the Christians nor to anyone else, but in matters of war was exceedingly soft and would not even let the thing come as far as his ears.

[55824] 9.2. Hoamer, then, being his nephew and a man good in war, was general against whomever the Vandals made war; and indeed they called him the Achilles of the Vandals.

[55991] 9.3. In the reign of this Ilderic the Vandals were worsted in battle by the Moors in Byzacium, whom Antalas ruled, and it befell them to become enemies of Theoderic and the Goths in Italy, from allies and friends.

[56206] 9.4. For they held Amalafrida in custody and destroyed all the Goths, bringing against them the charge of attempting a revolution against the Vandals and Ilderic.

[56370] 9.5. No vengeance, however, came from Theoderic, since he thought himself unable to make an expedition with a great fleet to Libya; and Ilderic became an exceeding friend and guest-friend of Justinian, who had not yet come to the kingship but was administering it at his pleasure, since his uncle Justin reigned, being very old and not altogether experienced in the affairs of the state. And they gifted one another with great wealth.

[56806] 9.6. There was in the family of Gizeric a certain Gelimer, son of Geilaris, son of Genzon, son of Gizeric, far on in age, after Ilderic, and on this account reckoned likely to come very soon to the kingship;

[57015] 9.7. who in war seemed to be the best of his time, but otherwise was clever and ill-disposed and knew how to set himself to revolution and to others' money.

[57173] 9.8. This Gelimer, when he saw the rule about to be his, was not able to live in the established manner, but, appropriating the deeds of king, laid claim to the honour, though it was not yet due to him; and Ilderic through fairness yielding, he was no longer able to restrain his mind, but winning over whatever was best among the Vandals, persuaded them to take the kingship from Ilderic, as unwarlike and worsted by the Moors and betraying the dominion of the Vandals to the Emperor Justin, that the kingship might not come to him, who was of the other house; for this, he slanderously said, was the purpose of the embassy to Byzantium, and that Ilderic was handing over the Vandals' dominion to him. And they, persuaded, did accordingly.

[57915] 9.9. Thus, then, Gelimer, seizing the leadership, held Ilderic, who had ruled the Vandals seven years, and Hoamer and his brother Euages, in custody.

[58066] 9.10. When Justinian heard this, having already received the kingship, sending envoys to Libya to Gelimer, he wrote as follows: "You do unholy things, and unworthy of the will of Gizeric, holding in custody an old man and a kinsman and king of the Vandals, if there is any profit in what Gizeric counselled, and taking the rule from him by force, when it was in your power to receive it a little later by law.

[58477] 9.11. Do no further harm, then, nor exchange the name of king for the appellation of tyrant, which precedes by a short time.

[58603] 9.12. But let this man, who is all but dead, carry off in word the image of the kingship, while you do all that a king is likely to do; and await receiving only the name of the thing from time and from Gizeric's law.

[58821] 9.13. For doing these things both the favour of the Higher Power will be kindly to you, and ours friendly."

[58930] 9.14. So much the writing declared. But Gelimer sent the envoys away unsuccessful, and blinded Hoamer and put Ilderic and Euages in stricter custody, charging them with planning flight to Byzantium.

[59130] 9.15. When the Emperor Justinian heard this too, sending other envoys he wrote as follows: "We, thinking that you would never come against our counsel, wrote you the former letter.

[59312] 9.16. But since it pleases you to hold the kingship as you have now received it, take what the fate from it may give.

[89518] 15.4. And yet I know that to propose anything to those set in danger brings no private profit to the one who advises, but for the most part turns to blame.

[89675] 15.5. For men, when they fare well, refer what is done to their own judgement or fortune, but when they stumble, blame only the one who advised.

[89821] 15.6. Yet it shall be said; for it is not holy for those deliberating about safety to shrink from blame.

[89927] 15.7. You purpose to disembark into the enemy's land, O commanders; in what harbour are you thinking to deposit the ships? Or with the wall of what city will you have safety for yourselves?

[90118] 15.8. Or have you not heard that this shore, I mean from Carthage to Iouce, they say reaches a journey of nine days, being altogether harbourless and exposed to the winds from whatever quarter they may blow?

[90327] 15.9. And there is no wall left in all Libya except that of Carthage,

[90398] 15.10. Gizeric having counselled this. And one might add that this place too, they say, altogether lacks water. Come, then, if it please, let us make the decision having supposed even some of the contrary things.

[90612] 15.11. For those entering into contests must expect nothing untoward beyond what is human or worthy of the nature of things.

[90738] 15.12. If, then, when we have disembarked onto the mainland, a storm should fall upon us, is it not necessary that one of two things befall the ships, either to flee very far off, or to perish on this shore?

[90947] 15.13. Then what provision of necessaries will there be for us? Let no one look to me, the prefect, the paymaster of the expense; for an office deprived of all its service must be reduced to the name and person of a private man.

[91177] 15.14. And where, having deposited the superfluous arms or any other of the necessaries, shall we have to receive the barbarian as he comes on? But these things it is not even good to say how they will turn out.

[91390] 15.15. I hold that we ought to go straight to Carthage. For they say there is a harbour distant not more than forty stades from it, which they call Stagnum, altogether unguarded and well able to hold the whole fleet. Setting out from there it will not be hard for us to carry the war.

[91676] 15.16. And I think it likely that we shall master Carthage by a sudden raid, especially as the enemy are left far from it, and having mastered it nothing for the rest will be troublesome.

[91865] 15.17. For all human things, the chief points being seized, are wont not long after to fall away. Considering all of which it befits us to choose the best." So much Archelaus said.

[92047] 15.18. And Belisarius spoke as follows: "Let none of you, fellow-commanders, think the words to be those of a judge, nor on this account spoken last, so that to follow them, of whatever sort they may be, becomes necessary for all.

[92279] 15.19. For whatever seems best to each of you I have heard; and it befits me too, having brought out into the midst what I know, thus with you to choose the better.

[92445] 15.20. And it is worth reminding you of this, that a little before the soldiers openly said they feared the dangers on the sea and would turn to flight, should a hostile ship come against them, while we begged God to show us the land of Libya and a peaceful disembarkation onto it.

[92728] 15.21. And these things being so, I think it the part of unintelligent men to pray to God to receive the better, but, when they are given, to shake them off and to go against them.

[92910] 15.22. And if, as we sail straight to Carthage, a hostile fleet should meet us, for the soldiers fleeing at full force there will be left the not being blamed; for a fault foretold carries its own defence; but for us, even if saved, there is no forgiveness.

[93169] 15.23. And there being many difficulties if we remain in the ships, this alone, I think, will be sufficiently said, by which most of all they think fit to frighten us, brandishing the storm.

[93361] 15.24. For if a storm should fall upon us, it is necessary, as they say, that one of two things befall the ships, either to flee far off from Libya, or to perish on this shore.

[93539] 15.25. Which, then, as from the present, will profit us more in our choice? That the ships alone be destroyed, or that all our affairs perish, men and all? And apart from this, now, falling upon the enemy unprepared, we shall come off according to our wish, as is likely; for the things of war are wont to be enslaved by the unexpected;

[93877] 15.26. but a little later, the enemy being now in readiness, the contest will be for us on equal terms of power.

[93991] 15.27. And one might add that perhaps we shall have to fight even for the disembarkation itself, and to seek those things which, now present to us, come into counsel as not necessary.

[94176] 15.28. And if a storm should then come upon us as we fight, as for the most part is wont to happen on the sea, fighting against both the waves and the Vandals, we shall know we have learned good counsel too late.

[94390] 15.29. I say, then, that we ought to disembark onto the mainland very soon, disembarking horses and arms and whatever else we think necessary for our need, and quickly to dig a trench and throw up a stockade, no less able to accomplish safety for us than whatever wall one might name, and from there, setting out, to carry the war,

[94723] 15.30. should anyone come on. And nothing of provisions will fail us if we are brave men. For to those who master the enemy it befalls to be masters of what belongs to those, and victory, encompassing all goods, wherever it inclines, is wont to deposit them. So that for you both safety and the abundance of goods lie in the hands."

[95057] 15.31. When Belisarius had said this, the whole assembly agreed and accepted it, and breaking up they made the disembarkation as quickly as possible, about three months after their departure from Byzantium.

[95265] 15.32. And the general, pointing out a place on the shore, bade the soldiers and sailors both dig the trench and throw up the stockade.

[95402] 15.33. And they did accordingly. And a great throng working, fear rousing their eagerness and the general urging them on, on that very day the trench was dug and the stockade completed and the palisade fixed in a circle on every side.

[95638] 15.34. There a marvellous thing befell those digging the trench. A great quantity of water the earth sent up, this not having happened in Byzacium before, especially as the place was waterless.

[95833] 15.35. This water sufficed the men and the other animals for every need. And Procopius, rejoicing with the general, said that he was glad not because of the need from the abundance of water, but because it seemed to him to be a token of an effortless victory and that the divine power foretold this to them.

[96142] 15.36. Which indeed came to pass. That night, then, all the soldiers bivouacked in the camp, making guards and the rest as was wont, except that Belisarius bade five archers remain in each ship for guard, and the dromons to moor in a circle about them, guarding lest anyone should come to do them mischief.

[96450] 16.1. On the next day, when some of the soldiers, going up into the fields, laid hands on the produce, the general both chastised their bodies not lightly and, calling all together, spoke as follows:

[96651] 16.2. "To use violence and feed on others' goods seems in other times to be base only in this, that it carries injustice in itself; but now there is so great a difficulty in it that, not to speak bitterly, holding the account of justice of less account, we must reckon how great the danger from it is.

[96954] 16.3. For I disembarked you onto this land having confidence in that alone, that the Libyans, being Romans from of old, are faithless to the Vandals and hard toward them, and on this account I thought that neither would anything of necessaries fail us nor would the enemy work us any harm by a raid.

[97255] 16.4. But now this lack of self-control of yours has changed these things to the opposite for us. For you have reconciled the Libyans, no doubt, to the Vandals, having now turned their ill-will against yourselves.

[97470] 16.5. For by nature there belongs to those who are wronged hatred toward those who use violence, and it has come about for you to exchange your own safety and the abundance of good things for a few pieces of silver, when it was in your power, buying provisions from willing owners, both not to seem unjust and to use them as exceeding friends.

[97815] 16.6. Now, then, your war will be against both the Vandals and the Libyans, and I say even against God himself, whom no one doing wrong calls to his aid.

[97970] 16.7. But cease leaping upon others' goods, and shake off gain full of dangers.

[98051] 16.8. For this is that occasion in which prudence is most able to save, but disorder carries to death. For if you take care of these things, God will be propitious, the people of the Libyans well-disposed, and the nation of the Vandals easy to attack."

[98305] 16.9. Having said so much, Belisarius broke up the assembly, and when he heard that the city of Syllectum, distant a day's journey from the camp, was on the sea, on the road leading to Carthage, whose wall chanced to have been pulled down of old, but the inhabitants, blocking up the walls of the houses on all sides because of the inroads of the Moors, kept the form of a wall, he sent one of the bodyguards, Boriades, together with some of the shield-bearers, charging them to make trial of the city, and, if they should take it, to do no harm in it, but to promise countless good things, and that they had come for their freedom, so that the army might have entrance into it.

[98985] 16.10. And they, coming near the city about lamp-lighting and hiding in a glen, passed the night. And at dawn, chancing upon country folk with wagons going into it, they entered in silence with them and held the city with no trouble.

[99220] 16.11. And when day came, no tumult having preceded, calling together the priest and whatever was notable, they reported the commands of the general, and receiving from willing hands the keys of the entrances, sent them to the general.

[99457] 16.12. On the same day the overseer of the public post too deserted, handing over all the public horses. And one of those ever sent for the imperial responses, whom they call veredarii, being seized, the general did him no harm, but gifting him with much gold and receiving pledges, put into his hands the letters which the Emperor Justinian had written to the Vandals, that he should give them to the commanders of the Vandals.

[99887] 16.13. And the writing declared as follows: "We have neither resolved to make war on the Vandals nor do we break the treaty of Gizeric, but we are attempting to put down your tyrant, who, holding the will of Gizeric of small account, holds your king shut up in custody, and of his kinsmen those whom he greatly hated at the beginning he killed, and the rest, having taken away their eyes, he holds in custody, not allowing them to end their misfortunes by death.

[100351] 16.14. Help us, then, and free yourselves together from so base a tyranny, that you may be able to enjoy peace and freedom. For these things, we give you pledges before God that they shall be yours from us."

[100560] 16.15. So much the emperor's letter declared. But he who received this from Belisarius did not dare to bring it into the open, and showing it secretly to his friends accomplished nothing worth account.

[100763] 17.1. And Belisarius, having arrayed the army thus as for battle, marched toward Carthage. Choosing out three hundred of the shield-bearers, men good in war, he handed them to John, who managed for him the expense of his household; the Romans call this man an optio.

[101031] 17.2. He was a man Armenian by race, but come to the highest pitch of intelligence and manliness. This John he bade lead the army, distant not less than twenty stades, and if he should see anything hostile, to signal it quickly, so that they should not be forced to enter battle unprepared.

[101323] 17.3. And the allied Massagetae he bade ever make their journey distant by so many or even more stades on the left side; and he himself marched behind with the best men.

[101494] 17.4. For he suspected that Gelimer, following them from Hermione, would not long after attack them. For on the right side there was no fear, as they journeyed not far from the shore.

[101679] 17.5. And to the sailors he charged ever to keep pace and not to be far separated from the army, but, when the wind was favourable, lowering the great sails to follow with the small ones, which they call dolones, and, when the wind altogether ceased, to force their way as best they could by rowing.

[101980] 17.6. And coming to Syllectum, Belisarius kept the soldiers prudent, neither beginning unjust hands nor doing anything out of place, and himself displaying gentleness and much humanity, so won over the Libyans that for the rest he made the journey as in his own land, the inhabitants neither withdrawing nor wishing to hide anything, but both providing a market and serving the soldiers in the rest as they wished.

[102396] 17.7. Accomplishing eighty stades each day, we proceeded all the way to Carthage, either in a city, should it so chance, or in a camp, bivouacking as safely as we could from the present.

[102584] 17.8. Thus, then, through the city of Leptis and Adrumetum we came to the place Grasse, distant from Carthage three hundred and fifty stades.

[102727] 17.9. There was a palace of the leader of the Vandals and a park the fairest of all we know.

[102821] 17.10. For it is exceedingly watered by springs and has a great abundance of grove. And the trees are all full of fruit; so that each of the soldiers pitched his hut among the fruit-bearing trees, and, the fruits being then in season, all came to satiety of them, yet no perception of the fruit being diminished was made.

[103144] 17.11. And Gelimer, when he first heard at Hermione that the enemy were present, wrote to his brother Ammatas in Carthage to kill Ilderic and the others whom, whether by birth or otherwise belonging to him, he held in custody, and himself to put the Vandals and whatever else was warlike in the city in readiness, so that, the enemy being in narrows about the suburb of the city, which they call Decimum, coming together from both sides they might encircle and, netting, destroy them.

[103630] 17.12. And Ammatas did accordingly, and killed Ilderic, who was a kinsman, and Euages, and as many of the Libyans as were friendly to them.

[103771] 17.13. For Hoamer had already vanished from among men. And having armed the Vandals, as though to attack at the right moment,

[103898] 17.14. he held them in readiness. And Gelimer followed behind, giving us no perception, except that in the very night when we bivouacked at Grasse, scouts going from both sides met, and striking one another both withdrew to their own camp, and by this it became plain to us that the enemy were not far off.

[104206] 17.15. As we journeyed thence it was impossible to see the ships. For high rocks, reaching far from the sea, make a great circuit for those sailing, and a promontory rises, within which is the town of Hermes.

[104608] 17.17. And rising up from Grasse, on the fourth day we came to Decimum, distant seventy stades from Carthage.

And in this toil it came to me to marvel at the things both divine and human, how God, seeing from afar what was to be, sketches how matters seem to Him to turn out, while men, whether erring or counselling rightly, do not know that they have stumbled, should it so chance, or done rightly, in order that there may be a path for fortune, bearing altogether toward what was before resolved.

[106576] 18.8. For they journeyed in no order and not arrayed as for battle, but in companies, and these small; for they went in thirties or twenties.

[106719] 18.9. And seeing the Vandals under Ammatas fleeing, and thinking the pursuers to be a multitude, they turned their backs and fled together with them.
