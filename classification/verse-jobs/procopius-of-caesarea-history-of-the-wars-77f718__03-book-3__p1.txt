[110890] 19.13. Sending ahead, then, the commanders of the federates, with the rest of the army and his own bodyguards and shield-bearers he himself followed.

[111041] 19.14. And when the federates with their leaders came to Decimum, they see the bodies of the fallen, twelve comrades of those with John, and near them Ammatas and some of the Vandals.

[111226] 19.15. And hearing the whole story from the inhabitants, they were distressed, at a loss where they should go. And while they were still at a loss and scanning all the places there from the hills, a dust appeared from the south, and a little later a very great quantity of Vandal horsemen.

[111517] 19.16. And they sent to Belisarius, asking him to come as quickly as possible, since the enemy were pressing them. And the opinions of the commanders were divided.

[111682] 19.17. For some thought fit to go to close quarters with the assailants, while others said their force was not adequate for this.

[111813] 19.18. And while they were disputing among themselves about these things, the barbarians came near, Gelimer leading them and using a road between that which Belisarius had and that by which the Massagetae had come, who joined with Gibamundus.

[112057] 19.19. And hilly places being on either side did not allow him to see either the disaster of Gibamundus or the stockade of Belisarius, nor indeed the road by which those under Belisarius journeyed.

[112256] 19.20. And when they were near one another, strife fell upon each army, which should master the highest of the hills there.

[112381] 19.21. For it seemed suitable for encamping, and both alike chose to come to blows with the enemy from there.

[112492] 19.22. And the Vandals, anticipating, seize the hill, using a push, and rout the enemy, having now become terrible to them.

[112617] 19.23. And the Romans, fleeing, come to a place distant seven stades from Decimum, where it chanced Uliaris, the bodyguard of Belisarius, was with eight hundred shield-bearers.

[112795] 19.24. And all thought that those under Uliaris, receiving them, would stand and together with them go to close quarters with the Vandals; but when they came together, beyond expectation now all fled at full force and went at a run toward Belisarius.

[113047] 19.25. From here I cannot say what befell Gelimer, that, holding the mastery of the war in his hands, he of his own will gave it up to the enemy, unless one must refer the things of his folly to God too, who, when He purposes that something untoward befall a man, first lays hold of his reckonings and does not allow what would profit to come into counsel.

[113405] 19.26. For had he made the pursuit straightway, I think Belisarius himself would not have withstood him, but all our affairs would have been utterly destroyed;

[113566] 19.27. so great did the multitude of the Vandals appear, and their fear of the Romans; and had he driven straight to Carthage, he would easily have killed all those with John, who, in ones and twos, careless and making walks in the plain, were stripping the fallen.

[113833] 19.28. And having saved the city with the money, and the ships of ours having come not far off, he would have mastered them, and would have removed all hope from us of return and of victory. But he did neither of these.

[114054] 19.29. Coming down at a walk from the hill, when he was on the level ground and saw the body of his brother, he turned to lamentations, and being concerned about the burial, thus blunted the edge of the moment, which he was no longer able to recover.

[114306] 19.30. And Belisarius, meeting the fugitives, bids them halt, and arranging them all in order and reproaching them much, when he had heard of the death of Ammatas and the pursuit of John, and had learned about the place and the enemy what he wished, went at a run against Gelimer and the Vandals.

[114604] 19.31. And the barbarians, having now become disordered and unprepared, did not withstand them as they came on, but fled at full force, losing many there,

[114760] 19.32. and the battle ended at night. And the Vandals fled, not to Carthage nor to Byzacium, whence they had come, but to the Plain of Boulla and the road leading to the Numidians.

[114942] 19.33. And those under John and the Massagetae, turning back to us about lamp-lighting and learning and reporting all that had befallen, passed the night with us at Decimum.

[115117] 20.1. On the next day, the foot together with the wife of Belisarius having joined us, we all proceeded toward Carthage, where, coming about late afternoon, we bivouacked, although no one prevented us from at once riding into the city. For the Carthaginians, flinging open the gates, lit lamps all together, and the city was bright with fire all that night, and the Vandals who had been left behind sat as suppliants in the sanctuaries.

[115555] 20.2. But Belisarius prevented it, that there might be no ambush against them from the enemy, and that the soldiers might have no license, in the dark unobserved, to turn to plunder.

[115739] 20.3. On this day the ships, a wind of the East having fallen upon them, came to the promontory. And the Carthaginians, for they now saw them, taking away the iron chains of the harbour, which they call Mandracium, made entrance for the fleet.

[115984] 20.4. And there is in the emperor's court a building full of darkness, which the Carthaginians call Ancon, where all were thrown with whom the tyrant was angry.

[116146] 20.5. There were shut up many of the eastern merchants at that time.

[116216] 20.6. For Gelimer was harsh with these, charging that they had led the emperor into the war, and they were all about to be destroyed, this having seemed best to Gelimer on that day when Ammatas died at Decimum; by so much of a danger they came.

[116462] 20.7. The keeper of this prison, when he heard what had been done at Decimum and saw the fleet within the promontory, going into the building inquired of the men, who had not yet learned the good news, but sat in the dark awaiting death, what they would wish if, giving up their possessions, they should be saved.

[116777] 20.8. And when they chose to give whatever he wished, he asked nothing of all their money, but begged them all to swear that, if they should escape, they would help him with all their power, should he come into dangers. And they did accordingly.

[117024] 20.9. And he told the whole story and, taking off a board from the part toward the sea, showed the fleet approaching, and releasing all from the prison went out with them.

[117197] 20.10. And those in the ships, not yet having heard what the army had done on land, were at a loss, and lowering the sails and sending to Mercurium learned what had been done at Decimum, and being overjoyed sailed.

[117413] 20.11. And when, the wind being favourable for them, they were a hundred and fifty stades from Carthage, Archelaus and his soldiers bade them moor, fearing the general's instruction, but the sailors did not obey.

[117627] 20.12. For they said that the shore there was harbourless and that a notable storm was very soon reckoned likely to come, which the natives call Cypriana.

[117783] 20.13. And they foretold that, if it should come upon them there, they would not be able to save even one of the ships. And so it was.

[117919] 20.14. In a short while, then, lowering the sails and deliberating, they thought they ought not to make trial of Mandracium

[118044] 20.15. (for they shrank from the instructions of Belisarius and at the same time suspected that the entrance into Mandracium was shut off by the chains, especially as that harbour was not adequate for the whole fleet), but Stagnum seemed to them to lie well (for it is distant from Carthage a measure of forty stades) and to have no obstacle in it and to be well able to hold the whole fleet.

[118438] 20.16. There, coming about lamp-lighting, they all moored, except that Calonymus with some of the sailors, taking no thought of the general and of all the rest, secretly disembarked at Mandracium, no one daring to prevent him, and plundered the goods of the merchants dwelling by the sea, both foreigners and Carthaginians.

[118763] 20.17. On the following day Belisarius bade those in the ships make the disembarkation, and arranging the whole army and ordering it as for battle, rode into Carthage; for he feared lest some ambush of the enemy should meet him.

[118993] 20.18. There he much reminded the soldiers how great strokes of good fortune had come to them since they displayed prudence toward the Libyans, and much exhorted them to preserve good order in Carthage most of all.

[119209] 20.19. For all the Libyans, being Romans from of old, had become subject to the Vandals against their will and had suffered many unholy things from barbarian men.

[119373] 20.20. And on this account the emperor had set himself to war against the Vandals, and it was not holy for anything untoward to befall from them men whose freedom they had made the pretext of their expedition against the Vandals.

[119604] 20.21. Having exhorted so much, he entered Carthage, and since nothing hostile appeared to them, going up to the Palatium he sat on the throne of Gelimer.

[119760] 20.22. There, meeting Belisarius with much shouting, a multitude of merchants and other Carthaginians whose houses chanced to be by the sea charged that plunder of their possessions had befallen them on the previous night at the hands of the sailors.

[120012] 20.23. And he bound Calonymus with oaths surely to bring to light all the stolen goods.

[120101] 20.24. And Calonymus, having sworn and holding what he had sworn of no account, for the moment plundered the money, but not long after paid the penalty in Byzantium.

[120268] 20.25. For seized by the disease called apoplexy and going out of his mind and biting off his own tongue, he then died. But these things took place at a later time.

[120434] 21.1. Then Belisarius, since the occasion led to it, bade a breakfast be made for them where Gelimer was wont to feast the leaders of the Vandals.

[120582] 21.2. The Romans call the place Delphix, not in their own tongue, but hellenizing it according to the old fashion. For in the Palatium in Rome, where it chanced the king's couches were, a tripod stood from of old, on which the king's cup-bearers used to set the cups.

[120851] 21.3. And the tripod the Romans call Delphix, since it first came to be in Delphi, and from this both in Byzantium and wherever the king's couch chances to be they call the building Delphix, since the Romans, hellenizing, call the king's house Palatium too.

[121110] 21.4. For Pallas, a Greek man, having dwelt in this place before the capture of Ilium and built a house worthy of account there, they called this building Palatium, and since Augustus, having received the supreme rule, resolved first to lodge there, they call from him the place where the king lodges Palatium.

[121422] 21.5. In the Delphix, then, Belisarius dined, and whatever was notable in the army.

[121507] 21.6. And it chanced that the breakfast made on the previous day for Gelimer was in readiness. And we feasted on the very victuals, and the attendants of Gelimer set them before us and poured the wine and served us in the rest.

[121736] 21.7. And it was possible to see Fortune adorning herself and making display that all things are hers and nothing becomes the property of man.

[121880] 21.8. And it befell Belisarius on that day to win such repute as had never come to any of his time nor to any other of those of old.

[122014] 21.9. For though the Roman soldiers were not wont, without tumult, to go into a city subject to them, not even by five hundreds and especially unexpectedly, this general so kept all his subjects orderly that there was neither outrage nor threat,

[122261] 21.10. nor indeed any hindrance to the business of the city, but in a captured city which had changed its government and exchanged its kingship, not even of those in the market was the house of anyone shut, but the clerks, writing the billets, brought the soldiers into the houses, as was wont, and they, buying their breakfast from the market as each wished, remained quiet.

[122638] 21.11. After this Belisarius both gave pledges to the Vandals who had fled into the sanctuaries and took care of the walls. For the wall of Carthage was so neglected that it had become accessible in many places and easy to attack for one wishing.

[122886] 21.12. For a not small portion of it had fallen down, and on this account the Carthaginians said that Gelimer had not withstood in the city.

[123028] 21.13. For he never thought it possible in a short time to recover the safety of this wall.

[123121] 21.14. And they told a certain ancient saying that had been spoken in Carthage by the children in former times, that gamma should drive beta, and again beta itself should drive gamma.

[123306] 21.15. And then it was said by the children at play and had been left in the rank of a perplexing riddle, but now it was plainly clear to all.

[123450] 21.16. For before, Gizeric drove out Boniface, and now Belisarius Gelimer. This, then, whether it was a rumour or a saying, turned out thus.

[123592] 21.17. Then too a dream, often seen by many before, but unclear how it would turn out, came to light. And the dream was of this kind. The Carthaginians reverence Cyprian, a holy man, most of all.

[123789] 21.18. And having built for him a temple worthy of much account before the city by the shore of the sea, they perform all other rites and keep a festival which they call Cypriana, and from this the sailors are wont to name the storm, of which I just made mention, by the same name as the festival, since it loves to come at the time at which the Libyans have been accustomed ever to keep this festival.

[124193] 21.19. This temple the Vandals, in the reign of Honoric, having forced the Christians, held.

[124287] 21.20. And driving out the priests thence at once with much dishonour, they themselves for the rest took care of the rites, as belonging to the Arians.

[124440] 21.21. The Libyans being vexed and at a loss about these things, they say Cyprian often appeared to them in a dream and said that the Christians ought not at all to be anxious about him; for he himself, in course of time, would be their avenger.

[124687] 21.22. And when the word, carried about, came to all the Libyans, they awaited that some vengeance for these sanctuaries would come to the Vandals, but were not able to infer how the vision of the dream would turn out for them.

[124916] 21.23. Now, then, since the emperor's fleet came to Libya (for the time, coming round, was about to keep the festival on the morrow), the Arian priests, although Ammatas had led the Vandals to Decimum, both cleansed the whole sanctuary and hung the fairest of the offerings there, and preparing the lamps and bringing out the treasures from the storerooms, made all things exactly ready, as each of them chanced to be fit for its use.

[125352] 21.24. But the things at Decimum befell as I have shown.

[125410] 21.25. And the Arian priests fled and were gone, while the Christians who had rightly practised the things of the faith, coming to the temple of Cyprian, lit all the lamps and took care of the rites as it was the law for them to perform them, and thus to all what the vision of the dream foretold became known. But these things turned out thus.

[125756] 22.1. And the Vandals, remembering an ancient saying, marvelled, knowing for the rest that for a man, being mortal, neither any hope is incredible nor any possession secure.

[125931] 22.2. What this saying was and in what manner it was spoken, I shall show.

[126007] 22.3. When the Vandals at the beginning, pressed by famine, were about to rise up from their ancestral homes, a portion of them was left behind, who, held by hesitation, found it not to their pleasure to follow Godigisclus.

[126232] 22.4. And as time went on, to those who remained the abundance of provisions seemed to be well, and Gizeric with those about him held Libya.

[126374] 22.5. Hearing this, those who had not followed Godigisclus rejoiced, the country for the rest sufficing them most strongly for living.

[126510] 22.6. But fearing lest at some much later time either they themselves who held Libya, or their descendants, being driven out of Libya in some way, should return to their ancestral homes (for they suspected that the Romans would not overlook it for ever), they sent envoys to them.

[126792] 22.7. They, when they came into the sight of Gizeric, said that they rejoiced with their kinsmen who had thus prospered, but were not able to guard further the land which they themselves, holding of small account, had settled in Libya.

[127029] 22.8. They begged, then, that, if they did not lay claim to the ancestral land, they should gift them with a useless possession, so that, having become most securely masters of the country, if anyone should go to do it mischief, they might by no means decline to die for it.

[127305] 22.9. To Gizeric and the other Vandals, then, they seemed to speak well and justly, and they were ready to concede all that their envoys needed.

[127451] 22.10. But a certain old man among them, notable and having a great repute for intelligence, said that he would by no means allow such a thing. For none of human things stands on safety, but for them for all time nothing of what is is secure, but nothing of what is not is impossible.

[127737] 22.11. Gizeric, hearing this, praised it and resolved to send the envoys away unsuccessful. Then, indeed, both he and the one who advised, as foreseeing the impossible, incurred the laughter of all the Vandals.

[127949] 22.12. But when these things which were told happened, the Vandals both learned anew the nature of human affairs and knew the word to be that of a wise man.

[128107] 22.13. Of those Vandals, then, who remained in the ancestral land, neither any memory nor name is preserved to my time. For, I think, being few, it befell them either to be forced by the barbarians their neighbours or to be mingled with them not unwillingly, and their name to be merged into theirs.

[128408] 22.14. Nor indeed did it come into the mind of the Vandals, then worsted by Belisarius, to go thence to their ancestral homes.

[128536] 22.15. For they were not able from Libya, suddenly, especially as ships were not present to them, to be conveyed to Europe, but they paid there the penalty of all that they had wrought against the Romans and not least against the Zacynthians.

[128780] 22.16. For Gizeric, once attacking the places in the Peloponnese, attempted to assault Taenarum. And being quickly repulsed thence and losing many of his followers, he withdrew in no order.

[128971] 22.17. Therefore, still held by anger, he touched at Zacynthus, and killing many of those in his way and enslaving about five hundred of the notable, sailed off in a short time.

[129150] 22.18. And when he was in the middle of the so-called Adriatic sea, there cutting up the bodies of the five hundred, he threw them everywhere over the sea, taking no account. But these things took place in former times.

[129371] 23.1. And Gelimer then, giving much money to the husbandmen of the Libyans and using friendliness toward them, was able to win over many.

[129510] 23.2. Whom he bade kill the Romans who went about the country places, proclaiming a fixed sum of gold for each murder to the one who should do the killing.

[129667] 23.3. And they killed many of the Roman army, not soldiers, however, but slaves and servants, who, going up into the villages out of desire for money, were secretly caught.

[129841] 23.4. And the husbandmen, bringing their heads to Gelimer, themselves departed having been paid, while he supposed they had killed hostile soldiers.

[129991] 23.5. At this time Diogenes, the bodyguard of Belisarius, displayed deeds worthy of valour. For sent with twenty-two of the shield-bearers on a reconnaissance of the enemy, he was at a place distant two days' journey from Carthage.

[130224] 23.6. These men (for the husbandmen of the place were not able to kill them) they reported to Gelimer to be there.

[130340] 23.7. And he, choosing out three hundred Vandal horsemen, sent them against them, charging them to bring them all alive to him.

[130469] 23.8. For it seemed to him worth much account to make captive a bodyguard of Belisarius with twenty-two shield-bearers.

[130590] 23.9. Those under Diogenes, having entered a certain house, were sleeping in the upper room, having nothing hostile in mind, since they had learned the enemy to be far off.

[130764] 23.10. And the Vandals, coming there at deep dawn, thought it inexpedient for them either to break the doors there or to enter the house by night, fearing lest, falling into a night-battle, they should destroy themselves, and give an exit, should it so chance, in the dark to most of the enemy.

[131060] 23.11. And they did this, cowardice astounding their mind, when it was in their power with no trouble, both bringing fire and without it, to take the enemy not only unarmed but altogether naked on their bedding.

[131273] 23.12. But now, making a phalanx all around the house in a circle and especially at the doors, they all stood.

[131385] 23.13. Meanwhile it befell one of the Roman soldiers to rise from sleep, who, perceiving the tumult which the Vandals secretly made by speaking among themselves and moving with their arms, was able to infer what was being done, and waking each of his comrades in silence told what was being done.

[131683] 23.14. And they, by the counsel of Diogenes, all quietly put on their garments and, taking up their arms, went down.

[131801] 23.15. There, putting the bridles on their horses, they leaped onto them, giving no one perception. And standing some time by the courtyard, they suddenly fling open the doors there, and all go out at once.

[132009] 23.16. The Vandals, then, were already at work against them, but accomplished nothing. For the Romans, fenced with their shields and defending themselves with their spears against the assailants, drove on with haste.

[132227] 23.17. And thus Diogenes escaped the enemy, losing two of his followers and saving the rest.

[132321] 23.18. Wounds, however, he himself too received in this toil, three in the neck and face, from which he came near to dying, and one on the left hand, from which he was no longer able to work the smallest of his fingers. So these things befell thus.

[132571] 23.19. And Belisarius, offering great wealth to the craftsmen about the building and the other throng, dug a trench worthy of much account about the wall in a circle, and putting many palisades in it fenced it well all about.

[132798] 23.20. And indeed he rebuilt the suffered parts of the wall in a short time, a thing of wonder not only to the Carthaginians, but to Gelimer himself afterward.

[132959] 23.21. For when he came captive to Carthage, he marvelled at seeing the wall, and said that his own neglect had become the cause of all that had befallen him. These things, then, were accomplished by Belisarius while he was in Carthage.

[133197] 24.1. And Tzazon, the brother of Gelimer, with the fleet which has been mentioned, coming to Sardo, disembarked at the harbour of Caranalis, and took the city at the first shout, and killed the tyrant Godas and whatever was warlike about him.

[133598] 24.3. "Know that Godas the tyrant has perished, having come under our hands, and that the island is again under your kingship, O King of the Vandals and Alani, and keep the festival of victory.

[133793] 24.4. And as for the enemy who dared to make an expedition against our land, hope that their attempt will come to that fortune to which it came also before for those who made an expedition against our ancestors."

[134817] 24.9. And the envoys going up to him, Theudis deemed them worthy of friendliness and feasted them eagerly, and at the banquet inquired, ostensibly, how the affairs of Gelimer and the Vandals stood.

[135016] 24.10. And while these envoys were coming to him more slowly, he chanced to have heard all that had befallen the Vandals.

[135139] 24.11. For a single merchant vessel, sailing for trade on that day on which the army rode into Carthage, setting out thence and chancing upon a favourable wind,

[135301] 24.12. came to Spain. Whence Theudis, learning all that had come to pass in Libya, forbade the merchants to tell anyone, that these things might not become known to all.

[135472] 24.13. And when those under Gothaeus answered that all their affairs stood most excellently, he asked for what reason they had come.

[135606] 24.14. And when they held out the alliance, Theudis bade them go to the coast: "For from there," he said,

[135713] 24.15. "you will safely learn your affairs at home." And the envoys, suspecting that the word was not sound, since the man was drunk, kept silence.

[135862] 24.16. But when, being with him on the morrow, they made account of the alliance, and Theudis again used the same word, then, understanding that revolutionary things had befallen them in Libya, but having nothing in mind about Carthage, they sailed to it.

[136119] 24.17. And putting in near it and falling among Roman soldiers, they handed themselves over to use as they wished.

[136235] 24.18. Whence, being led to the general and reporting the whole story, they suffered no harm from him.

[136339] 24.19. These things, then, it chanced befell thus. And Cyril, being near Sardo and hearing what had befallen Godas, sailed to Carthage, where, finding the Roman army and Belisarius victorious, he remained quiet; and Solomon was sent to the emperor, to report what had been done.

[136619] 25.1. And Gelimer, when he was in the plain of Boulla, which is distant for an unencumbered man four days' journey from Carthage, not far from the borders of Numidia, gathered there all the Vandals and whatever was friendly to him among the Moors.

[136868] 25.2. A few Moors, however, came to him for alliance,

[136923] 25.3. and these altogether without a ruler. For as many as ruled the Moors in Mauritania and Numidia and Byzacium, sending envoys to Belisarius, said they were slaves of the emperor and promised to be allies.

[137133] 25.4. And there are some who even gave their children in the rank of hostages, and begged that the tokens of rule be sent to them by him according to the ancient law.

[137301] 25.5. For it was a law that no one should rule the Moors, even if he were an enemy of the Romans, until the Emperor of the Romans should give him the marks of rule.

[137467] 25.6. Which, though they had already received from the Vandals, they did not think they held the rule securely.

[137580] 25.7. And these tokens are a silver staff overlaid with gold and a silver cap not covering the whole head, but, like a band, held up on all sides by silver fillets, and a certain white cloak gathered to a golden brooch on the right shoulder in the form of a Thessalian chlamys, and a white tunic with embroidery, and a gilded boot.

[137913] 25.8. And Belisarius sent these to them and gifted each of them with much money.

[137995] 25.9. They did not, however, come to him for alliance, nor indeed did they dare to help the Vandals, but standing aloof from both awaited how the fortune of the war would turn out. So the affairs of the Romans stood.

[138213] 25.10. And Gelimer sent one of the Vandals to Sardo, holding a letter to his brother Tzazon. He, coming quickly to the coast and chancing upon a merchant vessel setting out, sailed to the harbour of Caranalis and put the letter into the hands of Tzazon. And the writing declared as follows:

[138505] 25.11. "It was not, I think, Godas who made our island revolt, but some doom from heaven falling upon the Vandals.

[138621] 25.12. For taking away you and the notable of the Vandals from us, it has snatched all good things at once from the house of Gizeric.

[138756] 25.13. For you set out thence not to recover the island for us, but that Justinian might be master of Libya. For what fortune resolved before it is now possible to know from the outcomes.

[138945] 25.14. Belisarius, then, has come against us with a small army; but virtue, departing from the Vandals, at once went, taking good fortune with her.

[139094] 25.15. For Ammatas and Gibamundus have fallen, the Vandals playing the weakling, and horses and dockyards and all Libya and not least Carthage itself are already held by the enemy.

[139276] 25.16. And the Vandals sit, having exchanged the not playing the man in the toils for children and women and all their goods, and for us there is left only the plain of Boulla, where the hope in you, having seated us, keeps us.

[139505] 25.17. But you, leaving the tyranny and Sardo and the cares about these things, come as quickly as possible to us with the whole fleet. For those whose danger is for the chief things, to be exact about the rest is inexpedient.

[139733] 25.18. And jointly for the rest contending against the enemy, we shall either recover our former fortune, or we shall gain the not bearing, apart from one another, the untoward things from the divine power."

[139942] 25.19. When Tzazon saw this brought to him and brought it out to the Vandals, they turned to wailing and lamentations, not, however, openly, but as much as possible hiding it and escaping the notice of the islanders, in silence to themselves bewailing their present circumstances.

[140224] 25.20. And at once disposing of the things in hand, as it chanced, they filled the ships.

[140315] 25.21. And setting out thence with the whole fleet, on the third day they sailed to the shore of Libya which divides the Numidians and the Mauritanians.

[140469] 25.22. And going on foot they come to the plain of Boulla, where they mingled with the rest of the army. There many things worthy of much pity befell the Vandals, which I could no longer adequately tell.

[140674] 25.23. For I think that even if a man of the very enemy had chanced to be a spectator, perhaps he too would then have pitied both the Vandals and human fortune.

[140836] 25.24. For Gelimer and Tzazon, when they had thrown their arms about each other's neck, were no longer able to let go, and uttered nothing at all to one another, but, clasping hands, wept; and each of the Vandals with Gelimer, embracing one of those come from Sardo, did the same.

[141118] 25.25. And for a long time, as though grown into one another, they enjoyed the pleasure thence, and neither did those about Gelimer ask about Godas (since their present fortune, having struck them, ranked what before had seemed to them most earnest among things now exceedingly neglected), nor did those come from Sardo think fit to ask anything about what had befallen in Libya. For the place sufficed them to infer what had come to pass.

[141559] 25.26. Nor indeed did they make any account of their own women or children, knowing well that, if any of them were not there, plainly either they had died or had come under the hands of the enemy. So these things stood thus.
