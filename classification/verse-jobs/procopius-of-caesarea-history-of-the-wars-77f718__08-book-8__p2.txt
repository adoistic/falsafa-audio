[221262] 30.17. And the throng of the enemy is worth despising, gathered as it is from nations the most numerous possible. For an alliance contributed from many quarters carries neither faith nor power secure, but, being torn apart in its races, is divided also in its purposes, as is likely.

[221547] 30.18. For do not think that Huns and Lombards and Heruls, hired for I know not how much money, will ever risk their lives for them unto death.

[221692] 30.19. For their souls are not so cheap that they should hold even their lives second to silver; but I know well that, making the matters of battle a thing for the eyes, they will very straightway play the coward, either having received their wages, or having fulfilled the command of their own commanders.

[222000] 30.20. For to men, even what seem to be the most delightful of things—not to say warlike—if they be not done according to their wish, but under compulsion or for hire or constrained by something else, no longer turn out to their liking, but, through necessity, appear grievous. Considering these things, with all eagerness let us go against the enemy."

[222354] 31.1. Totila too said this much. And the armies came together for battle and were drawn up as follows. Front to front, all on both sides stood, intending to make the front of the phalanx as deep and as long as possible.

[222575] 31.2. And of the Romans, the left wing Narses and John held, about the mound, and with them whatever was best in the Roman army;

[222705] 31.3. for each of them, apart from the other soldiers, was followed by a multitude of bodyguards and shield-bearers and barbarian Huns, gathered for their valour;

[222869] 31.4. while on the right Valerian and John the Glutton with Dagisthaeus and all the rest of the Romans were drawn up.

[222988] 31.5. And foot-archers from the register-soldiers, about eight thousand, they set on both wings. And in the middle of the phalanx Narses set the Lombards and the Herul nation and all the other barbarians, dismounting them from their horses and making them foot-soldiers, in order that, if they should prove cowards in the work or play the malingerer, if it should so happen, they might not be the quicker to flight.

[223405] 31.6. And the end of the left wing of the Roman front Narses set at an angle, stationing there one thousand five hundred horsemen.

[223537] 31.7. And it had been ordered to five hundred of them, whenever any of the Romans should happen to be routed, to bring them aid in haste, and to the thousand, whenever the foot-soldiers of the enemy should begin the work, to come at once behind them and place them between two fires.

[223822] 31.8. And Totila too in the same manner set the whole army opposite the enemy. And, going round his own array, he encouraged the soldiers and called them to daring by countenance and word.

[224012] 31.9. And Narses too did this same thing, raising on poles bracelets and necklaces and golden bridles and certain other incitements to eagerness for the danger.

[224174] 31.10. And for some time neither began the battle, but both remained quiet, awaiting the attack of their opponents.

[224291] 31.11. But afterwards one from the Gothic army, Coccas by name, having a sufficient reputation for energy, riding out his horse, came near the Roman army and challenged, if anyone wished to come out against him to single combat.

[224521] 31.12. Now this Coccas happened to be one of the Roman soldiers who had earlier deserted to Totila.

[224622] 31.13. And at once one of the bodyguards of Narses stood opposite him, an Armenian by race, Anzalas by name, himself too mounted on a horse.

[224764] 31.14. Coccas, then, rushing first as if to strike with his spear, came at his enemy, aiming at his belly.

[224872] 31.15. But Anzalas, suddenly swerving his horse, made him fail of his own rush. And thereby, coming himself to the flank of the enemy, he thrust his spear into his left side.

[225048] 31.16. And he, falling from his horse, lay a corpse on the ground; and an extraordinary outcry rose from the Roman army, yet not even so did either side begin any battle.

[225220] 31.17. But Totila alone came into the no-man's-land, not to fight a single combat, but to baulk this opportunity for his opponents. For, learning that the two thousand Goths who had been left were coming up close by, he was putting off the engagement until their arrival, and did the following.

[225516] 31.18. First he did not disdain to show the enemy who he was. For both the equipment of his arms, thickly overlaid with gold, he was wearing, and the adornment of his cheek-pieces and from his cap and his spear hung purple and otherwise kingly, a marvel how great.

[225782] 31.19. And he himself, mounted on a surpassing horse, skilfully played the war-dance in the no-man's-land. For wheeling his horse in a circle, and turning him again to the other side, he made circular courses.

[225993] 31.20. And, as he rode, he hurled his spear into the air, and, snatching it as it quivered from there, then passing it from hand to hand frequently on either side, and shifting it skilfully, he prided himself on his practice in such things, leaning back and bending sideways and inclining to either side, like one accurately taught from boyhood the matters of the dance.

[226365] 31.21. And, doing these things, he wore away all the early morning. And, wishing to prolong the postponement of the battle to the utmost, he sent to the Roman camp, saying that he wished to confer with them. But Narses maintained that he was playing a trick, since, if he had wished to fight earlier, when there was opportunity to put forward proposals, now, having come into the no-man's-land, he would proceed to discussions.

[226794] 32.1. And meanwhile the two thousand too came to the Goths; and Totila, learning that they had come to the stockade, since the time also was leading toward the midday meal, both went himself to his own tent and the Goths, breaking up the battle-array, returned backward.

[227066] 32.2. And coming to be in his own halting-place, Totila found the two thousand already present. And, ordering all to take their meal, and changing the whole equipment of his arms, he armed himself accurately in the manner of a soldier, and at once led the army against the enemy, thinking to fall upon them unexpectedly and thereby to take them.

[227413] 32.3. But not even so did he find the Romans unprepared. For Narses, fearing—which happened—lest the enemy should fall upon them unexpectedly, had forbidden all to take their meal or to sleep or even to take off their cuirasses, no one of them all, or to let his horse loose from the bridle.

[227706] 32.4. Yet he did not leave them altogether without food, but ordered them to breakfast in their array and in the equipment of their arms, and thus, continually on the watch, to await the attack of the enemy.

[227915] 32.5. In the same manner, however, they were no longer drawn up, but for the Romans the wings, on which the foot-archers stood by four thousands, turned to a crescent shape, by the counsel of Narses.

[228116] 32.6. And the foot-soldiers of the Goths all stood in a mass behind the horsemen, in order that, if it should befall the horsemen to be routed, the fugitives, turning back upon them, might be saved, and both together at once advance. And it had been ordered to all the Goths to use neither bow nor anything else at all in this engagement except their spears.

[228476] 32.7. Wherefore it befell Totila to be outgeneralled by his own imprudence; for he, setting himself to this battle, persuaded by I know not whom, presented to his opponents his own army opposing them neither in arms nor balanced in array nor matched in any other respect, since the Romans, each, used whatever was most effective in the work, either shooting, or thrusting with spears, or handling swords, or doing some other of the things ready to their hand and serviceable for the moment, some riding, others setting themselves on foot in the array, according to what was profitable to the need, and in some places effecting an encirclement of the enemy, in others receiving them as they came on and beating off the attack with their shields.

[229222] 32.8. But the horsemen of the Goths, their foot-soldiers being left behind them, trusting in their spears alone, advanced with an inconsiderate rush, and, coming to be in the toil, reaped the fruit of their imprudence.

[229442] 32.9. For rushing against the middle of the enemy, they unwittingly came to be in the midst of the eight thousand foot-archers, and, being shot at by them on both sides, they straightway gave up, since the archers were turning both wings of the front gradually to the crescent, as I have said before.

[229744] 32.10. Many men, then, and many horses the Goths lost in this toil, before they had yet come to close quarters with their opponents, and, having made trial of many irreparable evils, late and with difficulty came to the array of the enemy.

[229985] 32.11. Here I cannot single out any of the Romans or of the barbarian allies with them for wonder above the rest.

[230100] 32.12. For there was one eagerness and valour and contending spirit in all, and each, as their opponents came on, receiving the onset most stoutly, beat it off.

[230262] 32.13. And it was now toward evening, and both camps were suddenly set in motion, the Goths to retreat, the Romans to pursuit.

[230390] 32.14. For when the Romans rushed against them, the Goths did not hold out against the enemy, but yielded as they advanced and turned in headlong flight, terrified by their throng and their good order.

[230593] 32.15. And they looked to no defence, as if phantoms had fallen upon them, fearing them, or as if warred upon from heaven.

[230717] 32.16. And in a little, when they came to their own foot-soldiers, the evil was raised much higher and went forward.

[230835] 32.17. For not in order making their withdrawal did they come to them (to recover breath and to renew the fight with them, as is the custom, or to push off the pursuers, or to make a counter-pursuit or some other form of war), but so disorderly that some of them even were destroyed, the cavalry falling upon them.

[231151] 32.18. Wherefore the foot-soldiers neither, opening their ranks, received them, nor stood firm to save them, but together with them all fled headlong, and there indeed they destroyed one another, as in a battle by night.

[231373] 32.19. And the Roman army, enjoying their terror, with no sparing kept ever killing those in their path, who neither defended themselves nor dared to look them in the face, but offered themselves to the enemy to do with as they wished; so much did the terrors weigh upon them and the fear prevail.

[231672] 32.20. And six thousand of them died in this work, and many gave themselves up to their adversaries. These the Romans for the moment took alive, but not long after killed. And not only Goths were slain, but very many too of the old Roman soldiers, those who had earlier renounced the Roman army and deserted, as I have said in the earlier books, to Totila and the Goths.

[232044] 32.21. But those of the Gothic army to whom it befell neither to perish nor to come into the hands of the enemy were able to escape notice and to flee, as each of them had of horse or of foot or of fortune (or as occasion or place served to attain this).

[232300] 32.22. This battle, then, ended thus, and it was now altogether growing dark. And as Totila fled in the darkness with men no more than five, of whom Scipuar happened to be one, some of the Romans pursued, not knowing that it was Totila; among whom Asbadus the Gepid happened to be.

[232583] 32.23. He, when he came very close to Totila, set upon him as if to strike him on the back with his spear.

[232691] 32.24. But a Gothic youth from the household of Totila, following his master as he fled, and indignant at the fortune then present, cried out loudly, "What is this, you dog? Are you rushing to strike your own master?" Asbadus, then, thrust his spear with all his might against Totila, but he himself, struck in the foot by Scipuar, remained there.

[233040] 32.25. And Scipuar himself, struck by one of the pursuers, stood still, and those who had made the pursuit with Asbadus, being four, no longer pursued, in order to save him,

[233215] 32.26. but turned back with him. But those who followed Totila, thinking the enemy were still pursuing them, none the less drove forward, although they were bringing along the man mortally struck and most surely fainting, necessity controlling their violent course.

[233482] 32.27. And, having accomplished eighty-four stades, they came to a place named Caprae. There, keeping quiet thenceforth, they tended Totila's wound; and he not long after measured out his life.

[233677] 32.28. And there those who had followed him, hiding him in the earth, withdrew. This was the end of the rule and the life of Totila, who had ruled the Goths eleven years, an end not coming worthily upon his former deeds, since both the affairs of the man had earlier gone forward, and his death came not according to reason upon his deeds.

[234018] 32.29. But now too fortune, plainly displaying her charms and mocking human affairs, both the unaccountable that is her own and the unpleadable of her will, gave Totila prosperity, of itself, for a length of time, from no cause, but then, in this stubborn way, allotted the man cowardice and ruin.

[234317] 32.30. from no fitting cause at the present time. But these things, I think, are neither comprehensible to man, nor ever will be later; yet they are forever spoken of and held in opinion, whispered abroad for all time, as is dear to each, the seemingly probable account consoling the ignorance. But I return to the former account.

[234649] 32.31. The Romans, then, did not know that Totila had thus vanished from among men, until a certain woman, a Goth by race, told them and pointed out the tomb.

[234809] 32.32. And they, hearing it, thinking the account not sound, came to the place, and, digging up the grave with no hesitation, brought out Totila a corpse from there, and, recognizing him, as they say, and having filled their desire with this sight too, hid him again in the earth, and at once reported the whole matter to Narses.

[235140] 32.33. But some say that the matters concerning Totila and this battle did not happen thus, but in some other manner; which it has seemed to me not inappropriate to record.

[235314] 32.34. For they say that the retreat of the Gothic army did not come about without cause or contrary to reason, but that, some of the Romans skirmishing, an arrow from a bow fell suddenly upon Totila, not by the forethought of the one who sent it, since Totila, armed in the manner of a soldier and arrayed somewhere in the phalanx unregarded, stood, not wishing to be plain to the enemy, nor in any way offering himself to a plot, but some fortune contriving these things for him and guiding the shaft to the man's body; and that he, mortally struck, becoming, so far as possible, in great pain, went out of the phalanx and, with a few, went backward little by little.

[235985] 32.35. And as far as Caprae he drove his horse, enduring the hardship, but there, fainting, remained thenceforth to tend the wound, and not long after the appointed day of life came upon him.

[236178] 32.36. And the Gothic army, being neither otherwise a match for their opponents, and their commander too having become, contrary to expectation, incapable of fighting, fell into amazement, that Totila alone, not from a plot of the enemy, should be mortally struck, and from this, becoming exceedingly fearful and dispirited, fell into a terror that had no bound and into a retreat thus shameful. But concerning these things let each man say as he knows.

[236633] 33.1. And Narses, exceedingly delighted at what had happened, ceased not referring all things to God, which indeed was the true account, and managed the matters at hand.

[236804] 33.2. And first, wishing to be rid of the absurdity of the Lombards who followed him (who, in addition to their other lawlessness in their manner of life, burned the buildings they fell in with, and forcibly approached the women who took refuge in the sanctuaries), he, having welcomed them with great sums, let them go to their ancestral homes, ordering Valerian and Damian his nephew, with their followers, to lead them on their way as far as the Roman borders, so that they should harm no one in their withdrawal.

[237322] 33.3. And when the Lombards had departed from the land of the Romans, Valerian encamped about the city of Verona, intending to besiege it and bring it over to the emperor.

[237495] 33.4. And the guards there, becoming afraid, came to a conference with Valerian, as about to hand over both themselves and the city by agreement.

[237642] 33.5. But when the Franks who held the garrison in the regions of Venetia learned this, they hindered it with all eagerness, thinking fit to lay claim to the country as belonging to them. And from this Valerian withdrew from there with his whole army, accomplishing nothing.

[237918] 33.6. And the Goths, all those who had fled from the engagement and been saved, crossing the river Po, held the city of Ticinum and the regions there, and appointed Teias as their ruler.

[238106] 33.7. And he, finding all the money which Totila had happened to lay up in Ticinum, planned to bring the Franks into alliance, and disposed and arranged the Goths as well as he could from the present circumstances, gathering them all about him in haste.

[238361] 33.8. Hearing this, Narses ordered Valerian, with all his followers, to keep guard about the river Po, so that the Goths might not be able more fearlessly to combine, while he himself, with all the rest of the army, went against Rome.

[238597] 33.9. And, coming to be in Tuscany, he took Narnia by agreement, and to the people of Spoletium, who were unwalled, he left a garrison, ordering them to rebuild as quickly as possible such parts of the circuit-wall as the Goths had happened to throw down.

[238854] 33.10. And he sent also some to make trial of the garrison in Perusia. And those who commanded the guards in Perusia were two Romans who had become deserters, Meligedius and Ulifus, who, having been a bodyguard of Cyprian before, persuaded by Totila who promised him much, then killed Cyprian by treachery, who at that time commanded the garrison there.

[239209] 33.11. Meligedius, then, accepting the proposals of Narses, planned, with his Roman followers, to hand over the city; but those about Ulifus, perceiving what was being done, combined openly against them.

[239414] 33.12. And Ulifus with those of his mind was destroyed, while Meligedius at once handed over Perusia to the Romans. To Ulifus, however, it befell that vengeance from God, clearly, fell upon him, in that he was destroyed in the very place where he had killed Cyprian. These things, then, went forward thus.

[239721] 33.13. And the Goths who held the guard in Rome, when they learned that Narses and the Roman army were coming against them and were very close, prepared to confront them with what they could.

[239914] 33.14. Now Totila had happened to burn many of the buildings of the city, when he first took it. . . . And at last, reckoning that the Goths, being reduced to a few, could no longer guard the whole circuit-wall of Rome, he, fencing off a small portion of the city about the tomb of Hadrian with a short wall and fastening it to the former wall, set up the shape of a fort.

[240288] 33.15. And there, laying up their most precious possessions, the Goths guarded this fort accurately, but the rest of the wall of the city they neglected and disregarded.

[240459] 33.16. Leaving, then, a few of themselves as guards in this place at that time, all the rest, coming to be about the battlements of the wall of the city, were eager to make trial of the enemy as they assaulted the wall.

[240680] 33.17. Now the whole circuit of Rome, through its excessive size, neither could the Romans, attacking, surround, nor the Goths guard.

[240815] 33.18. But, scattered, the one party attacked where they happened to come, the others defended as best they could from the present circumstances. And Narses, bringing along a great number of archers, made an attack upon a certain portion of the circuit-wall, while elsewhere John the son of Vitalian with his followers assaulted.

[241146] 33.19. And Philemuth and the Heruls harassed at another part, and very far from them the rest followed. And they all assaulted the wall, being as far as possible from one another.

[241327] 33.20. And against them the barbarians, gathering, received the attack. But the rest of the circuit-wall, where the Romans made no assault, was altogether empty of men, the Goths all gathering wherever the enemy should attack, as I have said.

[241571] 33.21. And meanwhile, by the counsel of Narses, Dagisthaeus, having a great multitude of soldiers and bringing along both the standard of Narses and that of John and many ladders, suddenly attacked a portion of the circuit-wall altogether bereft of guard.

[241828] 33.22. And at once setting all the ladders against the wall, with no one defending, with no toil he came inside the circuit-wall with his followers, and they opened the gates at will.

[242013] 33.23. And the Goths, perceiving this at once, no longer looked to defence, but all fled wherever it became possible for each. And some leaped into the fort, while others went at a run to Portus.

[242210] 33.24. And here a thought has come to me in my account, in what manner fortune mocks human affairs, not always going to men in the same way, nor regarding them with equal eyes, but changing with time and place, and plays a sort of game against them, by varying the worth of the wretched men according to the occasion or the place or the manner, if indeed Bessas, who earlier lost Rome, not long after recovered for the Romans Petra in Lazica, while conversely Dagisthaeus, having given up Petra to the enemy, recovered Rome for the emperor in a short time.

[242768] 33.25. But these things have been from the beginning and will always be, so long as the same fortune is with men. And Narses then went with all his army to make war upon the fort.

[242949] 33.26. And the barbarians, becoming terrified and having received pledges concerning their lives, surrendered to him at once both themselves and the fort, in the twenty-sixth year of the reign of the emperor Justinian.

[243169] 33.27. And thus, under the reign of this emperor, Rome was taken for the fifth time; and Narses at once sent the keys of its gates to the emperor.

[243317] 34.1. Then indeed it was shown most plainly to men that to all, for whom it must be ill, even what seem to be good fortunes are turned to destruction, and, having fared according to their wish perhaps, by such prosperity they are destroyed together with it.

[243576] 34.2. For to the senatorial council and the people of the Romans this victory happened to become, much more, the cause of ruin, in the following manner.

[243730] 34.3. The Goths, fleeing and despairing of the dominion of Italy, making it a by-task of their journey, dispatched with no sparing the Romans whom they fell in with.

[243897] 34.4. And the barbarians of the Roman army treated as enemies all whom they fell in with at their entrance into the city.

[244020] 34.5. And besides, this too befell them. Many of those from the senatorial council, this being decided by Totila, had earlier remained in the regions about Campania.

[244187] 34.6. And some of these, when they learned that Rome was held by the emperor's army, rising up from Campania, went to it. When the Goths learned this, all of them who happened to be in the strongholds there, searching out all the regions there, killed all the patricians. Among whom too was Maximus, of whom I made mention in the earlier books.

[244533] 34.7. And Totila too had happened, when he was going from there to confront Narses, to gather the children of the approved Romans from each city, and, picking out about three hundred of them, whom he thought most beautiful of body, telling their parents that they would live with him,

[244819] 34.8. but in true account being his hostages. And these Totila had then ordered to be beyond the river Po, and Teias, now finding them all there, killed them.

[244979] 34.9. And Ragnaris, a Gothic man, who commanded the garrison among the Tarentines, although by the emperor's decision he had received pledges from Pacurius and had agreed to go over to the Romans, as I have said before, and had provided six Goths too in the rank of hostages to the Romans on this agreement, when he heard that Teias had been appointed king of the Goths and was both bringing the Franks into aid and willing with his whole army to come to close quarters with the enemy, changing his counsel was by no means willing to fulfil what had been agreed.

[245543] 34.10. And, eager and straining to recover the hostages, he devised the following. Sending to Pacurius, he asked that a few of the Roman soldiers be sent to him, in order that they might be able to go in safety to Hydrus, and from there, ferrying across the Ionian gulf, to be conveyed to Byzantium.

[245844] 34.11. Pacurius, then, far removed from what the man had planned, sent fifty of his followers.

[245940] 34.12. And he, receiving them into the fort, shut them up at once, and signified to Pacurius that, if he wished to rescue his own soldiers, it would be necessary to give back the Gothic hostages.

[246137] 34.13. Hearing this, Pacurius left a few men for the guard of Hydrus, and with all the rest of the army went at once against the enemy.

[246274] 34.14. And Ragnaris, with no delay, killed the fifty, and, as if to confront the enemy, led the Goths out from Tarentum. And when they came to blows with one another, the Goths were worsted.

[246466] 34.15. And Ragnaris, losing very many there, with the rest had recourse to flight. He was in no way able, however, to enter Tarentum, the Romans having surrounded it on all sides, but, coming to Acherontia, he remained there.

[246693] 34.16. These things, then, had come about thus. And the Romans, not long after, besieging Portus, took it by agreement, and a fort in Tuscany which they call Nepa, and the stronghold in the place named Petra Pertusa.

[246911] 34.17. But Teias (for he did not think the Goths a match for the Roman army by themselves) sent to Theudibald the ruler of the Franks, offering great sums and calling him to alliance.

[247096] 34.18. But the Franks, having taken counsel, I think, of what was profitable, were willing to die neither for the advantage of the Goths nor for that of the Romans, but were eager to make Italy their own, and on this account were willing to undergo the dangers in war. And it happened

[247382] 34.19. that Totila had laid up some of his money in Ticinum, as I have said before, but most of it in an exceedingly strong fort, which happens to be in Cumae in Campania, and had set guards there, and appointed as commander over them his own brother with Herodian.

[247649] 34.20. These Narses, wishing to take, sent some to Cumae to besiege the fort, while he himself remained there putting Rome in order. And, sending others, he ordered them to besiege Centumcellae.

[247845] 34.21. But Teias, fearing for the guards in Cumae and the money, and despairing of the hope from the Franks, drew up his followers to come to close quarters with the enemy.

[248019] 34.22. Perceiving this, Narses ordered John the nephew of Vitalian and Philemuth with his own army to go to the regions of Tuscany, both to sit there and to check for their opponents the road to Campania, in order that those who were besieging Cumae might more fearlessly take it either by force or by agreement.

[248333] 34.23. But Teias, leaving for the most part the shortest roads on the right, and making many and very long roundabout ways, going through the coast of the Ionian gulf, came into Campania,

[248522] 34.24. escaping the notice of all the enemy. When Narses learned this, he sent for those about John and Philemuth, who were guarding the passage in Tuscany, and recalled Valerian, who was just then taking Petra the so-called Pertusa, with his followers, and gathered the forces together, and himself too, with all the army drawn up as for battle, went against the Campanians.

[248899] 35.1. Now there is in Campania a certain mountain, Vesuvius, of which I made mention in the earlier books, in that it often sends forth a sound resembling a bellowing. And whenever this befalls it, it then belches up also a great quantity of boiling ashes. These things were said by me at that point in my account.

[249215] 35.2. Of this mountain—as also of Aetna in Sicily—the middle parts, from the lowest to the summit, happen of their own accord to be hollow, where, below, the fire burns continually.

[249398] 35.3. And to such a depth does this hollow extend, that to a man standing on the summit and daring to peer over from there the flame is not easily visible.

[249555] 35.4. And whenever it befalls this mountain to belch up the ashes, as I have said, and the flame, breaking off rocks from the topmost parts of Vesuvius, lifts them above the peak of this mountain, some small, others exceedingly great, and, sending them from there, scatters them wherever it happens.

[249856] 35.5. And there flows here a stream of fire too, extending from the summit down to the foot of the mountain and still further, all which things are wont to occur about Aetna also. And the stream of fire makes high banks on either side, cutting the lower parts.

[250118] 35.6. And, carried at first along the channel, the flame is likened to a burning outflow of water; but when it befalls it to be quenched, the course is checked along the channel straightway, and the stream goes no further forward, and the sediment of this fire appears as mud resembling ashes.

[250413] 35.7. And at the foot of this Vesuvius are springs of potable water. And a river issues from them, Dracon by name, which flows very close to the city of Nuceria. On either side of this river both sides then encamped.

[250631] 35.8. And the Dracon is in its stream small, yet not passable either for horsemen or for foot-soldiers, since, gathering its current into a narrow space, and cutting the ground as deep as possible on either side, it makes its banks, as it were, overhanging.

[250890] 35.9. Whether the nature of the land or of the water bears the cause of this, I cannot know. And the Goths, having seized the bridge of the river, since they encamped very close to it, and putting wooden towers there and making other machines and the so-called ballistrae there, in order that from there they might be able to strike the enemy from above as they harassed them.

[251268] 35.10. It was impossible, then, for any engagement to take place hand to hand, the river, as I have said, being between; but both sides, coming as close as possible to the bank along it, used arrows for the most part against one another.

[251507] 35.11. And there were also certain single combats, a Gothic man, if it so happened, crossing the bridge on a challenge. And a period of two months was spent by the two camps in this way.

[251695] 35.12. So long, then, as the Goths commanded the sea there, bringing in provisions by ships, they held out, since they were encamped not far from the sea.

[251851] 35.13. But afterwards the Romans took the ships of the enemy by the betrayal of a Gothic man who was set over all the vessels, and countless ships came to them from Sicily and from the rest of the empire.

[252057] 35.14. And at the same time Narses too, setting up wooden towers on the bank of the river, was altogether able to enslave the spirit of his opponents.

[252209] 35.15. At which the Goths, becoming fearful and hard pressed by want of necessities, fled to a mountain which was near, which the Romans call in the Latin tongue Mons Lactarius (the Milk Mountain); where the Romans could in no way follow them, the difficulty of the ground withstanding them.

[252502] 35.16. But the barbarians, having at once gone up there, repented, since they were short of provisions much more, having no means by which to procure them for themselves and their horses.

[252691] 35.17. Wherefore, thinking the overthrow of life in battle preferable to that by famine, they went against the enemy contrary to expectation, and fell upon them suddenly and unexpectedly.

[252880] 35.18. And the Romans, as best they could from the present circumstances, stood to defend themselves, not arranging their array by commanders or companies or registers, nor in any other way distinguished from one another, nor about to listen to the commands given them in the engagement, but arraying themselves against the enemy with all their strength, wherever any chanced to be.

[253264] 35.19. The Goths, then, first dismounting from their horses, all stood on foot, front to front, in a deep phalanx; and the Romans too, seeing this, let their horses go, and were all drawn up in the same manner.

[253476] 35.20. And here a battle worthy of much account, and the valour of a man inferior, I think, to none of those called heroes, shall be recorded by me, which Teias displayed on this occasion.

[253666] 35.21. The Goths were roused to daring by the despair of the present circumstances, while the Romans, though seeing them desperate, withstood them with all their power, ashamed to give way to inferiors; and both went against their neighbours with much fury, the one party courting death,

[253955] 35.22. the other coveting valour. And the battle began in the early morning, and Teias, having become plain to all, holding his shield before him and brandishing his spear, stood first with a few others of the phalanx.

[254175] 35.23. And the Romans, seeing him, and thinking that, if he should fall, the engagement would be broken up for them very straightway, combined against him, all who laid claim to valour, being exceedingly many, and all hurled their spears against him, some thrusting, others throwing them.

[254465] 35.24. And he, covered by his shield, received all the spears upon it, and, charging upon them suddenly, dispatched many.

[254588] 35.25. And whenever he saw his shield full of spears fixed in it, handing it to one of his shield-bearers, he took another.

[254713] 35.26. Fighting thus, he continued through the third part of the day, and then indeed his shield held twelve spears fixed in it, and he could no longer move it where he wished and beat off the assailants.

[254919] 35.27. And he called eagerly for one of his shield-bearers, not leaving his array, nor even so much as to a single finger's breadth, nor giving back, nor leading the enemy forward, nor turning round, nor resting his back upon his shield, nor indeed becoming sideways, but, as if fixed to the ground, he stood there with his shield, killing with his right hand and beating off with his left and calling the name of his shield-bearer.

[255353] 35.28. And he came to him with the shield, and Teias at once exchanged the one weighed down with spears for it.

[255466] 35.29. And in this interval, for a brief point of time, it befell his breast to become bare, and by some chance it befell him then to be struck by a javelin, and from this he died straightway.

[255660] 35.30. And some of the Romans, lifting his head upon a pole, going round to either army, showed it: to the Romans, that they might take more courage, and to the Goths, that, despairing, they might put an end to the war.

[255881] 35.31. Yet not even so did the Goths break off the engagement, but fought until night, although they knew well that their king was dead. And when it grew dark, both sides, parting, passed the night there in the equipment of their arms.

[256118] 35.32. And on the following day, rising at dawn, they arrayed themselves in the same manner again and fought until night, and neither gave way to the other nor in any way turned or drew back, although many were killed on both sides, but, maddened by their pitiless conduct toward one another, they kept at the work, the Goths knowing well that they were carrying on their last battle, and the Romans disdaining to be worsted by them.

[256553] 35.33. At last the barbarians, sending to Narses some of their men of repute, said that they had perceived that their struggle was against God; for they perceived the power arrayed against them; and, putting together what had befallen them, they understood the truth of the matter, and wished thenceforth to abandon contention and to leave the struggle—not, however, to obey the emperor, but to live as independent men together with some of the other barbarians; and they begged the Romans to grant them a peaceful withdrawal, not grudging them a prudent resolve, but even presenting them with their own money by way of journey-provisions, all that each of them had happened to lay up before in the strongholds in Italy.

[257275] 35.34. Narses took these things under counsel. And John the son of Vitalian advised him to accept this request, and not to go on, by battle, against men who courted death, nor to make trial of a boldness that springs from despair of life, which is grievous both to those who hold it and to those who confront them.

[257591] 35.35. "For it suffices," he said, "to the prudent to conquer, and the wish for too much might perhaps turn even to disadvantage."

[257723] 35.36. Narses was persuaded by the advice, and they came to an agreement, on condition that the barbarians who had been left should, taking with them their own money, depart at once from all Italy, and should by no device carry on war against the Romans any longer.

[257990] 35.37. A thousand Goths, then, in the meanwhile, rising up from the camp, went to the city of Ticinum and the regions beyond the river Po, of whom, among others, Indulf was leader, of whom I made mention before.

[258203] 35.38. And all the rest, giving oaths, confirmed all that had been agreed. And thus the Romans took both Cumae and all the rest, and the eighteenth year came to a close in this Gothic war, the history of which Procopius has written.
