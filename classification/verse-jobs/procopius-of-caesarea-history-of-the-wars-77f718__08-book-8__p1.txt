[110633] 15.14. But they said that this agreement had been made while Lazica was most securely under the rule of the Persians, so that no one should trouble them for five years, but they might more fearlessly and with less toil dwell in all the fairest parts of the land of Colchis during this time.

[110925] 15.15. Wherefore the Romans would have no device for all time to drive them out from there thenceforth, but Byzantium too would be easy of approach from there for the Persians henceforth. Considering these things, then, the multitude were grieved and, distressed, were at a loss;

[111206] 15.16. and also that the Persians had now most strongly secured, in the name of the truce, what from of old had been their eager aim, but which had seemed impossible to attain either by war or by any other means—I mean, that the Romans should be their tributaries through the payment of tribute.

[111503] 15.17. For Chosroes, having fixed a yearly tribute of four centenaria upon the Romans, of which from the beginning he had been plainly grasping, had now for eleven years and six months, under a specious pretext, received forty-six centenaria on the plea of the truce, giving the name of treaty to the tribute, although meanwhile he was using force and making war over Lazica, as has been said.

[111898] 15.18. From which the Romans had no hope thenceforth of redeeming themselves in the time to come, but perceived that they had become tributaries to the Persians, not secretly.

[112075] 15.19. These things, then, had been done thus. And Isdigousnas, having gathered to himself more money than any envoy ever, and having become, I think, the richest of all the Persians, was carried off home, since the emperor Justinian had honoured him exceedingly and, presenting him with great sums, sent him away.

[112391] 15.20. And he alone of all envoys came to the trial of no surveillance, but he himself and all the barbarians who followed him, being exceedingly many, were for a length of time in great liberty to meet and consort with whomever they wished, and to make walks everywhere about the city, and to buy and sell whatever they wished, and to make all contracts and to busy themselves with the trade about these things in all freedom, as if in their own city, no Roman following or accompanying them at all or thinking fit to watch them, as was the custom.

[112942] 15.21. In this time there happened to occur a thing of those that had not happened before, so far as we know. For it was autumn in the year, but there came a drought and a sultriness as in midsummer, wondrous how great; so that, in fact, a multitude of roses sprang up as if it were spring, differing in nothing at all from the usual.

[113278] 15.22. And almost all the trees bore some new fruits again, and in the vines no fewer clusters formed, although the vintage had taken place not many days before.

[113441] 15.23. From these things those skilled in such matters, drawing their conclusions, foretold that something great and unexpected would happen, some saying good, others the contrary.

[113623] 15.24. But I think that these things happened by some chance, south winds for the most part, as is usual, having fallen, and from this much heat, contrary to custom and not in accordance with the nature of the season, having come upon the country.

[113872] 15.25. But if it also signifies, as these men say, something that will happen contrary to expectation, we shall know most surely from the events.

[114019] 16.1. And while these things concerning the treaty were being done in Byzantium between the Romans and the Persians, meanwhile in Lazica the following happened.

[114181] 16.2. Gubazes the king of the Lazi was favourably disposed toward the Romans, since he had perceived that Chosroes was plotting his death, as I have said in the earlier books.

[114358] 16.3. But most of the other Lazi, suffering irreparable terrible things from the Roman soldiers, and exceedingly vexed with the commanders of the army, for the most part inclined to the Medes—not that they welcomed the things of the Persians, but wishing to be rid of the rule of the Romans, and choosing, of the troubles, those that for the time being were not present.

[114730] 16.4. And there was a certain not undistinguished man among the Lazi, Theophobius by name, who, conferring most secretly with Mermeroes, agreed to hand over the fort of Uthimereos.

[114912] 16.5. And he, raising the man with great hopes, set him in motion toward this enterprise, maintaining that from this deed he would become most dear to the king Chosroes, and recorded among the Persians for his benefaction for all time, and that from this he would become great in fame and wealth and power. Lifted up by which, Theophobius hastened much the more to the deed.

[115288] 16.6. And there was at that time no intercourse between the Romans and the Lazi, but the Persians went about in great liberty everywhere among the regions there, while of the Romans and the Lazi some lay hid beside the river Phasis, others, having seized Archaeopolis or some other of the strongholds there, kept hidden; and Gubazes himself, the king of the Lazi, remained quiet in the heights of the mountains.

[115701] 16.7. With no toil, then, Theophobius was able to fulfil his promise to Mermeroes. For, coming into the fort, he told the Lazi and Romans who held the garrison there that the whole Roman army had been destroyed, and that the affairs of the king Gubazes and of all the Lazi about him had been ruined, and that all Colchis was held by the Persians, and that there was no hope at all for the Romans or for Gubazes ever to recover the mastery of the country.

[116157] 16.8. For formerly Mermeroes had accomplished this by himself alone, bringing more than seventy thousand Persian fighting men and a great multitude of barbarian Sabeiri; but now the king Chosroes too, coming there with an unspeakable army, had suddenly joined them, and the land of the Colchians itself would no longer suffice this army.

[116496] 16.9. Inventing these portentous tales, Theophobius brought the guards there into great fear and helplessness.

[116608] 16.10. And they entreated him, beseeching him by their ancestral God, to settle the present situation as well as he could for them.

[116741] 16.11. And he agreed to obtain for them from Chosroes pledges concerning their safety, on condition that they should hand over the fort to the Persians. And when this pleased the men, departing thence at once and coming again into the presence of Mermeroes, he told everything.

[117020] 16.12. And Mermeroes, picking out the most approved men among the Persians, sent them with him to Uthimereos, both to give pledges concerning their money and their safety to the guards there, and to hold this fort.

[117236] 16.13. Thus the Persians held the fort of Uthimereos and most firmly secured the mastery of Lazica.

[117337] 16.14. And not only this part of Lazica did the Persians make subject, but also Scymnia and Suania, and from Mocheresis as far as Iberia all the regions there became, in this way, impassable for the Romans and for the king of the Lazi.

[117574] 16.15. And neither the Romans nor the Lazi were able to defend against the enemy, since they did not even dare to come down from the mountains or the strongholds, nor in any way to go out against the enemy.

[117782] 16.16. And Mermeroes, the season of winter pressing, built a wooden wall in Cotais, and, setting there a garrison of no fewer than three thousand of the warlike Persians, and leaving sufficient men in Uthimereos,

[117996] 16.17. and rebuilding also the other fort of the Lazi, which they call Sarapanis, lying at the very last borders of Lazica, remained there.

[118137] 16.18. And afterwards, learning that the Romans and the Lazi were gathering and encamping about the outlets of the river Phasis, he went against them with all his army.

[118307] 16.19. When Gubazes and the commanders of the Roman army learned this, not withstanding the onset of the enemy, they dispersed and saved themselves as each was able.

[118474] 16.20. And Gubazes, running up to the heights of the mountains, passed the winter together with his children and his wife and his closest intimates, struggling on the one hand against the necessity arising from the season by the helplessness of the present evils, but, on the other, taking courage from the hope of Byzantium for the time to come, and thus consoling the fortune then present, such as are human affairs,

[118894] 16.21. and awaiting better things. And the other Lazi too, out of reverence for the king Gubazes, none the less wore out the season of winter in the crags, fearing no trouble there from the enemy, since for those plotting against them, especially during the winter, these mountains happen to be impossible and altogether inaccessible, but dying a hard death from famine and cold and other suffering.

[119295] 16.22. But Mermeroes built many houses at will in the villages of Mocheresis, and, establishing provisions everywhere among the regions there, and sending some of the deserters around to the summits of the mountains and giving pledges, was able to win over many; to whom indeed, when they lacked necessities, he supplied many things, and he cared for them as his own, and managed all other matters in great freedom, as one who had become master of the country.

[119757] 16.23. And to Gubazes he wrote thus: "Two things govern the life of men: power and prudence. For those who excel their neighbours in power both live themselves at will and ever lead the weaker where they wish; while those who, through weakness, are enslaved to the stronger, healing their want of power by their understanding, court the rulers with flattery, and none the less are able to live among their own people, enjoying by flattery all those things of which, through their weakness, it befalls them to be deprived.

[120280] 16.24. And this is so not among some nations, while among others it is otherwise, but with men, so to speak, all, everywhere on the inhabited earth, it is, as it were, implanted by nature.

[120470] 16.25. And you too, then, dear Gubazes, if you think to get the better of the Persians in war, neither delay nor let anything be an obstacle to you.

[120620] 16.26. For you will find us withstanding your attack wherever you wish in Lazica, and arrayed against you on behalf of this country with all our power; so that it will be in your power, contending, to play the man against us.

[120847] 16.27. If, however, being unable to set yourself against the power of the Persians, you yourself too know it, then, good sir, practise the second course, the 'Know thyself,' and do obeisance to your own master Chosroes as king and conqueror and lord.

[121099] 16.28. And ask him to be gracious to you for what you have done, in order that henceforth you may be able to be rid of the evils that trouble you.

[121247] 16.29. For I undertake that the king Chosroes will both be gracious to you and give you pledges, providing you with hostages, the children of the notable rulers among the Persians, so that, with all else, you may have your safety and your kingship secure for all time.

[121517] 16.30. But if neither of these is your wish, then do you, departing into some other country, give the Lazi, who have become wretched through your imprudence, a chance to breathe at some time and to recover from the troubles that beset them, and do not wish to bring upon them this endless destruction, riding upon a treacherous hope—I mean the help of the Romans.

[121882] 16.31. For they will never be able to defend you, just as down to this day they have not been able to." Mermeroes wrote these things.

[122017] 16.32. But not even so did he persuade Gubazes, who remained in the hills of the mountains, awaiting the help from the Romans and, out of his hatred toward Chosroes, by no means willing to incline to despair of the Romans.

[122241] 16.33. For men for the most part fit their thinking to the need of their wish, and ever incline to the argument that pleases them, and accept all things from it, not examining whether it be false; while to what grieves them, being ill-disposed, they refuse belief, not searching whether it be true.

[122541] 17.1. About this time some of the monks who had come from the Indians, learning that it was the eager aim of the emperor Justinian that the Romans should no longer buy silk from the Persians, came into the emperor's presence and promised so to settle the matter of the silk that the Romans should no longer make this purchase either from the Persians, their enemies, or from any other nation;

[122935] 17.2. for they had spent a length of time in a country which lies above the many Indian nations, which is named Serinda, and there had learned accurately by what device silk could be produced in the land of the Romans.

[123155] 17.3. And when the emperor inquired most assiduously and questioned whether the account were true, the monks said that certain worms were the makers of the silk, nature being their teacher and continually compelling them to work.

[123386] 17.4. But to bring the worms here alive was impossible, while their breeding was easy and altogether simple. And the breeding of these worms was eggs, countless from each.

[123559] 17.5. And these eggs, a long time after the breeding, men cover with dung, and, having warmed them for a sufficient time, make them into living creatures.

[123715] 17.6. When they had said this, the emperor, promising to reward the men with great benefits, persuaded them to confirm their word by deed.

[123855] 17.7. And they, going to Serinda again, brought the eggs to Byzantium, and, contriving that they should turn into worms in the way described, fed them on the leaves of the mulberry, and from this they established that thenceforth silk should be made in the land of the Romans.

[124133] 17.8. At that time, then, the affairs of the war for the Romans and the Persians, and those concerning the silk, stood thus.

[124259] 17.9. And after the season of winter, Isdigousnas, coming to Chosroes with the money, reported the agreement made between them. And he, having received the money, with no delay set his seal upon the truce, but was by no means willing to give up Lazica.

[124513] 17.10. But, with this very money making allies of a great multitude of the Sabeiri Huns, he at once sent them with certain Persians to Mermeroes. To him he gave orders to set about the work with all his might, and indeed he sent him also many elephants.

[124768] 17.11. And Mermeroes, with all the army of Persians and Huns, rising from Mocheresis, went against the strongholds of the Lazi, bringing the elephants.

[124921] 17.12. But the Romans in no way confronted him, but, about the outlets of the river Phasis, with Martin leading them, fortifying themselves most safely by the strength of the place, remained quiet.

[125120] 17.13. And with them was also Gubazes the king of the Lazi. But this Median army, some chance befalling it, did no harm to any of the Romans or the Lazi.

[125275] 17.14. For at first Mermeroes, learning that the sister of Gubazes was in a certain fort, led the army against it, to take it by every device.

[125419] 17.15. But, the guards there defending most stoutly and the strength of the place by its nature aiding them, the barbarians, beaten off from there without accomplishing anything, withdrew; then they hastened against the Abasgi.

[125648] 17.16. But the Romans in Tzibile, holding the garrison and seizing the passage there, which was, as I have said before, exceedingly narrow and precipitous and altogether without exit, became an obstacle to them.

[125861] 17.17. Wherefore Mermeroes, having no way to force those who withstood him, led the army back and went to Archaeopolis, intending to besiege it at once. And, making trial of the circuit-wall, since nothing went forward, he turned back again.

[126104] 17.18. And the Romans, following after the enemy as they withdrew, killed many in the difficult ground, among whom it befell the commander of the Sabeiri to fall.

[126268] 17.19. And a fierce battle taking place about the corpse afterwards, the Persians, forcing their opponents about lamp-lighting time, routed them, and withdrew to Cotais and Mocheresis. These things, then, had been done thus by the Romans and the Persians.

[126525] 17.20. But the affairs in Libya all stood well and fair for the Romans. For John, whom the emperor Justinian appointed general there, met with successes greater than word and hearing.

[126710] 17.21. For he, making one of the rulers among the Moors his ally, Cutzinas by name, first conquered the others in battle, and not long after made Antalas and Iaudas, who held the power of the Moors in Byzacium and Numidia, subjects, and they followed him in the rank of slaves.

[126989] 17.22. And from this no enemy arose against the Romans in this time, at least in Libya. But by the wars and seditions that had gone before, the country remained for the most part empty of men.

[127183] 18.1. And while these things were being done thus, meanwhile the following happened in Europe. The Gepids, as I have said in the earlier books, happened to have made a treaty with the Lombards, who were their enemies.

[127402] 18.2. But, being altogether unable to settle their differences with them, they supposed that they would have to make war not long after.

[127540] 18.3. The Gepids, then, and the Lombards went in full force against one another, eager for war.

[127637] 18.4. And the leader of the Gepids was Thorisin, and of the Lombards a man named Audoin, and each of them was followed by many myriads of men. Now the two armies had already come very close to one another, but the camps did not yet see each other.

[127886] 18.5. But the so-called panic fears, falling suddenly upon both, carried them all off in flight backward from no cause, only the commanders being left there with a few men.

[128060] 18.6. And these, attempting to draw them back and to check their retreat, accomplished nothing, using neither piteous flatteries nor fearful threats.

[128211] 18.7. Audoin, then, becoming exceedingly afraid, seeing them dispersing thus in disorder (for he did not know that the enemy had been allotted the like fortune), at once sent some of his followers on an embassy to his opponents to ask for peace.

[128458] 18.8. And they, when, coming to Thorisin the leader of the Gepids, they saw what was being done, perceived from what had happened among themselves what had befallen the enemy, and, coming into the presence of Thorisin, inquired of him where in the world was the multitude of his subjects.

[128748] 18.9. And he, denying nothing of what had befallen, said, "They flee, with no one pursuing." And they, taking it up, said, "This same thing the Lombards too have suffered. For, since you speak the truth, O king, we will hide nothing of our affairs.

[128998] 18.10. Therefore, since it is by no means the will of God that these nations be destroyed, and from this He broke up the battle-array, casting upon both a saving fear, come, let us too yield to the will of God, putting an end to the war." "So be it, let these things be done," said Thorisin. And so they made a truce of two years,

[129330] 18.11. in order that, treating with one another meanwhile and going to and fro continually, they might accurately settle all their differences. At that time, then, both withdrew thus.

[129515] 18.12. But during this truce, being unable to come to agreement with one another about the matters in dispute, they were about to go again to deeds of war.

[129672] 18.13. And the Gepids, fearing the Roman empire (for it was likely that the Romans would draw up with the Lombards), planned to bring some of the Huns into alliance.

[129839] 18.14. They sent, then, to the rulers of the Cutrigurs, who are settled on the near side of the Maeotic Lake, and begged them to share in carrying through the war against the Lombards.

[130025] 18.15. And they at once sent them twelve thousand men, whom, among others, Chinialon led, a man exceptionally good in matters of war.

[130160] 18.16. But the Gepids, vexed at present by the presence of these barbarians, since the season of battle was not yet at hand, but a year still remained to the truce, persuaded them to overrun, meanwhile, the country of the emperor, making their plot against the Romans a by-product of their own untimely occasion.

[130474] 18.17. And since the Romans were guarding accurately the crossing of the river Ister in Illyria and in the regions of Thrace, the Gepids themselves ferried these Huns across the river Ister in the country about themselves and let them loose into the homes of the Romans.

[130746] 18.18. And these plundered almost all the regions there, while the emperor Justinian devised this. Sending to the rulers of the Utigur Huns, who are settled beyond the Maeotic Lake, he reproached them and called their inactivity toward the Cutrigurs unjust, if indeed to look on while one's friends are being destroyed must be counted among the most unjust of deeds. For their neighbours the Cutrigurs,

[131150] 18.19. he said, they had disregarded, and that too while receiving great sums of money from Byzantium every year, in no way willing to cease from their wrongdoing toward the Romans, but day by day overrunning and plundering them without reason.

[131396] 18.20. And of these things they themselves gained no share, nor had any part of the booty allotted to them from the Cutrigurs, yet they took no thought of the Romans being ill-used, though they had been their friends most exceedingly from of old.

[131644] 18.21. Having signified these things to the Utigurs, the emperor Justinian, both presenting them with money and reminding them of how many gifts they had often received from him before, persuaded them to make at once an attack upon the Cutrigurs who had been left behind.

[131917] 18.22. And they, bringing two thousand of the Goths, their neighbours, who are called Tetraxitae, into alliance, crossed the river Tanais in full force.

[132071] 18.23. And their leader was Sandil, a man most sagacious and experienced in many wars, and sufficiently endowed for strength and manliness.

[132212] 18.24. And when they had made the crossing of the river, they came to blows with many of the Cutrigurs who confronted them. And, these defending most stoutly against the assailants, the battle lasted very long, but afterwards the Utigurs, routing their opponents, killed many. And a few, fleeing, as each was able, were saved. And their enemies, enslaving their children and wives, were carried off home.

[132618] 19.1. And while these barbarians were thus fighting one another, as I have said, and the danger was at its height for them in the contest, it befell the Romans to enjoy great good fortune.

[132808] 19.2. For all those of them who happened to be among the Cutrigurs in the rank of captives, coming together, they say, to many myriads, escaping notice in this turmoil and rising up from there in haste, with no one pursuing them, came to their ancestral homes, and reaped the fruit of another's victory in their direst need.

[133134] 19.3. And the emperor Justinian, sending the general Aratius to Chinialon and the other Huns, ordered him to announce what had befallen them in their own land, and, presenting them with money, to persuade them to depart as quickly as possible from the land of the Romans.

[133407] 19.4. And they, learning of the attack of the Utigurs and having received great sums from Aratius, agreed neither to do any further slaughter nor to enslave any of the Romans nor to do any other unkind thing, but to make their withdrawal as through friends, the men there.

[133681] 19.5. And this too was agreed, that if these barbarians should be able, returning to their ancestral land, to settle there, they should remain there and thenceforth keep faith toward the Romans; but if it should be impossible for them to remain there, they should come again into the land of the Romans, and the emperor should present them with certain of the regions of Thrace, on condition that, settling there, they should be in a state of treaty with the Romans for all time and should accurately keep guard of the country from all barbarians.

[134230] 19.6. And already two thousand of the Huns too who had been worsted in the battle and had escaped the Utigurs came into the land of the Romans, bringing their children and wives;

[134410] 19.7. and their leaders were, among others, Sinnion, who had campaigned with Belisarius long before against Gelimer and the Vandals; and they became suppliants of the emperor Justinian. And he received them with all eagerness, and ordered them to settle in the regions of Thrace.

[134691] 19.8. And when Sandil, the king of the Utigurs, learned this, he was provoked and exceedingly angered, that he himself, in punishing the Cutrigurs, who were of his own race, for their wrongdoing toward the Romans, should have removed them from their ancestral homes, while they, the emperor having received them, settling in the land of the Romans, would live far better; and he sent envoys to the emperor to reproach him with what had been done, not putting into their hands any letter (since the Huns are altogether without acquaintance of letters and unpractised in them down to this day, and have neither schoolmaster nor do their children grow up with any toil over letters), but to recite by mouth, in barbarian fashion, all that he charged them with.

[135450] 19.9. Coming, then, into the presence of the emperor Justinian, the envoys said that Sandil bade them say through them, as in a letter, these things: "I know a proverb that I heard from boyhood, and if I have not forgotten any of it,

[135685] 19.10. the proverb runs somewhat thus. The wolf, that beast, may perhaps be able to change something of his coat, they say, but his disposition he does not turn, since nature does not allow him to alter it even though he should refashion himself.

[135933] 19.11. These things," speaking proverbially, says Sandil, "I heard from my elders, who indicated human affairs by a sidelong saying. But I know something too, having learned it from experience, such as it was likely a rustic barbarian should learn:

[136183] 19.12. the shepherds, taking up the dogs while still at the teat, rear them at home not carelessly; and the dog is a creature grateful to those who feed it and most mindful of kindness. This, then, is done by the shepherds for this reason: that, when the wolves at any time come on, the dogs may beat off their attacks, becoming defenders and saviours to the sheep. And these things, I think, happen in every land.

[136599] 19.13. For no one of all men has ever seen either dogs plotting against a flock, or wolves defending it, but nature, having ordained this as a kind of law for dogs and sheep and wolves, has established it.

[136806] 19.14. And I think that even in your kingdom, where for the most part there is an abundance of all things, and perhaps even of the impossible, no alteration of these things takes place at all.

[137000] 19.15. Either, then, show my envoys how I too may learn something of the unusual at the threshold of old age; or, if these things are everywhere fixed by nature, it is not seemly for you, I think, to take into your service the race of the Cutrigurs, troubled as they are, bringing in a fouled neighbour, and to make those whom you would not endure beyond your borders now dwellers within them.

[137395] 19.16. For they themselves will display their proper disposition toward the Romans not long hereafter; and apart from this, neither will an enemy fail to harass the Roman empire, in the hope that, if worsted, he will fare better with you, nor will a friend remain to the Romans, ready at any time to be an obstacle to those overrunning your land, through fear lest, when fortune brings him its best, he should behold the conquered faring more conspicuously than himself among you, if indeed we have our dwellings in a country desolate and otherwise barren, while it is in the Cutrigurs' power to buy grain and to carouse over their wine and to choose all manner of side-dishes.

[138074] 19.17. And surely they have a share of baths too, and these wanderers wear gold and are not without garments fine and embroidered and overlaid with gold. And yet the Cutrigurs,

[138252] 19.18. having before enslaved countless multitudes of Romans, carried them off to their own land.

[138351] 19.19. Over whom to lord it in everything in slavish fashion was no by-task to the accursed wretches, but to lay the lash even upon those who had done no wrong and to put them to death perhaps was ready at hand, and whatever else his disposition and his power permit to a barbarian master.

[138642] 19.20. But we, by our own toils and dangers that touch the soul, freeing them from the fortune that then held them, gave them back to their parents, after they had become for us the spoils of war.

[138840] 19.21. For which the recompense from you we have, each of us, received in opposite kind, if indeed we still enjoy our ancestral ills, while those who, through our valour, escaped enslavement to them, having an equal share of their country,

[139081] 19.22. have their portion." Thus much the envoys of the Utigurs said. But the emperor, after much soothing of them and consoling them with a multitude of gifts, sent them away not long after. These things, then, happened thus.

[139309] 20.1. About this time a war and battle arose between the nation of the Varni and the island soldiers who dwell in the island called Brittia, for the following reason.

[139477] 20.2. The Varni are settled above the river Ister, and they extend as far as the northern Ocean and the river Rhine, which separates them and the Franks and the other nations settled there.

[139668] 20.3. All these, who anciently dwelt about the river Rhine on either side, each had a name of their own, but in common they were all called Germans.

[139818] 20.4. And the island Brittia lies in this Ocean, not far from the shore, but at a distance of about two hundred stades, opposite the outlets of the Rhine for the most part, and it is between Britain and the island of Thule.

[140043] 20.5. For Britain lies toward the setting sun off the farthest part of the land of the Spaniards, distant from the mainland not less than about four thousand stades, while Brittia lies off the back parts of Gaul, the parts which are turned toward the Ocean—that is, toward the north wind, clearly, from Spain and Britain.

[140366] 20.6. And Thule, so far as men know, lies in the farthest parts of the Ocean toward the north. But the matters concerning Britain and Thule have been told by me in the earlier books; while the island Brittia three exceedingly populous nations hold, and one king is set over each of them.

[140655] 20.7. And the names of these nations are the Angiloi and the Frissones and those of the same name as the island, the Brittones.

[140784] 20.8. And so great does the populousness of these nations appear to be, that every year, removing from there in great numbers with their wives and children, they go to the Franks.

[140965] 20.9. And these settle them in the part of their own land that seems to be more deserted, and from this they say they lay claim to the island.

[141109] 20.10. So that, in fact, not long ago the king of the Franks, sending some of his intimates on an embassy to the emperor Justinian in Byzantium, sent along with them men of the Angiloi, vaunting himself that this island too is ruled by him. Such, then, are the matters concerning the island called Brittia.

[141609] 20.12. [142064] 20.14. And whether he understood the bird's voice, or knew it by some other means but feigned to understand the prophesying bird, he at once said to those present that he would die forty days later.

[142264] 20.15. For this, he said, the bird's prediction made plain to him. [142571] 20.16. [144646] 20.23. For chastity is so highly esteemed among these barbarians, that, the mere name of marriage having occurred among them, even though the act did not follow, the woman seems to have been violated.

[145349] 20.27. And she brought along too one of her own brothers, to help her settle the present affairs, not, however, as holding the kingship, but as still ranking in the position of a private man.

[145659] 20.29. For they are not only unpractised in riding, but they do not even know what a horse is, since a horse in this island they do not even behold in a picture; for this creature never appears to have existed in Brittia.

[145882] 20.30. [148630] 20.41. And when these things pleased the maiden, Radigis was loosed from his bonds and was thought worthy of all other kindness, and he at once sent away the sister of Theudibert and married the Brittian woman. These things went forward thus.

[148874] 20.42. And in this island of Brittia the men of old built a long wall, cutting off a great part of it; and the air and the soil and all else are not alike on the two sides of the wall.

[149060] 20.43. For the parts of the wall toward the rising sun there is a healthfulness of air, changing with the seasons, in summer moderately warm, cold in winter;

[150001] 20.47. But, having come to this point in my history, I am bound to mention an account most like a legend, which to me seemed by no means trustworthy at all, although it is forever brought out by countless men who maintained that they themselves had been the doers of the deeds and the hearers of the words; yet it is not to be passed over altogether, lest, in recording the matters of the island Brittia, I should win the reputation of a perpetual ignorance of what happens there.

[151174] 20.50. The men there say that the escort of the souls is laid upon them in turn. Those, then, who on the coming night must go to this task by the succession of the service, these, as soon as it grows dark, withdrawing to their own houses, sleep, awaiting the conductor of the matter.

[151459] 20.51. And in the dead of night they perceive their doors being knocked, and they hear a certain unseen voice calling them together to the work.

[151605] 20.52. And they themselves, with no hesitation, rising from their beds, go to the shore, not understanding what necessity leads them to this, but nevertheless compelled.

[151776] 20.53. And there they see boats made ready, altogether empty of men—not their own, however, but certain others—into which, embarking, they lay hold of the oars.

[151938] 20.54. And they perceive that the barges are weighed down with a multitude of passengers, sunk by the surge to the very edges of the planks and to the place of the oar-locks, lacking of the water no more than a single finger; yet they themselves see no one, but, rowing a single hour, they put in to Brittia.

[152248] 20.55. And yet, when they sail in their own boats, not using sails but rowing, they scarcely cross there in a night and a day. [152610] 20.56. And they themselves see no man, either sailing with them or departing from the ship, but they say they hear a certain voice from there seeming to announce to those who receive them each one of those who have sailed with them, by name, reciting the dignities they formerly enjoyed and calling them by their fathers' names.

[152940] 20.57. And if women too happen to have crossed with them, they recite the names of the men with whom they lived as wives.

[153063] 20.58. These things, then, the men there say happen. But I return to the former account.

[153757] 21.3. But even so all yielded the first place to him, ashamed to use the law against his merit and to gird themselves with the right derived from it.

[153908] 21.4. And these things pleased the emperor exceedingly. But John, the son of Vitalian, was wintering at Salona. [154395] 21.6. But the emperor hindered him, and ordered him to remain there until Narses the eunuch should arrive. For he had determined to appoint him commander-in-chief of this war.

[154572] 21.7. But for what reason these things were the emperor's wish became plainly clear to none of all men; for a counsel of the emperor is impossible to learn unless it be of his own free will. But what men, suspecting, said, I shall make plain.

[154816] 21.8. A thought came to the emperor Justinian that the other commanders of the Roman army would by no means be willing to obey John, not thinking fit to be in any way inferior to him in rank.

[155009] 21.9. And from this he feared lest, dissenting in opinion or playing the malingerer through envy, they should throw the operations into confusion.

[155157] 21.10. And I heard also this account, related by a Roman man, when I was staying at Rome; and this man was of the senatorial council.

[155811] 21.13. For there are in this place many statues, works of these two men. And there too is another work of Pheidias; for so the letters on the statue declare.

[155970] 21.14. There too is the heifer of Myron. For it had been the care of the Romans of old to make all the fairest things of Hellas the adornments of Rome.

[156123] 21.15. And he said that one bull of those then passing by, leaving the herd and mounting upon this fountain, stood above the bronze ox.

[156260] 21.16. And by some chance a passer-by, a Tuscan by race, who seemed to be altogether a rustic, putting together what was being done, said (for there are diviners among the Tuscans even down to my time) that a eunuch would one day overthrow the ruler of Rome.

[157069] 21.20. Narses, then, having received an army worthy of mention and great sums of money from the emperor, was sent forth.

[157592] 22.1. And while John was awaiting Narses at Salona, and Narses, hindered by the attack of the Huns, was going rather slowly, meanwhile Totila, awaiting the army of Narses, did the following.

[157784] 22.2. He established other Romans, and some of those from the senatorial council, in Rome, leaving the rest in Campania.

[157906] 22.3. And he ordered them to take care of the city as far as possible, showing that he repented of what he had earlier done to Rome, since he had happened to burn much of it, especially beyond the river Tiber.

[158117] 22.4. But they, being in the position of captives and stripped of all money—not only of the public funds, but even of their own private property—were unable to take charge of it.

[158512] 22.6. For even though they had been barbarized for a long age, they preserved the buildings of the city and most of its adornments, all that could, through the goodness of their making, hold out against so great a length of time and against neglect.

[158763] 22.7. And moreover, whatever memorials of the race were still left, among them too the ship of Aeneas, the founder of the city, lies even to this day, a sight altogether incredible.

[158946] 22.8. For, making a ship-house in the middle of the city, beside the bank of the Tiber, and laying it up there, from that time they have kept it. And what kind of thing it is, I, having seen it myself, go on to tell.

[159164] 22.9. This ship is a single-banked one and exceedingly long, in length one hundred and twenty feet, and in breadth twenty-five, and in height it is such that it is not impossible to row it.

[159355] 22.10. And there is no glueing of timbers at all there, nor by any other device of iron pins are the timbers of the vessel fastened to one another, but all are of a single piece, beyond word and hearing, and produced, so far as we know, in this ship alone.

[159613] 22.11. For the keel, being of one growth, extends from the very stern to the prow, gradually and wonderfully receding into the hollow, and again from there, by good proportion, rising up to the straight and extended.

[159831] 22.12. And all the thick timbers fitted into the keel (which the poets call dryochi, and others call nomeis) extend each one from one side of the ship to the other side.

[160002] 22.13. And these too, settling down from each end, make a curve exceedingly handsome, so that the ship has been rounded out to be most hollow—whether nature, in accordance with the necessity of use, having cut the timbers and fitted them together beforehand, made this curvature, or whether by hand-wrought craft and some other device the bending of the ribs was contrived in due fashion.

[160392] 22.14. And besides these, each plank extends from the very stern to the other end of the ship, being of a single piece and having taken on iron pins for this reason only, that, fitted to the beams, it may make the side.

[160613] 22.15. Thus this ship, having been made, presents a sight beyond words, since the nature of things always sets before men those of its works that occur most contrary to reason as not easy to describe, but, surpassing the customary by its contrivances, prevails over speech too.

[160892] 22.16. And of these timbers none either has rotted or shows any sign of being decayed, but the ship, being sound everywhere, as though just now built by its craftsman, whoever he was, is strong even to my time, a marvel how great. [161184] 22.17. But Totila, manning some three hundred long ships of the Goths, ordered them to go to Hellas, charging them to plunder all whom they fell in with, with all their power.

[161484] 22.19. For in this passage there happens to be no inhabited island from the strait at Charybdis as far as Corcyra, so that often, when I have been there, I have been at a loss as to where in the world the island of Calypso could be.

[161718] 22.20. For nowhere in this sea have I seen an island, except three, not far from the Phaeacian land, but at a distance of about three hundred stades, lying very close to one another, exceedingly small and having neither dwellings of men nor of beasts nor anything else at all. Othonoi are these islands now called.

[162298] 22.22. For to fit the true account accurately to the most ancient matters is not easy, since the great length of time is wont for the most part to change both the names of places and the opinion concerning them.

[162728] 22.24. And yet this vessel is not of a single piece, but is composed of very many stones.

[202307] 27.13. And of this army, among others, Aratius and Rhecithancus and Leonianus and Arimuth were commanders, who happened to have been riding the whole day.

[202463] 27.14. And coming to a wooded place about lamp-lighting time, they halted, intending to bivouac and pass the night there.

[202586] 27.15. These commanders, then, gave orders to the soldiers, among other things, to take care of their own horses and to refresh themselves beside the river flowing there, soothing the weariness of the road.

[202794] 27.16. And they themselves, each taking three or four bodyguards along, secretly drank of the river; for they were held, as was likely, by grievous thirst.

[202951] 27.17. And those about Goar and Ildigisal, being somewhere near and having sent scouts, learned these things. And, standing over them unexpectedly as they drank, they killed them all, and thenceforth more fearlessly arranged the matters of their journey as they wished.

[203222] 27.18. For the soldiers, having become without commanders, were at a loss and, altogether helpless, marched back. Goar, then, and Ildigisal, escaping thus, came to the Gepids.

[203399] 27.19. And there happened to be also a certain man of the Gepids, Ustrigotthus by name, who had fled to the Lombards in the following manner. Elemundus, who had been king of the Gepids, had not long before vanished from among men by sickness, and, only this Ustrigotthus, his son, being left, whom Thorisin, having done violence to him (for he was still a stripling), held the rule.

[203783] 27.20. Wherefore the boy, having no way to avenge himself upon the wrongdoer, rising up from his ancestral homes, went off to the Lombards, who were enemies of the Gepids.

[203956] 27.21. And there came to be a little later between the Gepids and the emperor Justinian and the nation of the Lombards a reconciliation, and they bound one another by most terrible oaths that they would thenceforth preserve an endless friendship toward one another.

[204223] 27.22. And when the matters of the reconciliation had been most surely accomplished for them, the emperor Justinian and Audoin the leader of the Lombards, sending to Thorisin the ruler of the Gepids, demanded Ildigisal back as a common enemy, asking him to make the betrayal of his own suppliant a first display of his friendship toward them.

[204567] 27.23. And he, conferring with the men of repute among the Gepids about the present matter, eagerly inquired whether he should do what was asked by the two kings.

[204731] 27.24. But they outright forbade it, maintaining that it was better for the nation of the Gepids to perish very straightway, women and all offspring, than to become unholy by such an impiety.

[204924] 27.25. Hearing this, Thorisin fell into helplessness. For he could neither, his subjects being unwilling, fulfil the deed, nor was he willing to renew war, which had been ended with much toil and time, against the Romans and the Lombards. Afterwards, however, he devised the following.

[205211] 27.26. Sending to Audoin, he demanded back the son of Elemundus, Ustrigotthus, urging him to a like sin to his own, and calling for an exchange of the betrayal of the suppliants.

[205391] 27.27. For he had hope that, by their shrinking from the like absurdity, he would check their demand; but Audoin would, with no delay, seize him by lawlessness and by the agreement.

[205574] 27.28. And, these things being decided by them, knowing well that neither the Lombards nor the Gepids were willing to take a share of the pollution among themselves, they did nothing openly, but each by craft killed the other's enemy.

[205810] 27.29. In what manner, however, I forbear to say; for the accounts about them do not agree with one another, but differ greatly, as is the way with the most secret of matters. The matters concerning Ildigisal and Ustrigotthus, then, ended thus.

[206056] 28.1. And to those about Narses, when they came to the city of Ravenna, there were joined Valerian and Justin the generals, and whatever other Roman army had been left there.

[206232] 28.2. And when a period of nine days had been spent by them in Ravenna, Usdrilas, a Goth, exceptionally good in matters of war, commander of the garrison in Ariminum, wrote to Valerian thus:

[206424] 28.3. "Having seized everything with rumours, and having now held all Italy with phantoms, and putting on airs beyond what is human, and having thus, as you suppose, frightened the Goths, you then sit now in Ravenna, in order, I think, to be as little plain as possible to the enemy by hiding yourselves—still keeping up this spirit—but with a motley throng of barbarians wearing out a country that belongs to you from no quarter. But rise up as quickly as possible and lay hold of deeds of war at last, and show yourselves to the Goths, and do not hang us up with longer hopes,

[207004] 28.4. awaiting the spectacle from of old." The letter declared this much. When Narses saw this, brought to him, laughing at the boastfulness of the Goths, he at once set about going out with the whole army, leaving a guard with Justin in Ravenna.

[207252] 28.5. And, coming near the city of Ariminum, they found the passage from there not easy for them, the Goths having destroyed the bridge there not long before.

[207412] 28.6. For the river which flows past Ariminum is scarcely passable for a single unarmed man going on foot over the bridge with toil and much hardship, and that too with no one harassing him or checking the passage; but for a multitude of men, and that too exceptionally armed, especially with enemies withstanding them, to cross there in any way is impossible.

[207774] 28.7. Wherefore Narses, coming to be at the place of the bridge with a few men, at a loss for a long time, considered from where he might find some means for the matter.

[207945] 28.8. Thither too Usdrilas came, bringing along certain horsemen, lest any of the operations should escape his notice. And one of those who followed Narses, drawing his bow, shot at them, and, hitting one of the horses, straightway killed it.

[208189] 28.9. And those about Usdrilas then, departing quickly from there, came within the circuit-wall, and at once, bringing along others of their most warlike men, rushed against them through another gate, in order to fall upon them unexpectedly and dispatch Narses very straightway.

[208469] 28.10. For he had already come to the other side of the river, searching out with the army the crossing. But some of the Heruls, by some chance meeting them there, killed Usdrilas, and, whoever he was, recognized by a Roman man, taking off his head, came to the Roman camp, and, showing it to Narses, encouraged all by their eagerness, who took from what had befallen the proof that the things from God were hostile to the Goths; since they who had laid an ambush for the general of the enemy had themselves, not by plot or any forethought, suddenly been deprived of their commander.

[209054] 28.11. But Narses, although Usdrilas had fallen, who commanded the garrison in Ariminum, pressed the army forward. For he wished to harass neither Ariminum nor any other place held by the enemy, so that no time should be wasted for him, nor the most urgent business be hindered by an incidental matter.

[209358] 28.12. And the enemy, since their commander had fallen, keeping quiet and no longer setting themselves as an obstacle, Narses, more fearlessly bridging the river, ferried his whole army across with no toil.

[209566] 28.13. And, leaving the Flaminian road from there, he went on the left. For Petra, the so-called Pertusa, the strength of whose stronghold by the nature of the place I have described in the earlier books, having been seized by the enemy long before, the regions there, at least along the Flaminian road, happened to be impassable for the Romans and altogether without exit. Narses, then, on this account, leaving the shorter road, went by the passable one.

[210024] 29.1. For the Roman army, then, the matters of the journey were thus. But Totila, having now learned what had happened in Venetia, awaiting at first Teias and the army with him, remained quietly in the regions about Rome.

[210247] 29.2. But when they were present, and only two thousand horsemen were still left, Totila did not wait for these, but, setting out with all the rest of the army, went to confront the enemy at a suitable place.

[210457] 29.3. And on this road, learning what had befallen Usdrilas and that the enemy had crossed Ariminum, he passed through all Tuscany, and, coming to be on the mountain called the Apennine, encamped there and remained near a village which the natives call Taginae.

[210720] 29.4. And the Roman army, with Narses leading, not long after, encamping themselves too on the mountain Apennine, remained, about a hundred stades distant from the camp of their opponents, in a place level, but encompassed close about with many hills, where once they say Camillus, a general of the Romans, destroyed a host of the Gauls, having conquered them in battle.

[211092] 29.5. And the place bears the name of this deed even to my time, and preserves in memory the calamity of the Gauls, being called Busta Gallorum; for the Latins call the remains from the funeral pyre busta.

[211299] 29.6. And there are here very many earthen mounds, tombs of those dead. At once, then, sending from there some of his intimates, Narses gave orders to make an exhortation to Totila, to lay down war and to take counsel for peace at some time, reckoning that he himself, ruling over a few men, and these gathered hastily by no law, could not contend for long against the whole Roman empire.

[211689] 29.7. And he said this too to them, that he, if they should see him wishing for war, would, with no delay, give command to fix some appointed day for the battle.

[211852] 29.8. When, then, these envoys came into the presence of Totila, they did what was ordered. And he, talking big, made the boast that they must fight in every way; but they, taking it up, said, "But, good sir, set some fixed time for the engagement." And he at once said, "In eight days let us join battle."

[212160] 29.9. The envoys, then, returning to Narses, reported the agreement made with them; and he, suspecting that Totila was contriving deceits, made ready as if to fight on the morrow.

[212341] 29.10. And indeed he hit upon the enemy's thought. For on the following day Totila, of his own announcing, was present with all his army. And already both sides faced one another, distant no more than two bow-shots apart.

[212564] 29.11. Now there was a certain small mound there, which both sides were eager to seize, thinking it to lie in a position suitable for them, both that they might be able to strike their opponents from above, and because, since wooded places, as I have shown, were somewhere there, it was impossible for any to encircle the Roman camp, going behind, except by a single path,

[212938] 29.12. which happened to be beside the mound. Wherefore it was necessary for both to make much account of it, for the Goths, that, encircling the enemy in the engagement, they might place them between two fires, and for the Romans, that they might not suffer this.

[213204] 29.13. But Narses, getting the start, picking out fifty foot-soldiers from a register, sent them in the dead of night to seize and hold it.

[213345] 29.14. And they, with none of the enemy standing in their way, coming to be there, remained quiet.

[213445] 29.15. And there is a certain torrent-bed before the mound, beside the path which I have just mentioned, and opposite the place where the Goths were encamped, where the fifty stood, coming close to one another and drawn up into a phalanx as in a narrow space.

[213706] 29.16. And by day, seeing what had happened, Totila was eager to push them off. And he at once sent a squadron of horsemen against them, ordering them to drive them out from there as quickly as possible.

[213911] 29.17. The horsemen, then, with much tumult and shouting rushed against them, as if to take them at the first onset; but they, drawn up in a small space and fencing themselves with their shields,

[214108] 29.18. holding their spears extended, stood. Then the Goths, advancing eagerly, drove against them in confusion; but the fifty, by the pushing of their shields and the most close-packed and in no way disordered presenting of their spears, beat off the assailants most stoutly, and on purpose made a clatter with their shields, in this way ever frightening the horses, and the men with the points of their spears.

[214522] 29.19. And the horses reared, distressed exceedingly by the difficult ground and the clatter of the shields and having no way out, and the men were worn down, fighting with men packed so close together and yielding in no way, and urging on horses that by no means obeyed. And, beaten off the first time, they retreated backward.

[214852] 29.20. And, making trial again and suffering the same things, they drew back. And often having departed thus, they no longer harassed them, but Totila set another squadron to this work.

[215039] 29.21. And these too departing like the former, others were set to the task. And Totila, having thus changed many squadrons, and after all of them having accomplished nothing, then desisted.

[215231] 29.22. The fifty, then, won great fame for valour, and two of them especially distinguished themselves in this struggle, Paul and Ansilas, who, leaping out from the phalanx, made a display of valour above all others.

[215449] 29.23. For, drawing their daggers, they laid them on the ground, and, drawing their bows, shot, aiming most effectively at the enemy.

[215584] 29.24. And many men and many horses they dispatched, so long as their quivers still had shafts for them. And when all the missiles had now failed them, they, taking up their swords and holding their shields before them, defended themselves singly against the assailants.

[215856] 29.25. And whenever some of their adversaries came riding against them with their spears, they, striking with their swords, at once cut off the points of the spears.

[216023] 29.26. And often, as they thus checked the attacks of the enemy, it befell the sword of one of them (this man's name was Paul) to be bent by continual cutting against the wood and to be altogether useless. Which he at once dashed to the ground,

[216269] 29.27. and, laying hold with both hands of the spears, kept taking them from the assailants. And, taking thus manifestly four spears from the enemy, he became most of all the cause of their giving up the enterprise.

[216486] 29.28. Wherefore Narses thenceforth appointed him his own private shield-bearer for this deed.

[216582] 30.1. These things, then, went thus. And both sides made ready for battle-array. And Narses, gathering the army into a small space, exhorted them as follows: "For those who set themselves to the contest against the enemy with a power equally matched, perhaps there would be need of much exhortation and of urging to eagerness, so that thereby, having the advantage over their opponents, they might come off from the battle-array most according to their wish; but for you, men, for whom both in valour and in numbers and in all other equipment, by a great difference, the battle is against inferiors, I think there is need of nothing else than to enter upon this engagement with God propitious.

[217277] 30.2. Calling Him most assiduously to your alliance by prayer, then, with great contempt go for the mastery against these robbers, who, being slaves of the great emperor from the beginning, and having become runaways, and having set up for themselves a vulgar tyrant out of the rabble, were able, in a more thievish fashion, for a certain occasion, to throw the Roman empire into confusion.

[217669] 30.3. And yet one would suspect that these men, reckoning what was likely, would not now even array themselves against us.

[217793] 30.4. But they, courting death by some senseless boldness and displaying a mad rashness, dare to take up manifest death, not putting forward good hope, nor awaiting what may come to them from the unexpected and the unlooked-for, but being expressly led by God to the punishments of their political deeds. For those who are doomed from above to suffer something go of their own accord to their chastisements.

[218202] 30.5. Apart from this, you set yourselves to this engagement contending for a lawful state, while they struggle on behalf of innovating against the laws, not expecting to hand on anything of their possessions to any successors, but knowing well that everything will perish together with them, and living with a hope that lasts but a day.

[218541] 30.6. So that they are most of all worthy to be despised. For from those who set themselves up not by law and a good state all valour has departed, and victory has been discerned, as is likely, not being wont

[218751] 30.7. to set itself against the virtues." Such an exhortation Narses made. And Totila too, seeing his followers amazed at the Roman army, called them together and himself spoke to them all as follows: "To make to you a last exhortation, fellow-soldiers, I have gathered you here.

[219032] 30.8. For there will, I think, be no need of any other exhortation after this engagement, but the war will altogether be decided in a single day.

[219179] 30.9. For so has it befallen both us and the emperor Justinian to be unnerved and stripped of all our powers, having met with toils and battles and hardships for a vast length of time, and having grown weary of the necessities of war, that, if we get the better of our opponents in the present engagement, they will by no means henceforth recover, but if we stumble in this battle, no hope will be left to the Goths of renewing the fight, but each side will have the defeat sufficiently as a specious pretext for quiet.

[219700] 30.10. For men, despairing of the worst of their affairs, no longer dare to return to them, but even though need, perhaps very strongly, thrust them toward these, they are held back in their resolves, the memory of their evils frightening their souls.

[219953] 30.11. Having heard this much, men, play the man with all your might, putting off the valour of your soul to no other time, and toil with all your strength, not husbanding your bodies for any other danger.

[220160] 30.12. And let there be for you no sparing of arms and horses, as they will no longer be useful to you. For fortune, having worn out all else beforehand, has kept the very head of hope for this day alone.

[220366] 30.13. Practise, then, good courage and prepare yourselves for daring. For those whose hope stands upon a hair, as now yours does, even the briefest moment of time it does not profit to be slack.

[220563] 30.14. For when the height of the opportunity has passed, eagerness thenceforth becomes profitless, even though it be exceedingly excessive, the nature of things not admitting a stale valour, since, the need being past, what comes after must of necessity be out of season too.

[220841] 30.15. I think, then, that it befits you to take the struggle in hand most effectively, that you may also be able to use its good things. And you know well that in the present case flight is most deadly.

[221046] 30.16. For men flee, leaving their array, for no other reason than that they may live; but if flight is likely to bring manifest death, he who has undergone the danger will be much more in safety than the fugitive.
