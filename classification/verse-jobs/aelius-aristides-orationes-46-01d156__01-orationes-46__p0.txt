CHAPTER: 01-orationes-46



[0] 116. I do not know why I should apologize repeatedly for treating the same matters again, the more so when I am about to speak not against men, but on behalf of men who were noble and good, and these not few in number, nor less ancient than Plato — indeed older than he, if anyone thinks even this deserves respect. It has often struck me to wonder, among the other things in the Gorgias, at the accusation which he brought against Miltiades, Themistocles, Pericles, and Cimon, and that he brought it so carelessly and so loosely, in a way that no one would have guessed before hearing the dialogue through. For in the first place the subject itself did not compel it, nor could anyone say that it did. "By Zeus," one might object, "his argument would have collapsed had he not spoken ill of these men." But it was open to him to bring his thesis to its conclusion even without this slander. For if from the very outset he had set himself this aim, and had undertaken to examine those who had taken part in the public life of Athens, then perhaps it would have arisen from the subject to speak of these men as well. But as it is, what does he say, and to what end was he directing the argument? That there are these two ways of caring for the body and the soul: one that consorts with us for pleasure, the other for what is best; and that the one aimed at pleasure is base and is flattery, while the other —

[1409] 117. is serious and noble. So what harm would it have done his argument if these men had not been spoken ill of? And there would have been no greater risk that speaking to please instead of speaking what is best would be reckoned good and useful — especially since Callicles had already given up many times, as he himself shows, and in the end said: "I do not know in what way you seem to me to speak well, Socrates; but I have undergone what most people undergo: I am not altogether persuaded by you." For it certainly cannot be said that he thought he would persuade and win him over any the more by censuring those men. On the contrary, it seems to me that he would rather have exasperated him and produced the opposite effect by his abuse of them, so that in this way, as he himself puts it, he would have scared off the quarry — and that too when he already held and had as good as caught the very thing he wished to demonstrate. One may recognize this from his own words. For as the argument proceeds he says: "Must we then set about treating the city and the citizens with a view to making the citizens themselves as good as possible?" "Certainly, if that is more to your liking," says Callicles. So once it had been agreed and conceded — the very point toward which the whole argument had been driving, and to which all this was being referred — what was the loss in not attacking these men? But as it is, he plainly came to his discussion treacherously, as though he were in labor and were circling round about the road that led against them. And indeed the difference is no small one. For the one thing was to contend on behalf of truth itself, while of the other I myself would never speak; but someone else, putting the worst construction on it, might say that it comes close to malice. And yet how is it not strange that a man so far surpassing others in humanity and greatness of soul should, at least as far as his words go, give a handle for the appearance of envy? I am astonished that comedy may be composed, even if it is not permitted —

[3467] 118. to ridicule by name, while the argument could not be made convincing unless he had spoken ill of certain men by name. For come, in the name of the gods: if he had happened to be born before these men, or, by Zeus, if anyone else among those above them, still earlier, had contended for this same argument, would he have been able to accuse Miltiades and Themistocles and men not yet born? Not at all. Since, then, there was something to say about the subject even apart from any mention of these men, it was not by necessity that they were spoken ill of. That, moreover, what he has said against them is not even true — over and above the fact that it is in no way fitting to slander, even if one were going to refute them most thoroughly — this I shall now try to demonstrate. And first let us examine Pericles, since it was him he undertook to examine first, to see whether the man deserved to hear such things, whether on account of his private conduct or his public life. He, then, is said to have lived so reverently that his life differed in nothing from that of the prophets and priests, and to have been so self-controlled that he walked with measured step and kept to the straight road, as the proverb has it; he chose a middle ordering of life, neither haughty nor servile, just such as Plato's discourse extols in the eldest of the Athenians. And though democratic in his policy and acting on behalf of the multitude — contrary to Thucydides son of Melesias — he kept as far as possible from vulgar rashness and cheapness, so much so that he was never seen by anyone even laughing; rather, by his guardianship of the constitution and by submitting to hold an equal share with the rest, he was as much one of the people as any man could be, while by the dignity of his judgment and by not being mastered by the same things as the many he left it to few to approach near to himself. As for money, he came to be so far superior that — the fact that he even gave up his lands —

[5458] 119. to the city, I pass over, lest anyone say that he did it through fear of slander; for this too seemed to be, as it were, another token of Pericles, his contempt for money. And so he too once, speaking freely before the people and recounting the good qualities that were his, set down among the first of them this very thing, his magnanimity in such matters. So consistent was he in his life and his public conduct, and so far from living to gratify the pleasures of others, that those who have written about him say he held toward the Athenians the position of a teacher toward children; so that often, when Pericles was absent, the assembly was in great disorder and indolence, but the moment he appeared it changed at once and was sobered at the sight of him, as though they feared lest Pericles should learn of their faults. And what they say belonged to Socrates beyond the other sophists, this belonged to him beyond the other popular leaders: that when the people were elated and presumptuous he was most skilled at restraining and humbling them, and when they were dispirited and brought low he could raise them again by his words and fill them with hope, just as Socrates was wont to do with the young. To put it in a word, Pericles was the figurehead of the city — not a slave to the desires of the many, but himself ruling the many; not deigning to say what seemed best to them, but compelling them to do what seemed best to him; not attached to them in the role of a flatterer, but, for his part, nowhere giving the flatterers an opening — in his kindness and equity standing toward the people in the place of a father, but, in confining them all and keeping all beneath himself, exercising more than a tyrant's power. From all this, it seems, one may clearly see that, if any man ever was, Pericles was willingly just. For nowhere did he prize selfish gain above the laws, nor did he provide that he might be greater than his station, though he might have done so more than any —

[7456] 120. Pisistratus; rather he was like one holding the Acropolis for the sake of preserving the laws and doing good impartially to all. And indeed, if you reproach Archelaus as wretched and ill-fated because he acquired his tyranny when it in no way belonged to him, overstepping justice and destroying those whom it was least likely he would, then to the man to whom it was open to be a tyrant in the same way, had he so wished, but who did not choose this and instead held the laws and justice to be of more worth than gain — how was it not fitting to rejoice with him? Now if the argument happened to concern some other matter or doctrine, I myself would not think it necessary to take refuge in witnesses, but I would show on my own how the truth stands. But since an examination lies before us of men who lived long ago — what sort of men they were and what ordering of public life they chose — there is need, Plato, of a witness, even for you who despise witnesses, though you yourself, I think, would never have been caught using none. I shall pass over all the others, though I could name many, and shall produce a single sufficient one: Thucydides son of Olorus, who seems to surpass the historians by far not only in the power and dignity of his language but also in the accuracy of his account of events. Come then, what testimony does he give about Pericles, and what does he say his character was, and how did he lead the people? For his voice would be to us as if we had Pericles himself before our eyes and, in his company, saw what sort of man he was. "For as long as he stood at the head of the city in peace he governed it with moderation and guarded it in safety, and under him it became greatest; and when the war came, here too he is seen to have foreseen its power. He survived two years and six months; and after he died his foresight regarding the war became known still more fully. For he —

[9371] 121. said that they would prevail if they kept quiet, attended to their fleet, did not seek to extend their empire in wartime, and did not put the city at risk. But they did the opposite of all this, and in other matters too, seemingly outside the war, they pursued policies harmful both to themselves and to their allies according to their private ambitions and private gains; which, when they succeeded, brought honor and advantage rather to individuals, but, when they failed, became a damage to the city for the war. The cause was that he, being powerful through his standing and his judgment, and having shown himself conspicuously incorruptible by money, restrained the multitude freely, and was not led by them so much as he himself led them, because he did not speak to please by acquiring power from improper means, but, holding it on his merit, could even speak against them in their anger. At any rate, whenever he perceived them growing over-bold through insolence at the wrong moment, he would strike them down by his words into fear, and when they were again unreasonably afraid he would restore them to confidence. And so it came about that in word it was a democracy, but in fact rule by the first man. But his successors, being more on a level with one another and each grasping to become first, turned to handing affairs over to the people to suit their pleasures; from which many errors arose, as in a great city possessing an empire, and among them the expedition to Sicily." Then upon this the concluding point is just so much: that Pericles' foresight at that time so abounded — from what he himself had foreseen — that the city would very easily have prevailed over the Peloponnesians alone in the war. These things this man recounts concerning Pericles' policy and public conduct — first as one who lived in his time and was in his company, not as Plato, who plainly never even saw him; and next, not for the sake of any contentiousness, nor —

[11340] 122. for the needs of a contest, nor referring everything to a single point he had set himself, but handing down the truth simply and just as it was, in the manner of history and narrative, exactly as when he narrates the Peloponnesian invasion or any other event of his own time. Now if Thucydides were in dispute about these matters, the case would be different; but since we must grant and necessarily believe that Pericles was such a man, where is it reasonable to think these slanders justified — that he was no better than a servant in place of a leader, and no more fit or suited to govern a city than the cooks? Truly flattery would be a thing of great value, if it were a name to lay upon Pericles. So go on: do not merely attach to him a portion of flattery, but bring all flattery and lay it upon him; and, by Zeus, if you like, that very form itself to which you refer everything — provided you can show that, in the man to whom these things belonged, they were allotted along with the fortune of Thearion and Mithaecus, and that he was their counterpart in political affairs. But perhaps the flatterers themselves will sooner be shown to speak the truth than we shall, if we think fit to bring such a charge against him. Come then, from where did this whole argument of his set out, and from where did he arrive at the slanders against these men? Perhaps it is not worse to frame the discussion as toward one present and in our company, reminding him thus: You said that the good citizen ought not to seek by every means to satisfy desires, neither his own nor those of the citizens, nor to set this as the limit of happiness, but to choose what is best rather than what is most pleasant; for the pleasant is not in every case good, nor again is the good for the sake of the pleasant, but the pleasant ought to be pursued for the sake of the good. Do you not turn these things round, up and down? Well done, my friend, in keeping to this principle of yours, and in speaking the truth. No one will contradict you up to this point — none, at least, who is not also against me. But look — for perhaps something else may turn out. You are describing to me, my excellent friend, the life and —

[13543] 123. public conduct of Pericles when you say these things, and you do not refute the man but rather cast your vote for him, as you call it; and though it is open to you to use him as a witness for your argument, you somehow transfer the man to the side of your opponents. For what you teach in your discourse, this very man is seen to have shown first in his deeds; so that even if you had added nothing, but had stood on these statements, anyone would have found Pericles out from what has been said, like characters recognized by tokens in the plays. Look at it again, then, from the beginning, as one looks at a law. Desires must not be indulged, nor allowed to advance without limit, but the upright and self-controlled man must master his pleasures, and not accustom the citizens to seek to have more. Now Pericles was so far from being a slave to desires, or from aiming at pleasure or at gain, instead of leading toward what is best, that he is seen to have won his reputation from the very opposites of these. For he himself disdained greater possessions as much as it was at all possible, and counseled the city neither to acquire empire nor to busy itself beyond necessities, but to wage the war while keeping quiet. And surely, when he urged them to wage the war in peace, he would scarcely otherwise have allowed them to meddle in affairs at random. Moreover, he himself chose to live in good order, not in the most pleasurable way, and whenever he perceived the people behaving with insolence he restrained them. How could anyone be shown to obey Plato's precepts more plainly — if indeed one ought to say that a man so much earlier than Plato obeyed Plato — or how could Plato more be convicted of saying things contrary to himself than if he is seen at once both claiming that one ought not to consort with the peoples for pleasure or in the role of a flatterer, but for what is best, and at the same time reviling Pericles, who consorted with them not for pleasure but always from the most upright judgment? For he seems to me to do much the same as if, having first told men that they ought to govern —

[15664] 124. the cities thus, as you once saw Pericles govern the Athenians, he then forbade them again to govern as that man did. For to one willing to look rightly, both in his words and in his deeds the man is seen to have fulfilled the chief points of the dialogue — I mean by his words those he addressed to the multitude on each occasion, and by his deeds both what he himself displayed to them in his daily life, deeds worthy of that frank speech, and the evils from which the city abstained in his times. For as long as Pericles addressed the people and held the multitude in check, the Athenians neither sent expeditions to Sicily nor entangled themselves in the long and interminable circuits of the war, but were even persuaded that to advance outside the walls was superfluous, and not safe, and not the act of men who knew how to preserve what they had. But when he died, they dreamed of Sicily, longed for Italy, reached after Carthage and Libya, looked round upon all mankind, found nothing enough for them, and made the war's appendage longer than itself. And the one who urged this, Plato and Socrates, was — your partner one cannot call him, but companion you yourself might say, or rather have said. How, then, could anyone justly bring these charges against Pericles, or how is it not necessary to bring them against Alcibiades, who, taking over a city accustomed to listen to its counselors, then drove it mad in this way? For Pericles, I think, associating with Anaxagoras, became better than his nature warranted, while Alcibiades, having Socrates for a companion, profited little, or rather nothing, from the association. So it is not to be endured if anyone, praising the former, blames the latter. Plato, then, as though deliberately saying the opposite of what is, or imitating the conjurers, has not only accused Pericles of what was not fitting, but says it is even a dreadful thing if anyone should charge Alcibiades with these very same matters. But from the argument —

[17658] 125. it appears that to the one not even any share of the blame belongs, while to the other belongs no small part. For the one said they would prevail if they kept quiet and attended to the fleet — that is, preserved what they had — and did not seek to extend their empire; but the other was one of those who persuaded them to undertake all those many ventures. Among these many other errors were committed, and in particular the expedition to Sicily, from which they gained more enemies than allies and in the end were ruined, as we know. It was not, then, Pericles who made the city swell and become unsound, nor through whom they were stripped, as you say, of their ancient flesh, but the man who kept increasing their desires, who baited them with the money of the Egestaeans and acted as host for the Sicilian table — being such a man himself, and nowhere letting the people halt in their hopes, but always setting before them aims more remote than what they wished and needed, and pursuing policies opposite to Pericles, after that man's death. So before catching any of your adversaries, you go off having hunted down one of your friends, and you have suffered the same fate as Pindar's Peleus, who both missed his quarry and destroyed besides Eurytion, who was most dear to him. And the same thing, I think, they say happened later to Adrastus son of Gordius. For my part, then, I have at present no need to speak about Alcibiades, but I was led even to this against my will by the argument itself. But if geometry is a fine thing, and the equality in accordance with it, and one ought not to neglect these things, then I find that Pericles honored equality most beautifully, not only in private —

[19365] 126. but also in public affairs. "For as long as he stood at the head in peace he governed with moderation," says the man who knew his affairs well — and this is the opposite of governing by force and grasping, of which you accuse him — "and when the war came, he alone knew what they had to do to be saved." Therefore by showing himself superior to every gain, practising justice, he was plainly just; by choosing a well-ordered life rather than one of pleasure, he gave manifest proof of self-control; and as for courage, surely he showed it in those matters in which, not to please nor in submission, but as freely as possible, he consorted with the people. He alone, then, both foreseeing what was to be and able to deal with what was present — how could he not justly bear the repute of wisdom, of the most useful kind at least, if one is willing to reckon in human terms? Then the man whom one may address by every part of virtue — courageous, just, prudent, self-controlled — this man Plato numbered among the flatterers? "For he made the Athenians chatterers," he says, "and idle and cowardly and money-loving, establishing them in a system of pay." Concerning chatterers, Plato, and the idle and the cowardly, drop the charge at once, lest some other god rouse the Trojans, and we give a handle against ourselves — least of all a fitting one, yet here too Homer's saying prevails: "Whatever word you speak, such you will hear in return." For my part I would say nothing mean, nor be led to it — may I never be so mad! — but I would sooner pass the harshest judgment on myself than willingly rob Plato of the highest praises. But there are perhaps some others who care less —

[21050] 127. for your worth, especially if they catch hold of a respectable pretext, and who defend themselves on behalf of men neither base themselves nor deserving to be spoken ill of. As for the fact that I knew how to speak about these matters too — that Pericles is not liable to these charges either — it was harder to keep silent than, in trying to demonstrate it, to find what one ought to say. First, then, that he did not make them chatterers, the greatest proof, I think, is this too: that this reproach did not arise for the city in his times but later, when they already missed him brilliantly and found the men of the day talking more than they thought, and in no way preserving the good that Pericles had been. So from the very things for which they blamed the later men, and for which they were slandered by those after him, his own merits are exalted. For just as when a good physician has died, and afterward men live in great license, the disease has power to come on; so from probability he ought not only to be acquitted of the charge but for this very reason to be the more esteemed than before — just as Diotima, by being able to postpone the disease for the city ten years, set this down, I think, as a benefaction, and no one blames her for what happened afterward; but everyone might reasonably blame her for its not having been put off still earlier, while no one blames her for its happening at all. For she did not cause these things, but, so far as she could, prevented them; so that, for her part, it would not have come about in the first place. But you know how to adorn a foreign Mantinean and Milesian woman, and have very readily deemed worthy of great things whomever you please, while you bring down to the level of the base the foremost of the Greeks, men renowned among all — in no way differently than if someone were to do so to a true cook, or some other chance slave. And the cause is not ignorance of their worth —

[23003] 128. but — how could I put it becomingly? You become exceedingly fond of fine speeches. But I return to my point: that, first, this reproach of chattering belongs not to the times of Pericles but to the later ones, the work of men in no way like him. Next, probability too is preserved thus: that anyone rather than Pericles would have helped them to chatter, and the charge falls so much the less upon him — and let no one wonder at the paradox — in proportion as he was the better speaker, if indeed this is true. For if he himself had handled his speeches anyhow, or dragged the matter down to something cheap, so that it was there for anyone who wished to grasp, he might reasonably have seemed to corrupt many and persuade them to chatter, neglecting thought — just as those who first display something easy quickly draw many after them. But since he himself displayed his speeches in order and in measure and with all the beauty that arises, and preserved at once the principle of Laches — or, if you like, of Plato — providing a life no worse than his words, how could he have corrupted the Athenians, or made them chatterers, who in his very speeches is seen to have honored nothing cheap or random? For the contrary seems to me to be entirely the case: that he accustomed them neither to say anything anyhow nor to act from the first impulse. At any rate one of three things must be conceded: either that there is nothing cheap or shameful in being chatterers, or that Pericles would not have made them chatterers, if indeed he was good at speaking, or — the third — that Pericles was not skilled at speaking, nor beyond the others. It remains, then, to teach that he was skilled and beyond the others. Who, then, could become more competent praisers and witnesses of his cleverness and power than those whose habit it is rather to censure than to adorn everyone? For if these are seen —

[24901] 129. casting their vote, scarcely would anyone else withhold it. Now to allege a comic poet as witness on some other matter might perhaps not be strong; but in the judgment of speeches may I never become so grand as to look down on these men as worth nothing. I say this now to those to whom it is permitted; for the uninitiated the discussion is brief. There is no need at all for them to set gates upon their ears; rather, the gates are set long since. But this very thing escapes them, so utterly without perception are they, and so far are they from the holy things. So then, of the witnesses summoned by us regarding speeches for Pericles, and of those whom we should call no small sharers in experience of these matters, the one said that his tongue was the greatest of all Grecian tongues — speaking of something equal also to voice, but mixing in some of the bitterness of his craft with what comes from the truth. Yet even so he did not escape giving the first place to the man, nor failing to display the magnificence of his speeches; while the other says that he flashed and thundered and confounded all Greece as he addressed the assembly. For do not let me take this if he is finding fault with him; rather, let us take from the testimony only as much as is relevant to the present argument, and then those other points a little later, if there is need, will appear to be on our side. But the third, plainly, this one no longer even as a poet of comedy, but as one of the noble and good, has rendered his testimony untouched by envy and pure, saying that he took the orators in the contest from ten feet off, and that on him alone a certain persuasion sat upon his lips; and that all the rest was trifling beside him. At any rate he says thus, in vexation:

[26672] 130. "Is there now any orator among those it is worth mentioning? The Buzyges is the best — that accursed wretch." So well do they know both whom it is fitting to tear apart for their speeches and whom it is worth admiring. Did the man, then, who so far surpassed the orators, who had persuasion on his lips, who met with so much respect from all, who heard such things from those who scrutinized everyone in other matters and ridiculed them in this very point as unable to speak — did he display some trifling and chatter in the assemblies, or did he accomplish a task beyond any man's, going through life thus not by any great surplus he was using, being himself far from idle talk and accustoming the others as far as possible to keep away from it? For let us consider what belongs to chatter and what to sensible speech. To chatter, I think, is to trifle vainly and to no purpose and to waste time idly; but to true speech belongs aiming at the occasions and the facts and preserving what is fitting everywhere. From these, I think, follow also mastery and the winning over of the souls of one's hearers. Pericles, then, who conquered so greatly and accomplished so much by the means by which he conquered, was reckoned, with reason, least of all a chatterer, but best at speaking. And indeed, in the very matters for which they blame the others, they at once acquit him and teach whom one ought to blame instead of him. But if there is need of a more dignified witness, consider what Thucydides says in his accounts of him. For you will find that everywhere he recalls him as best at speaking, and gives no room for dispute, except that this man adds to speaking that he could also act — when —

[28373] 131. he says that he was first. And this witness, my good man, is one of Antiphon's companions, and, as is likely, has some pride in himself as well; yet even so he renders to that man what is fitting. Surely, then, you who praise Aspasia before Antiphon must concede this. And what need is there any longer of Thucydides? For the argument has come to the very summit of the testimony. For it is Plato himself who, hymning Aspasia as a wonderful teacher of rhetoric, takes refuge in Pericles and through him gives confirmation, and has expressly called him in just this way superior to the Greeks: so great does he plainly grant to Pericles in the matter of speeches. And yet surely it was not a sign that Aspasia shared in eloquence that Pericles spoke so well, while for the sign of Pericles' own power one must seek elsewhere; nor ought he, on Aspasia's behalf, to call him superior to the Greeks, while in his own concern reckoning him no better than the many. And he has said this about the man not here only, but elsewhere too he reasonably says that Pericles became perfect in rhetoric through his association with Anaxagoras. But let not all this stand yet. That, however, he has agreed that he became perfect in speaking is clear. How, then, could the man who so far surpassed and alone conquered before all the judges bear the repute of having let the Athenians loose to chatter at random and out of season? For I think the contrary: that he became the cause of more silence than of rashness. At any rate we know that in the other arts too, where many are alike and rivals of one another, there are many affairs and more strife; but when some one man brilliantly excels, all have already given way. So too with —

[30097] 132. speaking: when some one man of the first rank arises, the rest must be content and not meddle, but be as cautious and orderly as possible about their speeches. For one would not, by the very thing in which one is best, make others worse — especially in regard to those very things in which one is best. For just as in the affairs of life, whoever shows himself orderly and self-controlled and well-disciplined in all things, no one would say that this man thereby corrupts those who meet him; rather, we should all say he is an example of self-control, if indeed we ourselves mean to seem self-controlled. So too in speeches, whoever keeps guard over the beautiful and the fitting, and nowhere overlooks the better, nor neglects order in these matters, does what is yours, Plato — reckoning, as you do, that this whole universe was called Cosmos, "order," and not disorder, nor disarray, nor any of the names of such things — and imitating, so far as he can in his own sphere, his betters. He could not, then, be the cause of idle talk, nor its leader, nor of disorder, nor of any shameful thing, any more than one can say that the trainer accustoms men to neglect the body — he whose business, the wise Plato says, is that the body shall be in good condition. But if anyone establishes his life on a level with his words, and is conspicuous not only in speaking beautifully but also in living so, how does this man accustom others to chatter, or to do anything else whatever apart from the share of the better? I cannot be persuaded of it. Rather, one of two things, I think, was bound to happen to those who in his day kept his company: either, emulating his power, they pursued something good and similar in their speeches, and the more they honored both him and his words, the more orderly and self-controlled they would keep themselves and abstain from all lawlessness; or else, despairing utterly and reckoning the thing beyond themselves, they would keep wholly quiet, since to be an orator in the way that Pericles was —

[32132] 133. was not for them a fortune on equal terms. So that in either case they were least likely to be chatterers. I observe, too, in regard to tragedy, that Aeschylus incurred no charge of having introduced chatter, nor did Sophocles, sweetest to listen to, anywhere hear it said that he raised the Athenians to chatter — because, I think, they clung as far as possible to dignity and offered characters better than those of the many; but Euripides was charged with accustoming them to chatter, since he seemed to take away something of the weight and the occasions; and after him again, Aristophanes says, the young men became more than a furlong more talkative, as falling so far short of Euripides in poetry. For as order slipped away little by little, it had to be carried down to this fault and to seem to chatter rather than to compose. So too with political speeches: not the most dignified man, nor he in whom the sense led the words, nor he who thought one ought not to speak without high spirit and who had justly acquired that spirit from all that he was conscious of in himself — not this man was the one who made men chatterers instead of orderly, but the one who, for his part, prevented their being chatterers. It was not Odysseus, then, nor Nestor, who incurred the charge of corrupting the camp of the Achaeans, but rather Thersites, "who knew in his mind many words, but disorderly, / vainly, and not according to order, to wrangle with the kings." Therefore the orator like Pericles restrained him and would not let him chatter. But you do much the same as if someone should then have accused Odysseus of making a tumult in the camp — Odysseus, who stopped the others from making tumult; just as if you should say that Heracles too accustomed men to be willful and bold because he went about using the bow and the club, when in fact he was accustoming them to the very opposite, my good man —

[34043] 134. accustoming all to be orderly and to abide by the laws, and to use these instruments, as they saw him do, with every regard for the better. In this way, it seems to me, one might also accuse Pericles of making men chatterers — he who so confined them all that they were silent even against their will, and was so far from rousing the multitude that he kept even the orators for the most part in quiet; so that, if not in the manner of Heracles, at least in the manner of Iolaus, he was "searing the heads of the more numerous," as the comic poet's phrase has it. And then you say that Pericles made the Athenians chatterers? As if one should accuse the teacher of making the children chatterers because he expounds everything to them. And yet this same man would not even let them so much as speak, but part of his very art was knowing how to bid them keep silence. Nor would I say that Socrates made the Athenians chatterers and contentious because he conversed with very many of them at the tables and the workshops, both with townsmen and with strangers; nor should we apply to him Hesiod's verse, where he rebukes and forbids: "Pass not by the smithy and the crowded lounge"; rather, I should bid him approach the smithies and the other workshops with confidence, where they were going to hear from him some such thing, whether some of the young or of the elder. For it would not be becoming accustomed to chatter, to hear something useful — just as, indeed, listening to Hesiod himself give this very counsel, we do not think we are losing our time or becoming worse. But if some, hearing Socrates or Pericles, and then desiring their power, imitated it badly, this is not the fault of those men. For those men, speaking what is best to their hearers, displayed their power thus, while those who missed the main point and spent their speeches on nothing useful would reasonably bear the blame, and would not even seem to do the same —

[35989] 135. as those men, I think, but the very opposite — like those who draft proposals contrary to the laws, yet still set the name "laws" upon what they draft. Pericles, then, laid down the finest law concerning public speeches: nowhere to relax from dignity. How, then, is he the cause of carelessness about these things? But that the power of speaking is not in itself a thing without honor, nor brings reproach, nor is worthy of accusation, nor even of pardon, but of something better — I should be ashamed before the gods of eloquence if I sought to demonstrate it; except that I would say this much (and may anyone else, if he is vexed, turn it all upon me, and I shall not blame the divine power): that I would choose to be able to speak, with a good and self-controlled life, as beautifully as is possible for a human being, rather than to become Darius son of Hystaspes ten thousand times over; and now all things truly seem small to me beside this. And I know that Plato's affairs too, unless we are deluded, are nearly nothing other than speeches, and I congratulate him on his diction; and even if, jesting with me thus, he tries to persuade me not to be very concerned with this, I shall not be persuaded, but shall know that he is in great earnest — and most of all then, when he speaks as though in play. So we should be slandering our treasure at home if we tore these things apart. But about these matters it is not even worth speaking — and so much the less in proportion as the power of speaking is the better thing. Very well. But how did Pericles make the Athenians idle? Or will you make us all too busy, compelling us to answer each of the things said? Is not this the man who carried them everywhere, who nowhere let them sleep, commanding at Samos as one of ten generals, eclipsing all the other generals and showing them to be a name and nothing more, and reducing the Samians to —

[37894] 136. siege — not keeping quiet, but sailing out with part of the ships against Caria; and after this, when he had gone, the Samians took courage and sallied out and had the better of the blockading squadron, but he turned back and again shut them in until he forced their surrender; and again, when the Euboeans revolted, leading the Athenians into Euboea; and when the Peloponnesians were reported to be at Megara, carrying them back again to Megara, and from Megara again to Euboea, until he subdued this island too? And what could one say of the circumnavigation of the Peloponnese, the landings everywhere in enemy country, and a continuity of operations not to be likened to that of any other city? Then am I to be persuaded that this man, so keen and sleepless, and — I would most gladly have said — winged, was himself idle or accustomed others to idleness? Surely I shall seem to understand nothing of affairs, if I do — a man who did not permit idleness not only to the Athenians but not even to the enemy, but made even them change their ways, so many necessities of movement did he set about them. "And justly was this added," said Demosthenes. But nonetheless Plato accused him of making them idle and cowardly. What are you saying — cowardly? Pericles, O gods, cowardly? — who, even when addressing the assembly, began straightway from this point: "Of the same judgment, men of Athens, I always hold," he said, "not to yield to the Peloponnesians." Which of all men down to that day ever once dared to say this? — they who, even when they were saving Greece, yielded to the Lacedaemonians their own claims. But he, with nothing veiled, and without waiting — if for nothing else — at least for the consolation that comes from a preamble, very readily declared at the very beginning of his speech what seemed best to himself, as one who claimed to be both himself superior to his hearers and they to their adversaries. So then — was it the voice of one accustoming them to fear the enemy and withdraw, or of one accustoming them —

[39937] 137. to be confident and to disdain, and showing his courage from the outset, that we are to say it was? For my part I think the latter rather. For if he had accused him of having stirred up the war, or of being the cause of the troubles, or anything of the sort, even so it would not be true — at any rate, later all agreed that the truce was not broken on that side — but it would seem to repeat what had been said by others too. As it is, how is it possible to accuse him of cowardice, when some have already laid the opposite charges against him? For the things we cited just now from Aristophanes' comedy as standing thus — I mean: "He flashed, he thundered, he confounded Greece, / he set down laws written like drinking-songs, / about how the Megarians should..." — these are not charges of cowardice against him; but we know what they do charge, of which, if they are true, the opposites are false; and if not even those charges fittingly belong to Pericles, but he deliberated justly and necessarily on behalf of the state's interests, how is he not at once both to be acquitted of charge and to deserve every praise? Was it, then, only toward the Lacedaemonians that he was so minded, as one privately contending, but toward the others slack? — he who thought the Piraeus a sufficient base for them, and would not let them admire either the King or anyone else in all the world, and showed that their strength was, against all other men together, only half — that the rest prevailed in one place or another, but they everywhere upon the sea. And yet — was this the act of one introducing idleness and cowardice and unmanliness and lowliness and sluggishness into the city and into their souls, or do I fear lest one say something —

[41680] 138. of another kind, except in so far as he will not speak rightly? As, then, you say that you hear those things said against Pericles, so listen in turn to those who say the truth about him, to which no one has spoken in contradiction down to this day — since you yourself say you accuse him not from your own judgment but from hearsay. "These things the Cretan, forsooth, of the sea," one will say. Since, then, you think fit to hear those things, but do not wish to hear these, come, I shall show you others still greater and more complete than these — signs of Pericles' courage and strength and ambition; and if you can show that any excess remains, let everything from us be nonsense. "By Zeus," for someone might say that to be bold before the war was nothing great or sufficient. But he indeed thought fit that the embassies should be received by the city while the Peloponnesians remained settled at home, but, once they had taken the field, not even after they had sent forward Melesippus. So very cowardly was he that, when he learned the enemy was advancing, he gave his answers to him in deed about all things, handing him over to those who would conduct him out of the country. And the last and greatest of all to hear of — let alone to imitate: when the war was already present and joined, and the plague pressed upon them, and the land was being ravaged, and men were perishing day by day and so many already lay dead as would have struck a small fraction of terror into the enemy, had the calamity befallen them — and when, on account of all this, he saw the multitude despondent and ill at ease, and repenting of what they had voted, and thinking fit to send heralds to the Lacedaemonians, and become other men under their misfortunes — he relaxed nothing the more, nor departed from his old judgment, nor was his soul turned by their —

[43537] 139. misfortunes; but, though so far surpassing others in the face of dangers that, while the rest were despondent at these present ills, he had taken on this further and greater thing, to see the citizens so disposed that they were at a loss how to deal with them — and this not only their having submitted to the enemy, but also their bearing him ill will as the author of the present troubles — seeing them, in addition to the two things that come from fortune, the war and the plague, take on these two more, grief and exasperation against himself, set in the midst of the terrors and, so to speak, isolated on every side, he did not flinch nor withdraw, nor did he change his judgment as one changes color, set in array at once against both the citizens and the enemy; but, as if teaching them some other lesson, he went through the same things he had set forth before, and did not corrupt his doctrines in the face of dangers, nor did he play the philosopher only when at liberty, but, as if questioned about numbers or measures, he would have answered the same both after and before; so even then he uttered the same words concerning the whole situation, thinking it right neither to yield to the terrors nor to blame himself, but to be ashamed on their behalf — on behalf of others who had arisen along with the times. So that to me even Ajax, fighting and shouting before the ships, seems to be in a lighter and easier case. For his battle was against the Trojans only, and the disposition of his own people was surely favorable; but for this man the things within fared no less wretchedly than those without, and the fear from the citizens was greater than that from the enemy. In these matters Pericles showed himself nothing like the cooks, but like the foremost of the Greeks. So that to me, when I look toward these straits and the throng of his affairs, there comes the thought of some helmsman, his ship awash and the clouds bursting down, staying at the tiller and not letting go — and besides, with those aboard threatening and ready to tear him apart, holding them in check and admonishing them, and at once both struggling for the vessel and against those in the vessel.

[45729] 140. From where, then, must one still contemplate his courage? For whether we look at the man from his deeds or from his words, how could anyone have shown himself more removed from all fear, or how could anyone have been better fitted by nature for high spirit, and a better leader of others to it, than one who neither thought fit that they should abandon, through fear of the enemy, what had been resolved from the first, nor himself, through fear of them, abandon the arguments on behalf of justice? But if you say that he did not lead them out, consider whether no man of military skill would accept these charges. For they would not say that in every season, nor surely in every place, one ought to fight; but that the good general ought least of all to run risks, and ought to make war by counsels rather than by hands, just as is held to be the Lacedaemonians' national practice; but if there should be need of a battle too, it is better to be willing to fight with allies than alone, and with more rather than fewer, and with better rather than worse, in whatever respect one has the advantage. For men brought into necessity are not likely to reckon accurately; but as long as one is master of his judgment, it is great folly and ill fortune to let go of safety and deliberately choose what will profit the enemy. For in that case one would already be a general for them, and would become for them, instead of for one's own, the leader on their side. Let us not, then, say this, that he did not lead them out; but let us consider this: whether, when it was fitting to lead out and fight, he overlooked it — since we hear, indeed, that the Lacedaemonians too, when the Thebans once pressed upon them and bade them come out and fight, or admit themselves worse than they were, answered that as to which of the two were the better, they would judge by the deeds done on behalf of the Greeks by each; but they would fight, not at the enemy's chosen moment nor when those men bade them, but whenever it seemed good to themselves —

[47766] 141. and they would not use the Thebans as advisers about this. These things they answered, and these they did, with Agesilaus standing at their head, who, if any of the Greeks, seemed to be a lover of war. But what was Pericles to do? For tell me, in the gods' name — or rather, if you yourself had happened to be our general in those times, what did you think you ought to do or to say, when the whole of the Athenians were so many, and of the allies some were scattered among the islands, others along the mainland, and sixty thousand of an enemy army had invaded, and these Peloponnesians — for this too is no slight addition — men competent to fight even against all the Greeks, and men of whom I would say that, with the Athenians and Pericles to lead them, they would not without difficulty have brought even the whole earth beneath themselves? And I do not yet mention the plague as so great a matter, which lay upon them driving hard, and had wholly consumed some of the men and made others useless for the present need — which even apart from battle and the onset of war was no easy thing to endure. And indeed Pericles was not ignorant of this either: that if he conquered he would fight again with far more, and would find many others disputing the trophy, as the Corinthians did before, and inheriting these very same affairs; so that he reckoned he would win a Cadmean victory, while by acting otherwise —

[49187] 142. he would ruin the city. For against the enemy each of their parts was at risk, but for themselves the very ground of the city lay along with the danger, and one die was being cast for everything — bodies, money, glory, hegemony, the foundation of the city, their hopes, everything one could name. He did not, then, think it right to contend in so unequal a contest, nor to risk all that was theirs for nothing greater — being prudent, I think, and using a general's reckoning. And we see, surely, that wrestlers too do not engage at the strong points of their adversaries, nor deliberately offer holds against themselves, but withdraw from these as far as possible, and try to use their own advantages and pursue victory from these. Pericles, considering this, did not give the enemy so great a hold against the city, but, letting them play the fool in Attica, he was fitting out an expedition of a hundred ships against the Peloponnese and, embarking himself, was carrying the army across. And it turned out that they had cut off some part of Attica, but he, as it were, the whole of the enemy country; so that those who had then marched as to a windfall were now compelled to flee from Attica. Thus the things he did not stir were of foresight, not cowardice, while the things he thought fit they should dare, even in such circumstances, hold the last proof of that man's courage. And yet how is it not strange, if we shall accuse him for not leading out, but shall not admire him for leading them outside Attica? And if we shall reproach him for not fighting where it was not fitting, but shall not take into account that he knew where one ought to do this? And if we shall accuse him for not filling Attica with war and confusion, but shall not reckon it to his credit that he disembarked into the enemy country and shifted the siege over to the Peloponnese? But shall we seek the things not done in place of the things done? And shall we not pardon him for not leading them out while they were sick, but shall we not —

[51217] 143. admire him for leading them out even while they were sick? — he who led them, sick as they were, in such a way as not one of the healthy could purely have done. So far superior was he to the enemy and the citizens and the plague and every circumstance. And surely he would scarcely have allowed the Athenians, while they were themselves, to be idle, or to lie down beneath any men — he who did not even permit them to be sick, but, at least for the struggle for freedom and for not deserting the post of high spirit, thought fit that they be strong even beyond their present fortune. Sooner would I have thought Meleager liable to a charge of cowardice than Pericles — Pericles, whom even before his birth the god foretold from the very opposites, foretelling to his mother that she would bear a lion; and she, upon this vision, bore Pericles. So beware lest we attempt to shave a lion — not undertaking to bring a false suit against Thrasymachus, but to make a comedy of Pericles, and that too for cowardice, and then we ourselves seem braver than we ought to those who wish to bring counter-accusations, and there appear some prize, even of cowardice — or, if you like, of silence — that is without danger, as one of the Cean poets said. But indeed "flax is joined to flax," as they say. For of him who dared to say that Pericles also showed men, and any men whatever, to be money-lovers — what could one say? "By Zeus, for he was the first to establish affairs on a system of pay." But I myself am not among those who praise the system of pay, and I think I have given practical proof of this. So I have no part in the matter at all, but I have presided over speeches more cleanly than, I think, my namesake presided over the tributes —

[52963] 144. if one may even speak somewhat boldly. For he, having assigned to each what was proportionate, gained such repute; but I have neither more nor less from anyone ever — nay, I have never even come to any of the distributions, unless someone willingly sent something. And the wisdom of Socrates too — whether one should call it wisdom or philosophy, or something else — this I admire as well: that he did not peddle it, nor make himself available to those who wished to buy, nor so strongly profess himself educated as to vote himself into slavery. For my part, then, as I say about these matters, I rejoice in magnificence above all else, as I persuade myself; but I see that there are in cities, as it were, certain trailing burdens, to which one must yield, as in a body. For I hear that bodies too, both the worst and the best, are compounded from the same elements, and that by each one's partaking of these in greater or smaller measure, the worse and the better are judged. So too cities must of necessity reap the fruit of human nature, even if they seem to be governed in the best way. Since even in this universe, which has partaken of so many fair things and so much good fortune, and has left, surely, nothing fair outside itself, you would find many things in which you would take the least pleasure; yet you do not on this account accuse the universe or its Maker, nor think that some other better craftsman of the whole would ever again come to be, but you grant that these things were dragged along by nature, and you do not make trouble about it. What wonder, then, if at Athens too one of all these things stood thus, just as perhaps elsewhere also, and the leaders conceded it — at once correcting the poverty and need of the many, through which not the least of errors arise, which they thought thus rather to prevent —

[54806] 145. and at the same time taking care lest they work some worse evil by confining them altogether? For what was one not to expect men so many in number, and so keen, with so much money present, to do, if no one shared the moderate portion with them? Would they not have attempted to seize everything? And again, just as in private households everyone, I think, wishes to enjoy what is at hand, so it is not at all unlikely also for a city, especially one possessing an empire, in which perhaps the best man is not he who forbids everything, but he who takes account of the measured course. Or what need was there either to receive tributes still or to have troubles, if they were to be relieved by nothing from them? So that when you say these things, you are not finding fault with the pay, but with the empire. For that pay is necessary in a city, and that it is not possible even for the most dignified to pass it by without supplying it and showing kindness, I have no need of many witnesses, but one, the best of the Greeks, suffices me. Who is this? You yourself. For to the happy and good city — for how could the city of which Plato is founder not be happy? — in giving it guardians you assign them pay at public expense, on the ground that you yourself counsel nothing shameful, nor will they do anything shameful, neither party, neither those about to give nor those about to receive. But if the pay is not silver or gold, but something else, that is another argument. For pay too, I think, is likely to suit the forms of the constitutions. For if the Byzantines use iron for currency, they are not on that account justly to be laughed at by the Greeks, nor would they seem any the less to receive pay because they receive not gold or silver; nor again the Carthaginians, who use leather; unless, when some receive silver, because they do not receive the more precious —

[56688] 146. gold, men will say this is anything other than pay. But I think not one man would concede this. For neither is it the law that all should choose the same nourishment, yet all alike have need of nourishment. So here too the question is not what the pay is, but whether you have spoken of pay at all — unless even Euneos sent his wine free to the Achaeans because they paid him not in silver but, some in bronze, others in gleaming iron. But not even he himself made any boast of this. Pericles, then, did not himself coin money, but, there being funds on the Acropolis, from there he supplied the pay. So that he is justly liable on neither count, neither for the coinage nor for the pay. And why do I contend so earnestly over these things? For if pay and distribution are as shameful as can be, they do not belong to Pericles' policy, nor did they begin from his times, but one of the lawgivers too knew of these things — for I pass over the rest. So the later parts of his policy he has charged him with, which we refuted a little before, and the earlier parts, those concerning the coinage, just as now in turn is being shown. How could a man use arguments more easily, or with greater license? But altogether I myself wonder whether there is any simpler or juster manner of examining whether Pericles made the Athenians money-lovers, or any others of men, Greeks or barbarians, than to look at Pericles himself and consider what sort of man he was. For Xenophon's argument seems to me to be sound. For just as we see the trainers, he said, instructing the boys partly by word, but advancing them most of all by demonstration, so he says it is right to judge Socrates too. In this way, then, let us also contemplate Pericles — how he himself was disposed toward money, and what sort of example he offered of himself to the Athenians and to the other Greeks in this respect; and if —

[58580] 147. we find him ignoble and illiberal and inferior to gain, let us say that he taught the others the same things and corrupted them and made them like himself; but if he disdained money so much as I do not know whether any man ever did, how did he make them lovers of money, or how did he corrupt others in matters in which he himself was upright? — just as if you were to say that Rhadamanthys accustomed men to swear falsely, he who had such a surplus of piety and justice that even after death he is held to judge those who arrive yonder concerning their deeds in life. So you too, having first shown Pericles petty and ignoble and lowly and looking toward silver, then say that he made the others such; just as one may say of the teachers of letters — and this image is no longer Xenophon's but Plato's own. For just as, he says, the writing-masters, for the children not yet skilled at writing, first sketch the lines with the stylus and then give them the tablet and compel them to write following the guidance of the letters, so too does the city. But let us take, instead of the city, Pericles. Pericles, then, by his words, it seems, gave guidance to the Athenians, in which there was nothing illiberal or ignoble; and by his life he showed himself and offered for examination what sort of man he was. Did he, then, being such as we hear, sketch out for them a beautiful life to lead, or one bent toward gain, with their hands held out, and least likely to fare rightly? And indeed, while they heard him speak in the assemblies and in the council, his life they saw, surely, every day. So that if they looked toward him, they were likely to be superior to money. Come then, let us in turn consider this man also with regard to all the other charges, and go through them in the same manner as concerning love of money, in both these images, that of the trainer and that of the writing-master, and let us sum everything up in brief. He made them chatterers, he says —

[60557] 148. and yet he himself was no chatterer. Idle — but he was energetic. Cowardly — but he is seen to conquer in courage. Money-lovers — but he least of all honored money. How, then, being himself good at speaking, competent in action, courageous, superior to money, did he make them chatterers, idle, cowardly, illiberal? For you are either telling a dream, or a riddle, or I do not know what to call it. For how is it not strange, if such as he himself was he did not make them, but such as he himself least of all was, this he wrought them into? For to me the contrary appears from these things: that he had made them, as far as in him lay, clever, energetic, courageous, magnificent — unless one must believe also against Socrates that he corrupted the young, who was to them an example of everything good. These things are not so, Plato, but one of two: either the Athenians were not such as you accused them of being, or anyone rather than Pericles was the cause, if indeed he neither displayed the wrestling-bouts badly nor offered any obscure or counterfeit or crooked lines. Let us, then, recall the points above and reason them out, and that too from what has been shown. Plato said one ought not to flatter the multitudes; but Pericles is seen to have used the greatest frankness. That one ought to fight on behalf of the better; but he fought in such crises as we displayed just now. Not to seek to satisfy the desires; he, then, restrained them and was not led rather than himself led. Nor by every means to accustom them to have more, says he who lays down the law; but he, beginning from his own hearth, chose the proportionate before the more. These things, then, seemed good to Plato; these things Pericles is seen to do. How could anyone be shown to have lived or governed more according to Plato's precepts? Or what should one have said first on his behalf, if anyone brought a charge?

[62461] 149. [Plato.] But the greatest sign of Pericles' rectitude, apart from what has been said — and that he was not the one who corrupted or accustomed the Athenians to be money-lovers, or by every means to seek to have more — is this. For neither is Pericles the one who carried the tributes to infinity; rather, of this excess too, dear Socrates, if you should seek the cause, you will find your own companion. For he is the one who advanced the tributes to so great a height as it was not possible to pay even for those willing, neither obeying you in anything nor imitating Pericles. But Pericles was not such, neither here nor elsewhere, but so very far from grasping, and from counseling the Athenians to take upon themselves affairs that did not befit them, that, when in the former war they had prevailed over the Peloponnesians and held Megara, Nisaea, Troezen, Pegae, and Achaea, he persuaded them to give these back and make a peace — so far was he from teaching them besides to keep ever something more than what they had. So that I, for my part, looking to these things and judging by them, think that if Pericles had survived and had not departed earlier, the Greeks would not have run aground into the uttermost evils, but, when the Athenians held the Lacedaemonian men and Pylos, they would have sought nothing more, but would straightway have freely given these back and made a common peace for the Greeks. For he did not even counsel them to choose the war itself for the sake of grasping more, but in order not to give up what they had; and to begin with, that man did not even think it right that a war should arise, but that disputes should be settled by justice. So he knew both how to accept war out of peace and, when it was possible to keep peace, nowhere chose war; nor did he accustom them to cling to the more, but to the fitting. And yet these are demonstrations of courage, good counsel, justice — the fairest things — not of those which Plato —

[64434] 150. ascribed to him. I think, then, for my part, that the surname which that man had among the Greeks — not the one of Mithaecus and Thearion, but the same as Zeus's — is the greatest testimony to the arguments on his behalf, and that he was not one of the base. Or shall we make the surname a token of Aristides' justice — for surely we ourselves were not present when he assigned the tributes — but shall not make the surname a token of Pericles' virtue and nature, which he acquired not from one part of virtue but from all together? For that one must not, in turn, dishonor the many nor neglect the repute that comes from them, but that there is in these too something well-aimed, touching upon the truth by some divine portion, Plato himself is the one who teaches and says it; and still higher, many generations before Plato, Hesiod sang, composing these verses which all chant: "Rumor is never wholly destroyed, whatever rumor many / peoples voice abroad; she too is some goddess." So Rumor bears Pericles the fairest witness, and, being herself a goddess, declares that he was greater than human measure. And surely it cannot even be said that the Athenians, faring badly under misfortunes, granted him this honor — as at a later time they conceded certain things even beyond desert, being humbled. But if ever at any other time, then too they were at their height, and the city was for them greatest under him, so that we, Plato, might call it greatest. But this is no harm to the argument. For those faring as I say must, I think, think most highly of themselves, and be farthest from lowliness, and nearest to willfulness. Then, therefore, they addressed him as Olympian —

[66120] 151. so great was the concession. And yet what am I saying? For even if they had been utterly humbled, the argument would have turned about thus too against Plato. For they would then appear to be flattering him, not Pericles the Athenians. But neither did Pericles flatter the Athenians nor the Athenians Pericles. Whence, then? But seeing a man supreme both in speaking and in acting, and nowise near to servility, but most fit and competent to rule and surpassing all the rest in nature, they experienced something Homeric and thought him like the gods. Then him whom those who used him and were in his company and knew him by experience deemed worthy of the same address as the gods — shall we enroll him with Thearion and Mithaecus and I know not what third in the same? We shall not, then, seem to be sane. But, by Zeus, Cimon was some base fellow, or such as one would pray that no child of his own should become! Yet on this account too one might justly be grateful to Plato, that he enrolls this man also among the orators for us. For I should rather have feared this — lest someone should rob him of the orators and set him among the generals. But as it is, Homer's saying exactly fits: that the same man would suffice "to be both a speaker of words and a doer of deeds," if indeed Cimon too shall be with us. So it is no reproach to rhetoric that Cimon is enrolled in it. But I could have wished that my contest were with some other man too and not with Plato — and I say this both about him and about the others — that I might use confidently all the means I had, and that it might not happen to me as it does, as if, when sailing and able to be carried by a fair wind, I then slackened through cowardice; or also —

[67849] 152. as if, contending with horses, when I could pass ahead both swiftly and by as much as I wished, I then deliberately held back out of sparing for the one ahead. So much more account do I make of chancing to say nothing discordant toward Plato, nor to seem, as it were, to be insolent, than of praising each of those men. And rightly; for the matter toward him is worth no less gratitude to me. Yet if both shall come about — both to absolve them of the charges against them and to have preserved for Plato all the respect and honor that is possible — and justice shall be added for both, it would be moderate every way. That Cimon, then, was not one of the elegant, nor of a kind to insinuate himself and flatter, his guardians bear witness, who would not consent to hand over to him his patrimony until far on in his age — so simple, and rather old-fashioned, did they think him. And that the affairs of Cimon were not for any man whatever, nor of the lowest sort, but that he was a man clever both at reasoning about affairs and at carrying them through, and such as not only reasonably to be of good repute among the Athenians, but also without difficulty to hold the first place among the Lacedaemonians — this would be the next thing to say. Now to relate all his affairs, or even those of the others who remain, would be wholly out of season, especially since no charge has been spoken against them to which one must reply, except that they were certain servants. But the things that are, as it were, the chief heads, and such as it is not possible to pass by even if one wished, these shall be told. And it is perhaps necessary to premise this much. For if all these things are servitude for us — to lay down laws and establish a constitution and command armies —

[69625] 153. and hold yearly office in a city, and besides to deliberate beforehand, to preside, to fulfill an embassy, to judge lawsuits, to adorn festivals if it so happens — I mean, to sum up, all action and leadership, if one must call it servitude and underling's work, such as even kingship itself and all dominion, and, if you wish, going still further back, that one who rules a household does not rule rather than serve, and the comic poet's saying holds firmly and fixedly, that the master is one of the household's slaves — if these things are so, as I said, then first of all I myself do not see how everything could be turned more upside down. For if we are to define opposites by their opposites, how will each thing still preserve its own nature for us? For if rule is servitude, scarcely would anything else escape servitude; and if we should lay it down that rule is servitude, what prevents us from laying it down by these same arguments that servitude too is rule? And from this the position goes round and round for us, and never stays in the same place, but rule, having first become servitude, through that becomes rule again, and servitude, in turn first reckoned rule, by that very rule comes round to be servitude again. And thus these names will wander and shift over, being names of opposite things — or, if you like, the opposites of the thing. And indeed, with these taken away, it is not easy for us to find what is left. For if we shall stand at the head neither of many nor of few, neither of a household nor of ourselves, so to speak, and nothing shall move any of us, neither the need of country nor the ancestral hearth nor servants looking to us nor anything else of human concerns, but we shall thus flee affairs in flight and sit, as it were, in mid-air, touching nothing of the earth, nor caring about any affair, not even if someone announce to us the same things as to Meleager —

[71539] 154. "Men they are slaying, and fire lays the city in ashes, / and others lead away the children and the deep-girded women" — but we shall condemn all this as illiberality and lowness, and this argument will suffice us for everything, that to busy oneself with these is to serve and to be an underling, and that to be wronged is better than to wrong — what is left but to live nearest to lifeless things, and like the sick, when they are ordered by the physicians to keep quiet? But if, then, all these things are servitude and nothing dignified, what has come over you, dear soul, that you yourself imitate the servants for us, devising a constitution and drafting laws, and you say both that tyranny contributes to the laying down of laws, and you say that philosophy needs dominion besides, and that the cities will not cease from evils before that much-hymned condition of yours comes — either the kings shall philosophize, or the philosophers shall be kings? — since, if these do not come together, philosophy and political power, there will be no further gain for men in common. And, the greatest thing of all: in the very matters in which you govern and legislate, you mark off guardians for the city, who will have the same art as Cimon, and you give them the precedence over the other citizens, having preferred them by no small margin, both honors and front seats, both living and dead, whoever in turn shall appear best of these; and altogether you spend most of your time about these men, as though this were the one safety for the city, if the guardians' affairs flourished. And you neither call them hired men, although you have assigned them pay from the others in the city, nor servants of the public — and these too men about to pass their lives sleepless on behalf of all and to risk every risk for the whole city; nor did you think they would seem truly to resemble certain hirelings and foreigners, nor that they would be maintained in the fashion and lot of Carians, having many troubles for little —

[73562] 155. and that little a pay — but you think it sufficient ambition for them, if they know how to preserve the country and the whole city looks toward them. For if again all these things are fine and just for you, and the eager pursuit is not useless nor illiberal nor a thing of shame, to have established such a guard for a city and such men as will be able both to help their friends and to ward off their enemies, consider Cimon not from afar but from these, and all these will seem to you to be present as witnesses to the man, that he was no servant nor one of those who consort for pleasure, but that the affairs of his public life stood for him as freely and beautifully as possible — and almost as your affairs of public life stand, in this dangerless condition of yours. Consider him, then, not only as to how he guarded the city and the country, but also from the two extreme limits: how he conducted himself toward the Greeks and how toward the barbarians; since to you yourself too it seems right that one ought not to deal with both in the same way, but with the one as is fitting toward kinsmen, with the other as one must toward natural enemies. For when no slight or small disturbance arose in the Peloponnese — but such that the Lacedaemonians came to every extremity, the helots having risen against them, and at the same time the earthquakes pressing upon them — he, leading four thousand Athenian hoplites, whom you honor, Plato, both saved the city and the country for them and dissolved all the fears that beset them and set in order the affairs in the Peloponnese. Such was he toward the Greeks, and toward the best of the Greeks and at the same time allies. And as to what sort he was toward the barbarians and enemies — there are not for any of the Greeks more or more renowned deeds in foreign lands, nor finer trophies over barbarians, nor any in which it is fitting to admire more both the courage and the purpose. For not because —

[75523] 156. they failed of what they had hoped and eagerly aimed at did he therefore think fit to let them go as if they were benefactors, nor was he unmindful of the temples outraged by them, nor of the things they had displayed in the matters in which they were able; but, thinking it right that they should owe the utmost penalty for these too, and for still greater things, which, had they succeeded, they would plainly have brought to pass, he did not think it right to keep quiet nor to advise the others to do so, nor to render thanks shamefully to the gods, content if they were safe, but what poets and lawgivers and proverbs and orators and all bid — to ward off the aggressors — this he proposed and to this he led them, not as one saying the most pleasant thing, but as one saying the most just. And besides this, he thought the truest guard of Greece to be, not if, having shut them up at home, he should let them sleep in safety, but if he should cast into the barbarians a fear of devising any such thing again in the future, and if he should thrust them away from Greece as far as possible. For these reasons he led the war out into their country, and sailed round Cyprus, coasted along Pamphylia, fought at sea against Phoenicians and Cyprians and whomever of them he engaged. And at the river Eurymedon he set up the memorials of both a sea-battle and a land-battle, conquering both in a single day. So that, while the poets had been struck with amazement at the earlier deeds, those done when the barbarians attacked, nevertheless one of them sang afterward of these — not all, but the deeds of a single day: "Since the time when the sea divided Europe from Asia, / and impetuous Ares besets the cities of mortals, / no fairer deed than this of men upon the earth / was ever wrought on the mainland and the sea together. / For these men, having slain many Medes on land, / took a hundred Phoenician ships at sea, / full of men; and greatly groaned Asia, smitten / by them with both hands by the might of war."

[77546] 157. And these things he hymned not unfittingly, nor did he exalt them so much as a poet. For those former dangers were risked at the gates of Greece — some at Thermopylae itself, others off Artemisium of Euboea — but these in the midst of enemy country. And then the cities divided the danger, but both of these were the work of one man and one fleet. And those men divided, as it were, the fortune as well as the danger: for the Athenians and Themistocles conquered brilliantly off Artemisium, while those who met the foe at Thermopylae accomplished nothing more for Greece, save that they brought in their bodies, striking the barbarians with terror by their death, though unable to check them from what they had set out for. But these men, he says, conquered the enemy both by land and by sea, and took so many prisoners of them as it was a task even for all the rest to withstand. For this reason, he says, I say with confidence: "No fairer deed than this of men upon the earth / was ever wrought on the mainland and the sea together." So accurate and steadfast a guardian was that man of Greece, not only of his own city, and such defenders, Plato, he provided for the Greeks. So that as long as Cimon lived, the barbarians preferred to be dead through fear of the Greeks, and not to consider which of our people they should bring beneath themselves, but how they themselves should be saved. From which they gave up all the cities and withdrew from no small part of the inland country — the contrary of which the Lacedaemonians later conceded, toward whom you look and with whom you contend in training your own people, to whom belongs experience and habit in wars alone. By so much better did Cimon guard —

[79261] 158. the Greeks, and was thus worth much both in speaking and in acting. Would he, then, have accomplished these things, or won victories so many and brilliant both by land and by sea in succession, if he had thought fit to be a slave to the multitude and not, on the contrary, to rule as exactly as was possible — or if he had led the army without order and arrangement and conceded everything they wished, and had not everywhere taken all thought and forethought for their good order and harmony and their being ready to hand for him in all things? For I think that men neither wanton nor insolent nor mastered by pleasures, but as orderly as possible and keeping their station and, as Homer says, having a sense of shame, would master the enemy and be useful both to him and to themselves. And tell me now all those things: the shipwright, how he sets the timbers in order; the builder, how he sets the stones in order; the chorus-trainer; whomever you please. For whatever of these you mention, you will be speaking on behalf of Cimon's leadership and public conduct. And indeed, when you say that this whole is called Cosmos, "order," you say rightly, as I for my part affirm, leading us on toward doing nothing at random; Cimon, then, preserved for the city what corresponds to the name of the universe. For he made all as orderly as he could. Such was his public conduct, and such things he taught both the people in the city and his followers on the campaigns. "But, by Zeus, they ostracized him, that for ten years they might not hear his voice." And again they brought him back before the ten years were out, that they might hear his voice — so much did they long for it. But you speak of how they condemned him, while you do not consider how they changed their minds. And the things they unjustly voted against both men you mention, but the things they rightly resolved you do not think are, for those who were driven out, an excuse, and for the one acquitted the greatest proof of virtue. And, it seems, those who —

[81290] 159. condemned themselves repented and did not abide by what they had resolved at first; but you have brought your charge as though it were something authoritative, and you did not think fit in this to imitate the Athenians by acquitting the man of the charge, but what was harsher you imitated of them, and the rest you let go — and this, what is most absurd of all, while accusing the very Athenians, and, what is finer, not these second things but those which one would gladly attribute to Cimon. And if nothing of this kind had happened, you would have had nothing to accuse; but since they dissolved the event by their repentance and transferred the blame to themselves, do you not reckon it equal as if we had had nothing to say from the first? And surely it is far greater and finer, for the account of virtue, to have fallen and returned than not to have fled at all. For the latter belongs even to ordinary men, surely, while this was not according to the many; and the one a man might reckon to fortune, but this to being recognized as superior to the rest. For of the falling and fleeing, envy is the cause, and perhaps some other such thing might appear; but of the recall and summons before the time, there is nothing else to allege but virtue, on account of which they were ashamed in his absence and supposed his presence profitable to themselves, and, the greatest thing, thought fit to condemn themselves rather than him. So that if one must consider him by himself, what need is there to speak, if anyone offended against him? But if one must contemplate him from the rest, this man appears honored above all others. But not yet these things; for presently they will perhaps fit better. But what, of the many and great things for which one might justly praise Cimon, is the greatest and, as it were, the chief head, and belongs preeminently to that man, this I wish still to say and to render to the man as something else his due. For in few of all men, as it seems to me, both of the earlier and of the later, does this argument hold and not contend along with it nor give room to the slander, that —

[83411] 160. it is, as it were, fated for the sons of noble and good men to turn out base and worth little of their fathers' virtue — he who, being the son of Miltiades and inheriting so great a peril of that man's glory, disgraced nothing of what he had, nor thought it enough to inscribe his father's name, like one claiming to win at Olympia because he is the son of an Olympic victor; but he so well and so beautifully imitated that man, and clung as it were to the tracks of his father's deeds, that even if none of the historians had happened to say whose son he was, one could from the deeds themselves conjecture his father and to whom he belonged. But our argument has come round to Miltiades himself, whom I should be more ashamed to praise than to blame; so much do I think I should say less than I wish in every way. And yet so much I would say confidently: for if Plato, rising up — I say — shall himself think fit to accuse Miltiades and shall judge this to be the better argument for him, I am ready to concede everything and not to think that man worth very much. But it does not seem to me that he would easily do so, who, even when he mentioned him, said: "Miltiades, the one at Marathon." And yet this is nearer to a kind of lament than to a reproach, and to one honoring rather than disparaging, like those whom Simonides was wont to honor in his epigrams. Nevertheless Miltiades too has a share in the catalogue, and there has come round to him the lot of the —

[84887] 161. Plataeans who were marshaled by him at that time. For, no accusation against him having been spoken beforehand, he is in a way of necessity put on trial, because his name is among the servants. And yet, Plato, one of the forensic men might say, he who laid down the law did rightly in thinking fit that the suits and the indictments should be expressed plainly: "So-and-so injured me by doing this and that"; "and he proposed these illegal measures"; and if anyone brings any other charge, to state it in the same manner and not to frighten men idly. But you put off declaring, it seems, until the morrow what each of these men did or said to do wrong — as in some other dialogue — and you accuse them all in common, and not even him whom you thought fit to honor above the others, Pericles, by apportioning the charges to him and laying more of them on him than on any, not even him did you convict, nor did you show by saying or doing what he made them such; but by this one thing you honored him beyond the rest, making the charges against him more numerous and, as it were, clearer, so that one knows about what one must speak. But he has perhaps had no less even from us, so that in this respect one could not greatly blame the one who furnished the starting-point. So be it. But Miltiades, the one at Marathon — where in the chorus shall we station him, or in what rank? Or is it clear that the one before the theater and where it will be in a fine position for viewing for all? — except in so far that the man is not one to stand on the left rather than on the right wing for the Greeks. And, by Zeus, if anyone should question him and lead him back step by step from the beginning, as Plato is wont to question — by teaching what, or counseling what, —

[86654] 162. did he make the Athenians better, or by leading and rearing them straightway from youth, or what sorts of customs and pursuits did he introduce into the city — we should say that he would not be at a loss for a measured and true answer, but would answer thus; and we shall write down for Miltiades the answers in the verses: "I shall, then, tell of the ancient education, how it was disposed, / when I flourished speaking what is just, and self-control was the custom. / First it was required that no one should hear the voice of a boy uttering a sound; / next, that they should walk in the streets in good order to the music-master's, / the lads of the same quarter, naked, in a body, even if it snowed like meal." Then concerning the music of that time, of what sort it was — that not the all-harmonious nor what aimed at pleasure was honored in it, but one had to sing either "Pallas, sacker of cities, terrible," or "some far-resounding shout," straining the harmony which the fathers handed down. "And if any of them played the buffoon or bent some bending, / he was thrashed with many blows, as one doing away with the Muses." Sitting at the trainer's, and so on, going through each of the rest, he will at the end set upon some of those who are vexed: "But yet these are those things / from which my training reared the men who fought at Marathon." Such things Miltiades will answer — to me neither otherwise base nor anywhere false, but befitting both him to have given this training, being a leader in those times, and the Athenians, long since educated, to come to the battle. For it was not possible, not possible, for men to become orderly and brave and superior to terrors in a single day, having been reared from the first in indolence and basely in —

[88426] 163. all respects, but it was necessary that they should long beforehand have been laid hold of both by good nurture and by fitting words and by measured habits and by the best opinions. At any rate none of the Dolopians met them on that day, nor any of the others reared like them. Nor indeed was Datis, the master and leader of the enemy's camp, able either himself to become a brave man in a single day or to make those under him such, many as they were. And yet it had been told him beforehand by the King that he would be deprived of his head if he did not bring back the Eretrians and Athenians. But nonetheless fear made him no better. For those were temporary injunctions from a master, but Miltiades had long since persuaded the Athenians to consider no master but the laws, and to think no fear stronger than the beautiful and the just, nor any necessity so strong on account of which it would be fitting to become worse. For these reasons he had the means to use them. For he could not so suddenly have made them sailors, not even if it had been very necessary that they be sailors on that day, nor musicians, nor horsemen. Nor, then, good hoplites, nor, to sum up, good men profitable to themselves, could so brief a time have sufficed to make; but just as the farmers reap the fruit of their seeds many months later and not at the moment of casting them down, so too Miltiades, having laid hold of those men by old agreements, had them useful at the crisis, not taking up their lessons at the moment of need — for the chorus would have contended shamefully for him in that case — but having trained them at leisure for the need. And indeed of their training and care all Greece reaped the common fruits. And surely, even if one had nothing else to say of Miltiades, nor were it possible to find many and dignified things, it seems to me enough that the decree should stand, instead of an image —

[90336] 164. of his soul, for all. Or, in the gods' name, shall we think fit that the spirit and strength of our speeches and writings be esteemed, and if there is anything in them tending toward courage or justice, shall we say it is not useless to those who wish to use it — but of that man's purpose and intention, of what sort it was and whether to be imitated by ordinary men or by the more virtuous, is the decree not a sufficient sign, which he composed in the very midst of the dangers, declaring one judgment to the Greeks concerning all that is just? And surely, if it is right to honor ancestors, how is it not right to honor Miltiades as a common ancestor of the Greeks who came after those times? For he who not only presided over their safety, but also showed of what sort men ought to be in the struggles for freedom, and set up a clear example for all, this man is most manifestly of all those of old. If, then, we praise both Solon and Lycurgus for the laws they laid down, how is it not just to praise Miltiades, who in deed laid down this law, that no one should admire the worse nor choose the easiest before the better? For let us examine Miltiades by comparison with Lycurgus. For surely it cannot be said of him either that Plato set him as one of the servants — this is not so, nor near it. Lycurgus, then, because he laid down laws looking toward courage and endurance, and because he made the citizens better in the affairs of war, is thus of good repute — like some trainer who has prepared others for victory, but has himself displayed nothing of the kind. But Miltiades, besides accustoming the citizens to the best things and preparing them to be inferior to none in the affairs of war, was himself the leader of the greatest of deeds —

[92096] 165. he plainly was. So he appears to us to have become not by much second to Lycurgus, nor among any others more justly of good repute than among those who justly praise that man. And indeed, in the other works — I mean both pursuits and arts — some were honored as having begun, others as having surpassed; but Homer they praise as having both begun and brought poetry to completion as beautifully as possible. Miltiades, then, both himself began the freedom for the Greeks and left to none of the Greeks any surpassing of his own deeds — not, at least, among those we can name from the other cities. So that I fear it may be of a piece, neither to receive Homer into the city nor to be willing to praise Miltiades. Then Plato asks whom Miltiades made better, whether of the citizens or of the strangers, or whether he means slave or free, or man or woman. Whom, then, Plato, did Miltiades make worse, whether of the citizens or of the strangers — whether I mean slave or free, or man or woman? Or did association with him end in reproach and loss to any of them all? And I shall answer you brilliantly: whom did Miltiades make better? And I would not name man and woman to you, like those who inquire about small works, but, if you grant me to say it, that man made all the Athenians better and the whole city better, as I speak of a city and as I speak of a people and as it is meant of the commonwealth. For do not make me go through everything with precision, nor by a single measure, nor, like Geryon's heads, equal through...

[93631] 166. You should not suppose that all men must stand in a single rank; rather, leave room for some who are inferior, since not all men are alike in war. Just so, neither in a city are all alike in judgment, any more than all bodies are equal and nearly the same; but as among the leaders themselves some are worse and some are better, so a people too could never become precisely one, but of necessity some are worse and some are better disposed in the very matter of obeying their leaders. Suppose, then, that under the leadership of Miltiades as well some drew the greatest profit from his power, others a moderate amount, and within the middle range too set down a further variation. And in a multitude one man might be less fortunate even in respect of his hearing. The sun itself cannot warm all men alike; before now a man has shivered in the clear light of noon when a chill came over him. If you examine the matter in this measured way, with regard to the power of nature, Miltiades too will appear to you to have benefited the whole city. But if you look for the best men one by one, you will not find even the Thessalian horses all excellent in a row, nor the Laconian hounds; yet the name is preserved for the breeds all the same. So examine the Athenians too in this way, looking at the whole character of their polity and how they conducted their common affairs in those times—just as we say, indeed, that a city is well governed, although it is not possible for such a city to exist in which no fault, greater or smaller, occurs, and no one does wrong at all. And further, on your own terms, you wise men, one might bring the argument round to this point: that if a city is to be well governed, then all who dwell in it must be law-abiding, both men and women; and if law-abiding, then orderly; and if orderly, then temperate; and if temperate, then prudent as well; and if all are prudent, surely no one can do wrong; so that, as long as the city is well governed, of necessity no one

[95631] 167. does any wrong at all. But I do not think this is possible, nor would you ever say that, because it does not come about, therefore no city should be held to be well governed; rather, examining the common form of the polity, and at the same time judging the city against the other cities, you reckon some things, I think, to the laws and customs, and assign others to the necessities of nature. If you consider the Athenians in this way too, and do not cross-examine them man by man as the lawgivers do, I have a further addition to make to you, greater still and more wonderful: that Miltiades made not only the city better, but, besides the city, all Greece as well. And as you ask which of the citizens or of the foreigners he improved, so I declare that both the citizens and all the other Greeks were made better by that man. For he led them all toward the noblest things, so that he made no one worse, but, so far as in him lay, made all better. But look at the matter from your own definitions, paying no heed to my arguments, except where something follows from necessity. Do you say anything throughout your whole discourse other than this: that justice is noble, and that whatever a man does he must do with justice, and must always use rhetoric for the sake of what is just? "Even if a man strikes me," you say, "it makes a difference whether justly or not; and if a man drives me into exile, then, if unjustly, he is wretched"; and everywhere you employ these qualifications, saying "justly" or "unjustly." And the just you rightly hold to be noble, and its opposite base; and justice you make the mark of a happy man, injustice of his opposite; and the one you say a man must pursue as far as he can, the other flee with all the speed he has. Are not these your arguments? Do you not praise all things done justly, and blame them when done otherwise? Hold, then, and consider; for let us not, as in a battle by night, lay hands on our own friends. Is it just, then, to ward off attackers? Most just of all. And what of this—is it not just to make more account of the Greeks than of the barbarians? Anyone, surely, at least anyone who is Greek, would say so. Miltiades, then, when the barbarians were attacking the

[97863] 168. Greeks, set nothing before the common safety, nor did he privately deliberate about his own affairs, nor put off to another occasion the showing of his own virtue. And yet it was open to him, had he looked not to the best but pursued the same ends as the tyrants, to join—if he wished—with Hippias and the Peisistratids in marching against Greece; or, if he wished, to come with the Aleuadae out of Thessaly, or before the Aleuadae. Nor, I think, would he have lacked men of the same mind even from Lacedaemon. And indeed the barbarians would have received him no less gladly than many others. But, as it seems, Miltiades did not think these things became him; rather, letting all this go and passing it by and judging it worth nothing in the reckoning of virtue, he gave himself to the common service of his fatherland and of the Greeks. And yet, in doing this and choosing this, did he display a mark of justice and nobility, or of vice and injustice? Or shall we say of neither? For my part, I think nothing prevents him from being called happy and enviable, if the saying of Plato is true—that a bright glory rests upon those who have served their city justly. So be it. But shall we have fears for his courage, or shall we say that the occasions which displayed it were small and trifling? Were not, I may almost say, all men at that time fallen beneath the Persians, and did not the king accomplish most things by his command? Wherever they set out, they supposed they held the place; and even those who marched against the Scythians were thought to have failed only because they could not find them. The Egyptians, the wisest of all men, did not find a single device among them all by which to escape slavery. And of Libya

[99599] 169. there was then no account that divided it from Asia; rather, all was one continent, so far as the Persians were concerned. Of the Greeks, those who dwelt in Asia were enslaved—some for three generations, some for longer; the islands were of no consequence; the Eretrians, of the Euboeans, who had raised their hands in resistance, were carried off, household and all, within three days; of the others in Europe, some had already given earth and water, while others were watching for what was to come, accounting themselves more fortunate than the Eretrians only insofar as they would suffer the same things later. The Lacedaemonians, whether on account of the war against the Messenians or because they were waiting for the full moon, were themselves unable to come to the rescue. And the army had already put in at Marathon from Eretria, on equal terms. That was no time for servants, nor for submissive men, nor for those who had long known how to do what they were bidden, nor for those who look to what seems good to others, nor for those who seek how they may live in safety and think this sufficient; rather, that occasion, like a herald, summoned the bravest of the Greeks and him who had longest consorted with the best reasonings, and one who knew how a man must both save and be saved. Nor do we praise those men—I mean those who led in those times—for this, that they saved Greece in any way whatever, like the pilot you speak of who brings a cargo from Aegina or from the Pontus; rather for this, that they saved both the citizens and all men with nobility of bearing. For if they had thought it right to save them by surrendering their arms, or by coming to terms with the heralds, or by choosing the Persian king as master in place of their laws, I for one would not have thought I ought to congratulate either the words that persuaded or those who were persuaded, on their safety; rather, I would have been able to call those men servants in truth, and hirelings of the passing day, like the ferrymen—and not only, if you like, the pilots. But if, beginning with the heralds themselves and with the answer given

[101732] 170. they proclaimed to the Athenians that they must practice for the contest on behalf of the freedom of the Greeks, and must not abandon the post of their high spirit, nor throw away their arms nor yield to their fears, but take up their arms and become superior to their fears—or rather, count this one fear alone, terrible and unbearable, namely to yield to baser men and to give up their ancestral bearing—if, saying these things and so prepared, they thought it right to meet the decision concerning all things and to receive whatever should come, as men who would gain alike in either event, then I do not see what ignoble service there is in this, or how it resembles the reckonings of a pilot. But if you mean this—that in the same way as in a ship the pilot is supreme, and to him belong all matters, both those of the sailors and those of the passengers, so too at that time the affairs of the Greeks came into the hands of those men and were directed by their judgment—if you mean this, and use the pilot only as an illustration, just as Homer likens men to lions and boars and many other things, not once for all, but so far as suits his purpose, if in this respect you too remember the pilot, then I grant and concede it. But if you misuse the comparison in all the same respects, and assign the same standard of justice to both, I do not know who will accept this—and I think not even you yourself. "The pilot," you say, "having taken two obols or a few drachmas, is discharged of his duty." But Miltiades did not, for two obols nor for a couple of drachmas—nor, I think, for any great sum of money—conduct his policy among the Athenians, or rather, if the truth must be told, among all the Greeks; rather he offered his virtue unpaid into the common stock for Greece, just as you say Socrates offered himself to the young. "The pilot," you say, "makes no better men of those who embark on the ship." Excellently put, Plato—and yet the pilot too perhaps makes the sailors better in seamanship at least. But I let this pass. Grant that the pilot makes no one any better; yet Miltiades, even before entering upon the danger, trained them all toward what was best, and, having led out men who were, so to speak, private persons,

[103974] 171. brought them back victorious. Do not, then, my good sir, set him on a level with the pilot who carries men to Olympia; rather let us say he resembles the trainer who, having long prepared the athlete for the contest there, then led him out and brought him back a victor. And if there is added to this the further fact that he himself perished while winning the first place of the contest at the same Olympiad—Heracles! who could charge such a man with mere servitude, or who would not rejoice with him over his crowns? It is not possible for the pilot to say to any of the passengers that they must despise death, nor that they should throw themselves on purpose into the sea—only to throw out so much of the gear, and this for the safety of their bodies; so far the pilot has authority to command. But the sum of the matter has been laid down for him beforehand by his art: to save, and not to meddle. But in the words of Miltiades there was none of this—to be saved by every means, not even at the cost of throwing away their arms, should it so chance. Far from it. Rather the very opposite: not to seek to be saved by every means, but, even if they must die, to dare it on behalf of fatherland and temples and tombs and polity. You see how far he has surpassed even the pilot. And so all who were persuaded of this by him lie august in their august rest, having ended their life most nobly of all the Greeks, possessing their tomb as a monument of their virtue, and, though beneath the earth, guarding the earth itself, not held captive by it—coming closest to sharing in that address which Hesiod made for the end of the golden race, when he said: "These are called the holy spirits beneath the earth, noble, averters of evil, guardians of mortal men." And if you call those men all but spirits—nay, daemonic—you may say with confidence that they are certain guardians beneath the earth and saviors of the Greeks, averters of evil and

[105919] 172. wholly good, and that they protect the land no worse than Oedipus who lies at Colonus, or any other who is believed to lie somewhere in the land for a season for the benefit of the living. And they seem to me so far to have surpassed Solon the founder that, whereas he, sown in Salamis, is thought to guard the island for the Athenians, these men, having fallen for the post to which they were assigned, preserved all Attica—nay, all the rest of Greece besides—not only by what they themselves contributed, nor by their hindering of the invaders, but because they also showed those who came after what kind of men they must be when like occasions arose. And indeed, when even for the dead among them the gain did not fall to the ground, but they themselves enjoyed the benefit of their own purpose, and all the rest the benefit of theirs, how much greater was the advantage that accrued to those at least who lived and conquered! And for my part I think that Heracles and Pan and those who came to the battle and shared the command with Miltiades are sufficient witnesses of his courage before all mankind, and that this brings to Miltiades no less

[107074] 173. honor for his whole conduct of the city than the saying that brings honor to Pindar for his music—that this same god danced one of his songs for him. And indeed Plato's own voice says that Pan, son of Hermes, is skilled in speech; and elsewhere he says that he is reason itself, or at least brother to reason. This god, then, plainly so delighted in Miltiades himself—which he would not have done, surely, had he seen him to be a worthless man in respect of speech. Therefore he bears witness to all at once: that the rhetorical art is indeed an art, and not an artless knack; that Miltiades was both a craftsman of speech, and that his courage in deeds was a near match for his words. Is it then better, in the sight of men or of gods, to speak ill of Miltiades? One might think it strange that the Athenians, on the strength of these deeds, founded the temple to Pan, having taken thought no sooner; while Plato, with these deeds of Miltiades before him, dares to say the baser things and is so far from honoring him that, for his own part, he robs him even of the honor that justly belongs to him—him who, though one of ten generals, is named, so to speak, alone, and that too with Aristides son of Lysimachus present, whom not even Plato thought fit to single out for blame among them all, but was ashamed to do so. Yet that man was conspicuous at Plataea as a general beside Pausanias, whereas at Marathon he was hidden beneath Miltiades and did not dispute for an equal share, being just, as you say, and indeed doing what was just in this very thing.
