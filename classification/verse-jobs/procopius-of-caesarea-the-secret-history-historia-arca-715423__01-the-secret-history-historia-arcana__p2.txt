[221667] 29.5. This Eudaemon, when he had heard what has been said, himself too inquired of the emperor whether the office was secure for his nephew.

[221809] 29.6. And he, denying what he had written to Liberius, wrote letters to John, charging him to hold fast to the office with all his power.

[221948] 29.7. For he had, he said, taken no new resolve concerning it. By which John, persuaded, ordered Liberius to withdraw from the official residence, as one relieved of the office.

[222127] 29.8. But Liberius declared he would by no means obey him, being plainly led himself too by the emperor's letters.

[222243] 29.9. So John, arming his followers, went against Liberius, and he, with those about him, faced him in resistance. And a battle taking place, many others fell, and John himself, who held the office.

[222443] 29.10. So, Eudaemon pressing most strongly, Liberius was at once summoned to Byzantium, and the senate, making a judgment of what had been done, acquitted the man, since the deed had come about not through his attacking, but through his defending himself.

[222700] 29.11. The emperor, however, did not relent until he had penalized him in money secretly.

[222791] 29.12. So Justinian knew how to speak the truth and was straight of tongue. But I think it not out of season to tell something incidental to this account. For this Eudaemon died not long after, having left many kinsmen, but neither making any will nor saying anything else at all.

[223073] 29.13. And about the same time a certain man who had become commander of the eunuchs in the Palace, Euphratas by name, departed from life, leaving a nephew, but having made no disposition of his property, which was very great.

[223301] 29.14. And both their properties the emperor took away, having become heir of his own accord, and paying out not so much as three obols to any of the lawful heirs.

[223466] 29.15. With such respect did this emperor treat both the laws and the kinsmen of his intimates.

[223563] 29.16. So too he took away the right of Irenaeus, who had died long before, although he had no claim against them whatever.

[223688] 29.17. What follows on these things, which happened about the same time, I would not pass over in silence. There was a certain Anatolius who held the first place on the roll of the Ascalonites. His daughter one of the Caesareans took to wife,

[223932] 29.18. Mamilianus by name, of an exceedingly distinguished house. And the girl was an heiress, since Anatolius had been father of her alone.

[224074] 29.19. Now it was ordained of old by law that, whenever a councillor of any city should depart from among men leaving no male offspring, of the money he left a fourth should be given to the council of the city, while the heirs of the dead man should enjoy all the rest; but here too the sovereign, displaying a token of his own character, had recently written a law administering the matter the other way round, so that, whenever a councillor died childless of male offspring, his heirs should have the fourth, while all the rest the public treasury and the city roll should carry off.

[224661] 29.20. And yet never, since men have existed, had the public treasury or an emperor any share in councillors' money.

[224779] 29.21. This law, then, being in force, the final day of life came upon Anatolius, and his daughter divided his estate between the public treasury and the city council according to the law; and both the emperor himself and the Ascalonites of the roll wrote letters to her, releasing her from any dispute over these matters, on the ground that they had received what belonged to them rightly and justly.

[225182] 29.22. Later Mamilianus too departed from life, who had become son-in-law to Anatolius, leaving one daughter, who alone, as was natural, held her father's property.

[225348] 29.23. And afterward she too, while her mother was still living, came to the measure of life, having been wedded to one of the men of distinction, but having become mother of no offspring, female or male.

[225554] 29.24. But Justinian at once laid hold of all the money, uttering that marvelous saying, that it was not holy for the daughter of Anatolius, now an old woman, to grow rich on the money both of her husband and of her father.

[225779] 29.25. And, so that the woman might not thenceforth be ranked among the beggars, he ordained that this woman should receive a gold stater each day so long as she lived, setting this down in the writings by which he plundered this money: that he gave out this stater for the sake of piety. "For it is my custom," he said, "to do what is holy and pious."

[226133] 29.26. But about these things it is enough to speak, lest my account become excessive, since it is not possible for a man to recall everything.

[226278] 29.27. And that he made no account even of the Blues, who seemed to be favored by him, when money was at stake, I shall make clear.

[226411] 29.28. There was a certain Malthanes among the Cilicians, son-in-law of that Leon who held, as I have said before, the honor of the so-called referendary.

[226567] 29.29. This man he charged to check the acts of violence in Cilicia. And Malthanes, taking this pretext, wrought irreparable evils upon most of the Cilicians, and, plundering their money, sent part to the tyrant, while of the rest he thought fit to grow rich himself.

[226836] 29.30. Now the others bore their present lot in silence; but those of the Tarsians who were Blues, taking courage in the license from the emperor, in the public part of the marketplace heaped many insults upon Malthanes, who was not present among them.

[227090] 29.31. When Malthanes learned of this, bringing a great number of soldiers, he came at once to Tarsus by night, and sending the soldiers round to the houses at deep dawn, ordered them to take lodging.

[227292] 29.32. And the Blues, supposing this to be an attack, defended themselves with what they had. So it befell that other evils came about in the dark, and that Damianus,

[227460] 29.33. a man of the council, struck by an arrow, fell. Now this Damianus was the patron of the Blues there. When this came to Byzantium, the Blues, vexed, were held by much uproar throughout the city, and greatly troubled the emperor about the matter, and reviled Leon and Malthanes with most dreadful threats.

[227772] 29.34. And the sovereign too pretended to grow no less fierce at what had been done. So he at once wrote a letter ordering that an inquiry and retribution be made for what Malthanes had done.

[227965] 29.35. But Leon, paying out to him a quantity of gold, at once put a stop both to his anger and to his affection for the Blues; and the matter remaining unexamined, the emperor received Malthanes, who came to Byzantium to him, with much friendliness and held him in honor.

[228239] 29.36. But the Blues, watching for him as he came out from the emperor, inflicted blows on him in the Palace, and would have made away with him, had not some of them prevented it—those who chanced to have already received money from Leon secretly.

[228488] 29.37. And yet who would not call that state most pitiable, in which the emperor, taking a bribe, left the crimes unexamined, while the partisans, with the emperor in the Palace, dared with no hesitation to rise against one of the magistrates,

[228733] 29.38. and to lay unjust hands upon him? Yet no retribution on this account came either upon Malthanes or upon those who rose against him. From these things, if anyone wishes, let him draw proof of the character of the emperor Justinian.

[228972] 30.1. Whether he made any account of the advantage of the state, what he did to the public post and to the spies will show.

[229097] 30.2. For the sovereigns of the Romans in former times, providing that everything should be reported to them most quickly and given over with no delay—both what befell at the hands of the enemy in each region, and what happened to the cities through faction or any other unforeseen calamity, and what was done by the magistrates and all others everywhere in the Roman empire, and so that those who conveyed the yearly taxes might be brought safely through, without delay and danger—established a swift public post everywhere in the following way.

[229645] 30.3. For a day's journey of an unencumbered man they set up stations, in some places eight, in others fewer, but for the most part not less than five.

[229798] 30.4. And up to forty horses stood at each station. And grooms in proportion to the number of horses were appointed at all the stations.

[229936] 30.5. And, by frequent relays of the finest horses, those on whom this work was laid, riding always, accomplished, as it might be, a ten days' journey in a single day, and did all that I have just disclosed; and besides, the owners of the lands everywhere, especially if the lands happened to be in the interior, were on this account most prosperous.

[230288] 30.6. For, selling each year to the public treasury the crops they had in surplus, for the feeding of the horses and grooms,

[230414] 30.7. they made great sums. And it came about by these means that the public treasury ever received the taxes laid upon each, and gave them over at once to those who brought them in; and besides, the needs of the state were met.

[230644] 30.8. So matters formerly stood thus. But this sovereign first abolished the post from Chalcedon as far as Dacibiza, and compelled all, against their will, to sail at once from Byzantium as far as Helenopolis.

[230855] 30.9. So sailing in certain small boats, such as are wont to cross over there, when a storm, as it might be, fell upon them, they came into great danger. For, with urgent haste pressing upon them, it is impossible to watch for the right moment and to await the coming calm.

[231130] 30.10. Then, along the road leading to the Persians, he allowed the post to remain in its former form, but for all the rest of the East, as far as Egypt, he established only one station for a day's journey, and that not of horses,

[231362] 30.11. but of a few asses. Therefore what befalls in each region, being reported with difficulty and late, after the events, naturally meets with no help, while the owners of the fields, their crops rotting and lying useless, are ever without profit.

[231614] 30.12. The matter of the spies is as follows. Many men were from of old fed at public expense, who, going to the enemy and getting into the Persian palaces, whether under the name of trade or in some other way, and searching out everything exactly, on returning to Roman soil were able to report to the magistrates all the secrets of the enemy.

[231960] 30.13. And they, forewarned, kept guard, and nothing unforeseen befell them. This practice existed among the Medes too from of old. And Chosroes, indeed, having, as they say, made the spies' pay greater, enjoyed the providence that came from it.

[232207] 30.14. For nothing of what was done among the Romans escaped him. But Justinian, spending nothing at all, wiped out the very name of spies from Roman soil; from which many other wrongs came about, and Lazica was captured by the enemy, the Romans having in no way learned where in the world the Persian king was with his army.

[232534] 30.15. Moreover, the public treasury was from of old wont to maintain a great number of camels, which followed the Roman army when it went against the enemy, carrying all the supplies.

[232720] 30.16. And then it was not necessary for the farmers to do forced transport, nor did the soldiers want for any necessity; but these too Justinian did away with, nearly all of them. Therefore now, when the Roman army goes against the enemy, it is impossible for any of what is needful to be done.

[233017] 30.17. So, then, the most serious affairs of the state were borne along thus. But it is no worse to mention also one of his absurdities.

[233155] 30.18. Among the rhetoricians in Caesarea there was a certain Evangelus, a man of no obscurity, who, a favoring breeze of fortune having come to him, became master of much money and much land.

[233349] 30.19. And later he bought a certain seaside village, Porphyreon by name, for three centenaria of gold. Learning this, the emperor Justinian at once took away the property, paying out to him only a small portion of the price, and uttering this saying, that it could never be seemly for Evangelus, being a rhetorician, to be master of such a village.

[233700] 30.20. But about these things, having mentioned them somewhat, we shall cease to speak.

[233789] 30.21. Of the innovations made by Justinian and Theodora upon the state, these too are part. Formerly the senate, coming before the emperor, was wont to do obeisance in the following way. A patrician would do obeisance at his right breast.

[234030] 30.22. And the emperor, kissing him on the head, would dismiss him, while all the rest, bending the right knee to the emperor, departed.

[234168] 30.23. The empress, however, it was by no means the custom to do obeisance to. But before Justinian and Theodora, those making their approaches—all the rest, and as many as held the patrician rank—fell at once upon the floor on their faces, and, with hands and feet stretched out exceedingly, having touched with their lips the foot of each, rose up.

[234520] 30.24. For Theodora too did not decline this claim—she who by no means disdained even to receive the envoys of the Persians and of the other barbarians, and to present them with money, as though the Roman empire lay under her—a thing that had never happened in all time.

[234792] 30.25. And formerly those who consorted with the emperor called him emperor and his wife empress, and each of the other magistrates by whatever rank he held at the time.

[234963] 30.26. But if anyone, falling into conversation with either of these two, made mention of emperor or empress, but did not call them master and mistress, or if he tried not to name himself a slave of any of the magistrates, he was held to be so ignorant and so unbridled of tongue, and, as one who had committed the bitterest fault and insulted those whom he least ought, departed from there.

[235356] 30.27. And formerly few, and those with difficulty, were admitted to the palace; but from the time these took over the kingship, magistrates together with all the rest had their dwelling most constantly in the Palace.

[235575] 30.28. The reason was that formerly the magistrates were permitted to do what was just and lawful by their own independent judgment.

[235709] 30.29. So the magistrates, administering the customary business, remained in their own residences, and the subjects, neither seeing nor hearing anything violent on the part of the emperor, naturally troubled him little.

[235930] 30.30. But these, ever drawing all things to themselves for the harm of their subjects, compelled all to attend upon them most slavishly; and one could see, nearly every day, all the law-courts for the most part empty of men, but in the emperor's court a crowd and insolence and great jostling and slavishness forever and altogether.

[236265] 30.31. And those who seemed to be their intimates, standing there the whole day continuously and a great part of the night too forever, sleepless and without food at the proper times, were ruined; and to this their seeming prosperity came in the end.

[236517] 30.32. But, released from all these things, men contended with one another as to where in the world the money of the Romans might be.

[236652] 30.33. For some insisted that it was all among the barbarians, while others said that the emperor kept it shut up in many small chambers.

[236791] 30.34. So whenever Justinian, if he is a man, departs from life, or, as chief of the demons, lays down his life, those who chance to be then alive will know the truth.
