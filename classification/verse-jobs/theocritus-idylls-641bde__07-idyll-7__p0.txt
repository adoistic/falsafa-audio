CHAPTER: 07-idyll-7



[0] 7. The Harvest-Home There was a time when Eucritus and I were walking from the city

[85] toward the Hales, and a third with us was Amyntas;

[137] for to Demeter, Phrasidamus and Antigenes were keeping

[193] the harvest-feast, two sons of Lycopeus, the best of any noble line

[262] descended from above, from Clytia and from Chalcon himself,

[323] who brought the spring Burina forth at the touch of his foot,

[386] setting his knee firmly against the rock; and beside it

[443] poplars and elms wove a shady grove,

[481] roofed over and leafy with their green foliage.

[530] We had not yet finished half the road, nor had the tomb

[587] of Brasilas come into our sight, when by the favor of the Muses

[652] we met a good wayfarer, a man of Cydonia,

[695] Lycidas by name, a goatherd, nor could anyone

[742] mistake him at a glance, for he looked a goatherd through and through.

[814] For from a shaggy, rough-haired he-goat he had

[862] a tawny skin upon his shoulders, smelling of fresh rennet,

[922] and about his breast an old cloak was girt

[966] with a broad belt, and in his right hand he held

[1016] a crooked club of wild olive. And gently, with a grin

[1071] and a smiling eye, he spoke to me, and laughter clung to his lip:

[1138] Simichidas, where are you dragging your feet at noon,

[1193] when even the lizard sleeps in the dry-stone walls,

[1246] and the crested larks of the tombs do not roam abroad?

[1302] Are you hurrying to a feast you were bidden to? Or do you leap

[1366] to some townsman's wine-press? For as you go, every

[1419] stone strikes ringing against your boots and sings.

[1472] And I answered him: Dear Lycidas, all men say

[1519] you are by far the best of pipers among the herdsmen

[1573] and among the reapers. This greatly warms my heart;

[1626] and yet in my own mind I hope to be your match.

[1675] This journey is to a harvest-feast; for some comrades

[1730] are making a banquet to fair-robed Demeter,

[1775] offering the first-fruits of their wealth; for with a rich measure

[1843] the goddess has filled their threshing-floor with abundant barley.

[1911] But come now—the road is common to us, and common too the dawn—

[1976] let us make our herdsman's songs; perhaps one will profit the other.

[2046] For I too am a clear-voiced mouth of the Muses, and all men call me

[2115] the best of singers; but I am not quick to believe,

[2168] no, by Earth; for in my own judgment I do not yet outsing

[2227] either the noble Sicelidas of Samos or Philitas,

[2277] but contend like a frog against the crickets.

[2324] So I spoke, on purpose; and the goatherd, with a sweet laugh,

[2387] said: I give you this club as a gift, because you are

[2442] a sapling shaped by Zeus, all true.

[2479] For I greatly hate the builder who tries

[2521] to raise a house as high as the peak of Oromedon the mountain,

[2585] and those cocks of the Muses who, crowing in rivalry

[2639] against the bard of Chios, toil all in vain.

[2685] But come, let us quickly begin the herdsman's song,

[2738] Simichidas; and I—see, friend, whether you like

[2787] this little song that I worked at on the hill the other day.

[2849] Lycidas

[2858] A fair voyage to Mitylene shall Ageanax have,

[2905] both when the south wind drives the wet waves before the Kids at evening,

[2980] and when Orion sets his feet upon the ocean,

[3026] if only he rescues Lycidas, who is scorched by Aphrodite;

[3085] for hot love for him consumes me.

[3120] And the halcyons shall calm the waves and the sea

[3171] and the south wind and the east, that stirs the seaweed in the deeps;

[3242] the halcyons, of all birds dearest to the gray Nereids,

[3299] and to all whose prey is gathered from the sea.

[3348] For Ageanax, who seeks a voyage to Mitylene,

[3394] may all be seasonable, and may he reach a safe harbor.

[3450] And I, on that day, with anise or roses

[3491] or with a wreath of white violets about my head,

[3541] will draw the Ptelean wine from the bowl,

[3584] lying beside the fire, while someone roasts a bean in the embers.

[3651] And the couch will be heaped a cubit deep

[3694] with fleabane and asphodel and curling celery.

[3742] And I will drink at ease, remembering Ageanax,

[3790] draining the very cups, pressing my lip to the dregs.

[3845] Two shepherds will play the flute for me, one from Acharnae,

[3907] one from Lycope; and Tityrus close by will sing

[3956] how once Daphnis the cowherd loved Xenea,

[3999] and how the mountain grieved about him, and how the oaks lamented him,

[4071] those that grow along the banks of the river Himeras,

[4126] while he melted like snow under high Haemus

[4171] or Athos or Rhodope or the far Caucasus.

[4213] And he will sing how once the wide chest received the goatherd,

[4278] still living, through the wicked malice of his lord,

[4332] and how the snub-nosed bees, coming from the meadow,

[4386] fed him in the sweet cedar-chest with soft flowers,

[4439] because the Muse poured sweet nectar over his mouth.

[4493] O blessed Comatas, these are the joys you knew;

[4542] you too were shut into the chest, and you, fed on the bees'

[4603] honeycomb, lived out the spring of the year.

[4649] Would that you had been counted among the living in my day,

[4710] that I might have pastured your fair goats upon the hills,

[4770] listening to your voice, while you under the oaks or the pines

[4834] lay piping sweetly, godlike Comatas.

[4872] When he had said so much he ceased; and after him in turn

[4931] I too spoke thus: Dear Lycidas, many other

[4975] fair things have the Nymphs taught me too, as I herded on the hills,

[5045] songs that perhaps even rumor has carried to the throne of Zeus;

[5111] but this is the best by far of all, with which I will begin

[5172] to honor you; only listen, since you are dear to the Muses.

[5233] Simichidas

[5245] The Loves have sneezed for Simichidas; for the poor wretch

[5305] loves Myrto as much as goats love the spring.

[5352] But Aratus, dearest of all to that man,

[5393] keeps in his heart a longing for a boy. Aristis knows it,

[5452] a good man, a great and excellent man, whom not even Phoebus himself

[5522] would grudge to sing with his lyre beside the tripods,

[5578] how Aratus from a boy has been burned to the bone with love.

[5640] For him, Pan, you who have the lovely plain of Homole for your portion,

[5713] set the boy unbidden in his dear hands,

[5754] whether it is soft Philinus or some other.

[5798] And if you do this, dear Pan, then may no Arcadian boys

[5855] scourge your sides and shoulders with squills

[5902] when there is scant meat at hand;

[5937] but if you nod the other way, then may you scratch and bite

[5998] yourself all over with your nails, and sleep among the nettles,

[6063] and may you be in the mountains of the Edonians at midwinter,

[6126] turned toward the river Hebrus, close to the Bear,

[6178] and in summer pasture among the farthest Ethiopians

[6231] under the rock of the Blemyes, whence the Nile is seen no more.

[6296] But you Loves, leaving the sweet stream of Hyetis and Byblis,

[6359] and Oecus, the steep seat of golden Dione,

[6403] you who are like to ruddy apples,

[6438] strike for me with your bows the lovely Philinus,

[6489] strike him, since the wretch has no pity on my friend the stranger.

[6558] And indeed he is now more overripe than a pear, and the women

[6621] say, Alas, Philinus, your fair bloom is dropping away.

[6677] Let us no longer keep watch at his doors, Aratus,

[6728] nor wear out our feet; but let the cock at dawn,

[6778] crowing, give some other man over to bitter numbness,

[6833] and let one man only, good Molon, be throttled at that wrestling-school,

[6907] while ease be our care, and an old woman be at hand,

[6961] who, spitting, may ward off all that is unlovely.

[7012] So much I said; and he, with a sweet laugh

[7056] as before, gave me the throwing-stick to be a gift of friendship from the Muses.

[7138] And he, turning aside to the left, took the road

[7188] to Pyxa; while Eucritus and I, and the fair Amyntichus,

[7245] turning to the house of Phrasidamus, lay down

[7292] among deep beds of sweet rushes,

[7326] rejoicing in the newly stripped vine-leaves.

[7372] Many poplars and elms waved above

[7407] over our heads; and close by, the holy water

[7453] trickled murmuring from the cave of the Nymphs.

[7502] In the shady boughs the sun-burnt cicadas

[7545] kept their chattering toil; and far off the tree-frog

[7600] cried among the thick thorns of the brambles.

[7647] The larks and goldfinches sang, the turtle-dove moaned,

[7704] and the tawny bees flitted about the springs.

[7751] All breathed of rich summer, all breathed of harvest-time.

[7811] Pears at our feet, and apples by our sides

[7855] rolled in abundance; and the boughs hung down to the ground

[7916] weighted with sloes;

[7938] and the four-year-old seal was loosed from the wine-jars' mouths.

[8005] You Nymphs of Castalia, who hold the peak of Parnassus,

[8062] was it ever such a bowl as this that old Chiron

[8111] set up for Heracles in the rocky cave of Pholus?

[8161] Was it such nectar that prompted that shepherd by the Anapus,

[8224] mighty Polyphemus, who hurled mountains at ships,

[8275] to dance about the steadings on his feet,

[8318] as the draught you then mingled for us, you Nymphs,

[8371] beside the altar of Demeter of the threshing-floor? Upon her heap

[8438] may I once again plant the great winnowing-fan,

[8487] while she smiles, holding sheaves and poppies in both her hands.
