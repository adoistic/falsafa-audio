CHAPTER: 01-the-secret-history-historia-arcana



[0] 1.1. All that has befallen the Roman nation in its wars down to the present I have already set out, having arranged my account of all those events by the appropriate occasions and places. What follows from here, however, will no longer be composed by me in the manner I have described, for here everything will be written down that has happened in every part of the Roman empire.

[381] 1.2. The reason is that, while those who did these deeds were still alive, it was not possible to record them as they ought to have been recorded. It was impossible to escape the notice of swarms of spies, and impossible, once detected, not to perish by the most pitiable death; for I could not put my confidence even in my closest kinsmen.

[723] 1.3. Indeed, in much of what I said in my earlier books I was forced to conceal the causes of events. So now I shall have to disclose both what until now has remained unspoken and the causes of what I set forth before.

[943] 1.4. But as I turn to a different contest, a hard one and terribly difficult to wage—the lives that Justinian and Theodora lived—I find myself stammering and shrinking back, reckoning over and over that what I am now going to write will seem to those who come after neither credible nor probable; and especially since, when in the long course of time the report has grown old, I fear I may even acquire the reputation of a teller of myths and be ranked among the tragic poets.

[1421] 1.5. Yet I shall not flinch from the vast bulk of my task, taking courage in this: that my account will not be left without witnesses. For the men of today, being the best-informed witnesses of these deeds, will be trustworthy guarantors, passing on to future time their confidence in what I report.

[1722] 1.6. And yet there was another thing besides that often held me back for a very long time, eager though I was to undertake this account. For I supposed it would be harmful to future generations, since it is most expedient that the foulest of deeds remain unknown to later time, rather than that they come to the ears of tyrants and be imitated.

[2068] 1.7. For most rulers, through ignorance, always find it easy to imitate the evil deeds of their predecessors, and they turn ever more readily and effortlessly to the wrongs committed by men of old.

[2267] 1.8. But afterward what brought me to the history of these deeds was this: that it will be plain to future tyrants that retribution for their crimes will in all likelihood overtake them, just as it befell these persons to suffer; and further, that their deeds and their characters will be on record forever, and so perhaps they will be more reluctant to transgress.

[2634] 1.9. For who among later men would have known of the unbridled life of Semiramis, or of the madness of Sardanapalus and Nero, had not these memorials been left by those who wrote in their day? And besides, to those who may suffer the like at the hands of tyrants this hearing will be by no means without profit.

[2947] 1.10. For the unfortunate are accustomed to console themselves with the thought that their disasters did not fall on them alone. For these reasons, then, I shall first proceed to tell what wicked things were done by Belisarius; and afterward I shall reveal what wicked things were done by Justinian and Theodora.

[3261] 1.11. Belisarius had a wife, whom I mentioned in my earlier books. Her grandfather and her father were charioteers who had displayed their craft in Byzantium and Thessalonica, and her mother was one of the women who had prostituted themselves on the stage.

[3519] 1.12. This woman, who in her earlier days had lived a lewd life and had broken loose in her ways, having had much to do with her father's sorcerers and learned what was useful to her, became in time the wedded wife of Belisarius,

[3750] 1.13. though already the mother of many children. From the very outset she chose to be an adulteress, but she was at pains to conceal this practice—not out of any shame at her own pursuits, nor from any fear of her husband (for she never felt shame at any deed whatsoever, and she had got her husband fast in the grip of many spells), but because she dreaded retribution from the empress. For Theodora was exceedingly fierce against her and bared her teeth at her.

[4216] 1.14. But once she had made Theodora pliant by serving her in matters of the greatest urgency—first by doing away with Silverius in a manner that will be told in a later book, and afterward by destroying John the Cappadocian, as I have related in my earlier books—then, with less fear and no longer concealing anything, she did not scruple to commit every kind of crime.

[4588] 1.15. There was a certain young man from Thrace in the household of Belisarius, Theodosius by name, who had been brought up in the belief of those called Eunomians by his forefathers.

[4773] 1.16. When Belisarius was about to sail for Libya, he bathed this youth in the sacred font, and taking him up in his own hands made him, together with his wife, an adopted son, in the way that Christians are accustomed to adopt; and so Antonina, since Theodosius was now her son by holy ordinance, loved him, as was natural, and kept him about her among those she cared for most.

[5154] 1.17. And she fell extravagantly in love with him at once during this voyage, and being utterly possessed by her passion she shook off all fear and shame, whether of things divine or human, and had intercourse with him—at first secretly, but in the end even with servants and maids present.

[5446] 1.18. For now, gripped by this lust and manifestly love-stricken, she could no longer see any obstacle to the deed. And once, in Carthage, Belisarius caught them in the very act, yet allowed himself to be deceived by his wife.

[5674] 1.19. For finding the two of them in an underground chamber, he was beside himself with rage; but she, neither flinching nor abashed at the deed, said, "I came here to hide the most precious of the spoils with the young man, so that they should not become known to the emperor."

[5954] 1.20. This she said by way of pretext, and he, pretending to be persuaded, let it go—though he saw that Theodosius had loosened the cord that fastened his trousers around his loins; for, compelled by love of the woman, he chose to disbelieve the testimony of his own eyes.

[6228] 1.21. As her lewdness steadily advanced to unspeakable evil, the others who saw what was going on held their peace; but a certain slave-woman named Macedonia, at Syracuse, when Belisarius had conquered Sicily, bound her master by the most dreadful oaths that he would never betray her to her mistress, and then disclosed to him the whole story, providing as witnesses two slave-boys whose task it was to attend to the bedchamber.

[6659] 1.22. On learning this, Belisarius ordered some of his attendants to put Theodosius to death.

[6754] 1.23. But Theodosius, learning of it beforehand, fled to Ephesus. For most of the attendants, swayed by the unsteadiness of the man's resolve, were more eager to please the wife than to seem well-disposed to the husband, and so they betrayed the orders then laid upon them concerning Theodosius.

[7051] 1.24. Constantinus, seeing Belisarius overcome with grief at what had happened, sympathized with him in general and added this remark: "For my part, I would sooner have made away with the wife than with the young man."

[7271] 1.25. When Antonina learned of this, she nursed a secret grudge against him, so as to show forth her rancorous

[7383] 1.26. hatred against him. For she was scorpion-like and dark in her wrath. Not long after, either by sorcery or by flattery, she persuaded her husband that the woman's accusation was not sound; and he, with no delay, sent for Theodosius, and undertook to hand Macedonia and the slave-boys over to his wife.

[7691] 1.27. All these she first—so they say—had their tongues cut out, then cut them up little by little, and stuffing them into sacks threw them into the sea without any hesitation, one of the household servants, Eugenius by name, assisting her in the whole abomination—the same man by whom the foul deed against Silverius was carried out.

[8027] 1.28. And not long afterward Belisarius, persuaded by his wife, killed Constantinus too. For the affair of Praesidius and the daggers happened at this time, as I have shown in my earlier books.

[8222] 1.29. For when the man was about to be set free, Antonina did not relent until she had exacted from him the penalty

[8339] 1.30. of the speech I just now mentioned. And from this Belisarius incurred great hatred both from the emperor and from all the men of distinction among the Romans.

[8505] 1.31. So matters went thus. But Theodosius said he was not able to come to Italy, where Belisarius and Antonina were then staying, unless Photius were got out of the way.

[8677] 1.32. For Photius was naturally prone to take offense if anyone could outdo him in influence with anybody, and in the case of Theodosius he had every reason to choke with vexation—because he himself, though a son, counted for nothing, while the other wielded great power and clothed himself in vast wealth.

[8985] 1.33. For they say that from Carthage and Ravenna he had plundered the two palaces to the amount of a hundred centenaria and kept it, since to him alone it fell to handle these things at his own discretion.

[9193] 1.34. But Antonina, when she learned the mind of Theodosius, did not let up in laying snares for her son and pursuing him with murderous plots, until she contrived that he, no longer able to bear the ambushes, departed thence and set out for Byzantium, while Theodosius came to her in Italy.

[9486] 1.35. There she sated herself to the full both with her lover's company and with her husband's folly, and in time came with the two of them to Byzantium.

[9641] 1.36. There the consciousness of guilt began to terrify Theodosius and to twist his mind. For he thought he could never escape detection entirely, since he saw that the woman was no longer able to keep her passion hidden, nor that it had broken out in secret, but that she did not in the least disdain to be openly an adulteress and to be so called.

[9992] 1.37. Therefore he went back again to Ephesus and, shaving his head in the customary fashion, enrolled himself among those called monks.

[10130] 1.38. Then indeed she went utterly mad, and changing her dress along with her manner of life to the mode of mourning, she went about the house constantly wailing, and using shrieks she lamented—though her husband was at her side—what a good thing had perished for her: how faithful, how charming, how kindly, how able a man.

[10456] 1.39. And in the end she drew her husband too into these lamentations and set him weeping. At any rate the wretched man wept, calling on the beloved Theodosius.

[10618] 1.40. Later he even went to the emperor, and entreating both him and the empress, persuaded them to send for Theodosius, on the ground that he was, and would be, indispensable to his household.

[10813] 1.41. But Theodosius refused to leave that place at all, asserting that he would most securely hold fast to the monk's calling.

[10942] 1.42. This, however, was a feigned excuse, so that as soon as Belisarius should leave Byzantium, he himself might come secretly to Antonina. And so indeed it came to pass.

[11115] 2.1. For at once Belisarius set out, with Photius, to make war on Chosroes, and Antonina remained behind—a thing she had not been accustomed to do before.

[11271] 2.2. For, that the man might not come to himself when left alone, and, making light of her spells, take some fitting thought concerning her, she made it her care to set out with him to every corner of the earth.

[11484] 2.3. And in order that Theodosius might once more have access to Antonina, she made it her business to get Photius out of the way.

[11616] 2.4. So she persuaded some of Belisarius' followers to mock and revile him perpetually, letting slip no opportunity; and she herself, writing nearly every day, slandered him continually and stirred up everything against her son.

[11846] 2.5. Driven by this, the young man resolved to deal with his mother as an enemy: when someone came from Byzantium who reported that Theodosius was secretly staying with Antonina, he brought him at once before Belisarius, charging him to tell the whole story.

[12106] 2.6. When Belisarius learned this, exceedingly enraged, he fell upon his face at Photius' feet and begged him to avenge him, who was suffering unholy treatment at the hands of those from whom it was least fitting, saying: "Sweetest child, you do not in the least know who your father was, since he left you while you were still nursed at the breast and measured out his own span of life, nor did you profit at all from anything of his; for in property he was not very prosperous.

[12587] 2.7. But brought up under me, though I am only a stepfather, you are of such an age that it is for you to defend me when I am most grievously wronged; you have attained to the rank of consul and are clothed in such wealth that I—and justly so, noble youth—might be called father and mother and the whole of your kin, and might rightly be such.

[12932] 2.8. For it is not by blood, but by deeds, that men are wont to measure their affection for one another.

[13038] 2.9. It is time, then, for you not to look on while I, beside the ruin of my house, am robbed of so vast a sum of money, and your mother fastens upon herself so great a disgrace in the eyes of all men.

[13241] 2.10. Bear in mind, too, that the sins of women do not fall upon their husbands alone, but touch their children even more—who will likely be reputed to resemble in character those who bore them.

[13437] 2.11. And think of me thus: that I love my own wife exceedingly, and if it be granted me to take vengeance on the destroyer of my house, I will do her no harm; but while Theodosius lives I cannot allow her this charge."

[13658] 2.12. Hearing this, Photius promised to assist in everything, though he feared he might suffer some harm from it, not having much confidence, in view of the unsteadiness of Belisarius' resolve where his wife was concerned; for many things gave him pause, and not least the fate of Macedonia.

[13951] 2.13. Therefore the two of them swore to each other all the oaths that are reckoned and named most dreadful among Christians, never to betray each other even amid the dangers that lead to destruction.

[14153] 2.14. For the present, then, they decided it was inexpedient to set their hand to the deed; but when Antonina should come from Byzantium, and Theodosius go to Ephesus, then Photius, being in Ephesus, should master Theodosius and the money with no trouble.

[14410] 2.15. At that time, then, they themselves made their invasion with the whole army into the land of Persia; and concerning John the Cappadocian in Byzantium there happened what I have related in my earlier books.

[14623] 2.16. There this one thing only was passed over by me out of fear: that Antonina deceived John and his daughter not at random, but by binding them with a multitude of oaths—than which nothing is held more dreadful among Christians—that she would use no treacherous intent against them.

[14910] 2.17. Having brought this about, and trusting still more in the empress' friendship, she sent Theodosius to Ephesus, while she herself, suspecting no opposition, made her way to the East.

[15099] 2.18. Belisarius had just taken the fortress of Sisauranon when she was reported to him on the road as coming. And he, reckoning everything else of no account, drew his army back.

[15280] 2.19. For there had happened, as I have related before, certain other things in the camp that urged him to retreat. But this report hastened his withdrawal still more.

[15449] 2.20. But, as I said at the beginning of this account, it did not seem to me free of danger at that time to tell all the causes of what was done.

[15596] 2.21. And from this a charge arose against Belisarius among all the Romans, that he had set the most vital interests of the state below his domestic affairs.

[15755] 2.22. For at first, gripped by passion for his wife, he was unwilling to go far from Roman soil, so that, as soon as he should learn that his wife had come from Byzantium, he might turn back at once and be able to catch and punish her.

[15992] 2.23. For this reason he ordered Arethas and his men to cross the river Tigris, and they, having accomplished nothing worth mention, returned home; while he himself was at pains not to be left even a day's journey

[16207] 2.24. from the Roman frontiers. For the fortress of Sisauranon is distant from the Roman borders more than a day's journey for an unencumbered man going by way of the city of Nisibis, and at another point the interval is the same.

[16439] 2.25. And yet, if at the outset he had been willing to cross the river Tigris with his whole army, I think he would have plundered all the regions of Assyria and reached the city of Ctesiphon with no one at all opposing him, and would have rescued the captives of Antioch and all the other Romans who happened to be there, and returned to his homeland. He was also most responsible for Chosroes returning home from Colchis with less fear. In what manner this came about I shall now make clear.

[16934] 2.26. When Chosroes, son of Cabades, invaded the land of Colchis and accomplished the other things I have related before, and took Petra, it happened that many of the Median army perished both in the war and through the difficulties of the ground. For Lazica is hard to traverse, as I have said, and altogether precipitous.

[17259] 2.27. And besides, a plague fell upon them, and most of the army perished; many of them also died for lack of necessities.

[17383] 2.28. Meanwhile certain men coming from the land of Persia reported that Belisarius had defeated Nabedes in battle near the city of Nisibis and was advancing, that he had taken the fortress of Sisauranon by siege and made captives of Bleschames and eight hundred Persian horsemen, and that he had sent another Roman army with Arethas the leader of the Saracens, which had crossed the river Tigris and was ravaging all the regions there, hitherto unspoiled.

[17841] 2.29. Chosroes had also sent an army of Huns against the Armenians who were subjects of the Romans, so that, by their preoccupation with these, no perception might come to the Romans there of what was being done in Lazica.

[18065] 2.30. But others reported that these barbarians, having met Valerian and the Romans in battle, had been worsted by far, most of them perishing.

[18210] 2.31. When the Persians heard this—being already worn down by hardship in the land of the Lazi, and fearing that, falling in on the retreat with some enemy army among precipices and wooded places, they might all be destroyed in disorder—being in great fear for their children and wives and their country, then, if there was anything sound in the Median army, they began to revile Chosroes, charging him with having sinned against his oaths and the common laws of all mankind: that in time of truce he had invaded the land of the Romans without any right, and was wronging a state ancient and most worthy of all, which he could not overcome in war; and they were on the point of attempting some revolution.

[18917] 2.32. Thrown into confusion by this, Chosroes found this remedy for the trouble. He read out to them a letter that the empress had recently happened to write to Zaberganes.

[19091] 2.33. The writing declared the following: "How highly I esteem you, Zaberganes, believing you to be well-disposed to our affairs, you know, having come to us on an embassy not long ago.

[19278] 2.34. You would, then, act in keeping with the opinion I have of you, if you should persuade King Chosroes to take peaceful counsel toward our state.

[19429] 2.35. For in that case I guarantee that great benefits will come to you from my husband, who would do nothing

[19540] 2.36. at all without my judgment." When Chosroes had read this out and reproached the Persian nobles, asking whether they considered that a state which a woman directed,

[19711] 2.37. he managed to check the impulse of the men. Yet even so he departed thence in great fear, supposing that Belisarius and his men would stand in his way. But when none of the enemy met him, he gladly made his way back to his own land.

[19951] 3.1. When Belisarius came into the land of the Romans, he found his wife arrived from Byzantium. He kept her under guard in disgrace, and though he often set his hand to making away with her, he grew soft—it seems to me,

[20173] 3.2. overcome by some burning passion. They say, too, that he was caught by his wife's spells and at once dissolved. But Photius was sent in haste to Ephesus, having bound one of the eunuchs, Calligonus by name, who was his mistress' pander, and keeping him with him; and this man, being tortured on the journey, disclosed all the secrets.

[20514] 3.3. Theodosius, learning of it beforehand, fled for refuge to the sanctuary of John the Apostle, which is there most holy and held in fair honor.

[20662] 3.4. But Andreas, the high priest of Ephesus, persuaded by money, handed the man over. Meanwhile Theodora, alarmed for Antonina—for she had heard all that had befallen her—sent for Belisarius with her to Byzantium.

[20878] 3.5. When Photius heard this, he sent Theodosius to Cilicia, where his bodyguards and shield-bearers happened to be wintering, charging the escorts to convey the man with the utmost secrecy, and on reaching Cilicia to keep him most carefully under guard, giving no one any perception of where in the world he was. He himself came to Byzantium with Calligonus and with Theodosius' money, which was considerable.

[21290] 3.6. There the empress gave a demonstration to all men that she knew how to requite murderous favors with greater and fouler gifts.

[21423] 3.7. For Antonina had lately entrapped and betrayed to her one enemy, the Cappadocian; but the empress, putting a multitude of men into Antonina's hands, did away with them without any charge.

[21617] 3.8. For some of those friendly to Belisarius and Photius she tortured in body, bringing this one accusation only—that they were well-disposed to these two men—and so disposed of them that to what end fortune brought them we do not even now know; others she punished with exile, bringing the same charge.

[21923] 3.9. One, however, of those who had followed Photius to Ephesus, Theodosius by name, though he had attained senatorial rank, she stripped of his property, and set him in an underground and wholly murky chamber, fastening a noose around his neck from a certain manger—so short that it was always taut and never at all slack.

[22248] 3.10. The wretch stood continually at this manger, eating and taking his sleep and accomplishing all the other needs of nature, and nothing else was left him to make him like the asses

[22434] 3.11. except braying. In this manner of life a span of not less than four months was worn away for the man, until, seized with the disease of melancholy and gone utterly mad, he was thus released from this prison and then died.

[22663] 3.12. And she compelled Belisarius, against his will, to be reconciled with his wife Antonina. As for Photius, surrounding him with other slavish tortures and scourging him many times over the back and shoulders, she ordered him to tell where in the world Theodosius and the pander were.

[22952] 3.13. But he, though racked by the torture, resolved to keep the oaths he had sworn—a man who, though sickly and slack before, and given to bodily indulgence, had never had experience of outrage or any hardship.

[23165] 3.14. At any rate he himself betrayed none of Belisarius' secrets. Later, however, all that had until then been secret came to light.

[23300] 3.15. And Calligonus she found there and handed over to Antonina; but Theodosius she summoned to Byzantium, and when he arrived she at once hid him in the Palace, and on the next day, sending for Antonina,

[23507] 3.16. said: "Dearest patrician lady, a pearl fell into my hands yesterday such as no one ever beheld. And if you wish, I would not begrudge you the sight of it,

[23669] 3.17. but will show it to you." And she, not understanding what was being done, earnestly entreated her to show her the pearl. But the empress brought Theodosius out from a little room belonging to one of the eunuchs

[23887] 3.18. and displayed him. Antonina, at first overjoyed beyond measure, remained speechless for pleasure, and acknowledged that the empress had done her many favors, calling her savior and benefactress and very mistress.

[24107] 3.19. This Theodosius the empress kept in the Palace and indulged with luxury and every other delight, and threatened that she would soon make him a general of the Romans.

[24280] 3.20. But some forestalling justice, falling on him with the disease of dysentery, removed him from among men.

[24392] 3.21. Now Theodora had certain chambers, hidden and altogether secret, murky and without neighbors, where there was no telling of either night or day.

[24544] 3.22. There she kept Photius shut up for a long time. From this place it befell him by some chance to make his escape and get away—not once only, but twice.

[24702] 3.23. The first time, fleeing for refuge to the church of the Mother of God, which is the holiest in Byzantium and so named, he sat as a suppliant beside the sacred table. But she raised him from there by main force and shut him up again.

[24942] 3.24. The second time, coming to the sanctuary of Sophia, he suddenly seated himself in the very divine font, which Christians have held most of all in reverence.

[25106] 3.25. But even from there the woman was able to drag him away. For no place was ever inviolable or untouched by her, but to violate all sanctuaries seemed to her no matter at all.

[25287] 3.26. And along with the people, the priests of the Christians, struck down by fear, stood aside and yielded everything to her.

[25416] 3.27. Three years' time, then, was worn away for him in this manner of life; but later the prophet Zacharias stood over him in a dream and bade him by oaths, they say, to flee, promising to assist him in this undertaking.

[25639] 3.28. Persuaded by this vision, he rose up from there and, escaping notice, came to Jerusalem; and though ten thousand men were searching for him, none caught sight of the young man, even when they met him.

[25847] 3.29. There, shaving his head and putting on the habit of those called monks, he was able to escape the punishment of Theodora.

[25976] 3.30. But Belisarius, having disregarded his oaths and having chosen in no way to avenge Photius when he suffered, as has been said, unholy treatment, found thereafter, as was to be expected, all his undertakings hostile through the act of God. For straightway, being sent against the Medes and Chosroes when they invaded Roman territory for the third time, he incurred a charge of cowardice.

[26370] 3.31. And yet he seemed to have accomplished something worth mention by shaking off the war from there. But when Chosroes, crossing the river Euphrates, took the populous city of Callinicum with no one defending it, and enslaved many tens of thousands of Romans, and Belisarius was not even eager to pursue the enemy, he carried off the reputation of one of two things—either of deliberate cowardice or of fear—and so he remained where he was.

[26815] 4.1. About this time another thing happened to befall him, as follows. The plague, of which I made mention in my earlier books, was ravaging the people in Byzantium. And it happened that the emperor Justinian fell most grievously ill, so that it was even said he had perished.

[27093] 4.2. Rumor, carrying this report about, conveyed it all the way to the Roman camp. There certain of the commanders said that if the Romans set up some other emperor for themselves in Byzantium, they would never permit it.

[27316] 4.3. A little later it happened that the emperor recovered, and that the commanders of the Roman army became mutual accusers of one another.

[27458] 4.4. For Peter the general and John, whom they called by the surname Phagas, insisted that they had heard Belisarius and Bouzes say the things I have just disclosed.

[27625] 4.5. The empress Theodora, charging that these things had been said against herself, became full of wrath against the men.

[27749] 4.6. So summoning all of them at once to Byzantium, and making an inquiry into this matter, she sent suddenly for Bouzes into the women's quarters, as though to confer with him on some most urgent affair.

[27955] 4.7. Now there was a certain chamber in the Palace underground, secure and labyrinthine and such as to be likened to Tartarus, where she kept shut up those who, for the most part, had given offense.

[28155] 4.8. Into this pit, then, Bouzes too was cast, and there a man of consular rank remained, ever ignorant of the present moment.

[28283] 4.9. For neither could he himself, sitting in darkness, distinguish whether it was day or night,

[28381] 4.10. nor was he able to meet anyone else. For the man who threw his food to him each day, like a beast meeting a beast, held wordless converse with the wordless.

[28545] 4.11. And at once everyone supposed him dead, but no one dared to make any mention or remembrance of him. After two years and four months she took pity on the man and let him go.

[28725] 4.12. And he was seen by all as though restored to life. From that time it befell the man ever after to be dim of sight and sickly in the rest of his body.

[28882] 4.13. So matters concerning Bouzes went thus. As for Belisarius, the emperor—though he was convicted of none of the things charged against him—at the empress' insistence relieved him of the command he held, and appointed Martin general of the East in his place; and he gave orders that Belisarius' bodyguards and shield-bearers, and whatever of his household servants were of proven worth in war, be distributed among certain of the commanders and of the eunuchs in the Palace.

[29361] 4.14. And they, casting lots for them, divided them all, arms and all, among themselves, as it chanced to fall to each.

[29482] 4.15. And to many of his friends and to others who had served him before, he forbade to go to Belisarius any longer.

[29600] 4.16. And there went about a bitter spectacle and an incredible sight: Belisarius a private man in Byzantium, almost alone, always brooding and gloomy, dreading death from a plot.

[29781] 4.17. When the empress learned that he had much money in the East, she sent one of the eunuchs of the Palace and fetched it all.

[29911] 4.18. Now Antonina, as I have said, had become at variance with her husband, but was dearest and most necessary to the empress beyond all others, since she had lately destroyed John the Cappadocian.

[30111] 4.19. Therefore the empress, resolving to gratify Antonina, did everything so that the wife might seem to beg off her husband and to rescue him from such great misfortunes; and thus not only would Antonina be wholly reconciled with the wretched man, but also openly, as one saved by her, she might snatch him up as her captive.

[30440] 4.20. It came about thus. Belisarius came once in the morning to the Palace, as was his custom, with a few wretched men.

[30562] 4.21. Meeting with no goodwill from the emperor and empress, but even insulted there by base and common men, he departed homeward toward late afternoon, turning round many times in this withdrawal and looking everywhere to see from what quarter those who would destroy him might be coming upon him.

[30862] 4.22. With this dread he went up into his chamber and sat alone upon his couch, thinking nothing noble, nor remembering that he had ever been a man, but sweating continually and dizzy and at a loss with much trembling, worn down by slavish fears and anxieties, both cowardly and altogether unmanly.

[31162] 4.23. Antonina, as though neither knowing at all what was being done nor awaiting anything that was to come, kept taking many walks there, pretending an upset stomach.

[31331] 4.24. For they were still suspicious of each other. Meanwhile a certain man from the Palace, Quadratus by name, came when the sun had already set, and crossing the courtyard suddenly stood at the door of the men's apartment, declaring that he had been sent there by the empress.

[31611] 4.25. When Belisarius heard this, he drew in his hands and feet upon the couch and lay on his back, utterly ready for the slaughter; so completely had all manliness deserted him.

[31791] 4.26. Quadratus, not yet having come in to him, showed him a letter from the empress.

[31878] 4.27. The writing declared the following: "What you have done to us, most excellent sir, you know. But I, owing much to your wife, have resolved to remit to you all these charges, granting your life to her as a gift.

[32096] 4.28. From now on, then, you may take courage for your safety and your money; and what sort of man you will be toward her we shall know by your future conduct."

[32258] 4.29. When Belisarius had read this, at once lifted up to great joy by pleasure, and at the same time wishing to give present proof of his disposition, he rose up and fell on his face at his wife's feet.

[32463] 4.30. And clasping both her legs with either hand, and ever shifting his tongue along the soles of the woman's feet, he called her the cause of his life and his safety, and acknowledged that henceforth he would be her faithful slave and not her husband.

[32718] 4.31. And of the money the empress gave thirty centenaria of gold to the emperor, and gave the rest back to Belisarius.

[32839] 4.32. So matters concerning Belisarius the general went thus—the man to whom fortune, not long before, had handed over Gelimer and Vittigis as captives of his spear.

[33006] 4.33. From of old the wealth of this man had keenly galled Justinian and Theodora, being excessive and worthy of a royal court.

[33135] 4.34. They said that he had secretly hidden away the greater part of the public treasures of Gelimer and Vittigis, and had given the emperor a small portion of them, and one in no way considerable.

[33334] 4.35. But reckoning over the man's labors and the calumny of outsiders, and at the same time having no plausible pretext against him, they kept quiet.

[33486] 4.36. Then, however, the empress, seizing him while he was terrified and utterly cowardly, contrived by a single act to become mistress of his entire property.

[33647] 4.37. For they at once entered into a marriage alliance with each other: Ioannina, the daughter of Belisarius, of whom alone he was father, was betrothed to Anastasius, the empress' grandson.

[33840] 4.38. Belisarius then thought to recover his own command, and, being appointed general of the East, to lead the Roman army again against Chosroes and the Medes; but Antonina by no means allowed it, for she said she had been insulted by him in those regions, and these she would never again look upon.

[34142] 4.39. Therefore Belisarius, appointed commander of the imperial grooms, was sent to Italy a second time, having agreed with the emperor, as they say, that he would never ask him for money in this war, but would make all the preparation for the war himself with his own funds.

[34419] 4.40. Everyone supposed that Belisarius had arranged matters concerning his wife in the way described, and had made these agreements with the emperor concerning the war, because he wished to be quit of his stay in Byzantium, and that as soon as he should get outside the city wall, he would at once snatch up his arms and contrive something noble and worthy of a man against his wife and those who had done him violence.

[34841] 4.41. But he himself, reckoning everything that had happened as of no account, and falling into deep forgetfulness and disregard of the oaths he had sworn to Photius and his other friends, followed his wife, having become extravagantly love-stricken for her—and that, too, when she was already sixty years old.

[35153] 4.42. Yet when he came to Italy, day by day affairs went contrary to him, since the things of God were plainly hostile to him.

[35281] 4.43. First, the plans devised by this general in his earlier dealings against Theudatus and Vittigis, though they seemed ill-suited to the circumstances, for the most part came to a useful end; but in the latter period he carried off the reputation of having planned the better course, as one experienced in the affairs of this war, while, faring badly in the outcomes, the result was for the most part set down to him as ill counsel.

[35718] 4.44. So true is it that human affairs are governed not by the counsels of men but by the impulse of God—which men are accustomed to call fortune, not knowing for what reason events proceed in the way

[35920] 4.45. that becomes evident to them. For to what seems irrational the name of fortune is wont to attach. But let these things be as is pleasing to each.

[36073] 5.1. Belisarius, coming into Italy a second time, departed thence most shamefully. For in a span of five years he was nowhere able to set foot on the land, as I have said in my earlier books, except where there was some fortified place; but sailing all this time he went about the coastal regions.

[36372] 5.2. Totila was raging to catch him outside a wall, yet did not find a way, since he himself and the whole Roman army were held by much dread.

[36516] 5.3. Therefore he recovered nothing of what had been lost, but even lost Rome besides, and, so to speak, everything else.

[36639] 5.4. He became in this time most avaricious of all men and a most exact pursuer of shameful gain, since he received nothing from the emperor; and he plundered, with no decency, nearly all the Italians who dwelt about Ravenna and Sicily, and any other he had it in his power to catch, pretending to exact account of their past lives.

[36973] 5.5. So, for instance, he went after Herodian too and demanded money, holding everything over the man as a threat.

[37089] 5.6. Vexed by this, Herodian renounced his allegiance to the Roman army and at once gave himself, with his followers and Spolitium, to Totila and the Goths.

[37247] 5.7. How it came about that he and John, the nephew of Vitalian, fell into discord—which most of all ruined the affairs of the Romans—I shall now make clear.

[37406] 5.8. The empress came to such a degree of hatred against Germanus, and made the hatred so very plain to all, that, although he was the emperor's cousin, no one dared to make a marriage alliance with him, and his children remained unwedded until she was released from life. His daughter Justina, too, though eighteen years old and of marriageable age, was still unwed.

[37775] 5.9. For this reason, when John, sent by Belisarius, came to Byzantium, Germanus was forced to enter into discussion with him about the marriage alliance, and that although John was much below his rank.

[37979] 5.10. And since the affair pleased them both, they resolved to bind each other by the most dreadful oaths that they would indeed accomplish the marriage with all their power, since each of them had not the least confidence in the other—the one from his consciousness that he was reaching after things above his station, the other from his want of a son-in-law.

[38341] 5.11. But she, at a loss what to do, going by every road, did not scruple to pursue each of them by every device, so as to hinder what was being done.

[38493] 5.12. And when, though she had frightened them with many threats, she could persuade neither, she openly threatened to destroy John.

[38627] 5.13. And from this John, sent again to Italy, in no way dared to join Belisarius, fearing the plot from Antonina, until Antonina should come to Byzantium.

[38784] 5.14. For one might not unreasonably suspect that the empress had charged her with John's murder; and to one who reckoned up the character of Antonina, and knew that Belisarius gave way to his wife in everything, great fear came, and entered into him.

[39037] 5.15. This, at any rate, dashed to the ground the affairs of the Romans, which already before were standing on but one leg.

[39162] 5.16. So the Gothic war went thus for Belisarius. Despairing, he begged the emperor that it might be granted him to get away from there as quickly as possible.

[39323] 5.17. And when he learned that the emperor accepted his entreaty, glad at once he departed and was gone, bidding a long farewell to the Roman army and the Italians, leaving most things subject to the enemy, and Perusia hard pressed by a most bitter siege—which, while he was still on the road, was taken by storm and came to every form of evil, as I have related before. And it befell that a reverse of fortune of the following sort came also in his household.

[39785] 5.18. The empress Theodora, urgent to complete the betrothal of Belisarius' child to her grandson, kept harassing the parents of the girl by writing often.

[39942] 5.19. They, drawing back from the alliance, put off the marriage to the time of their own presence, and when the empress summoned them to Byzantium, they pretended that they were unable to leave Italy at the present time.

[40165] 5.20. But she, longing to make her grandson master of Belisarius' wealth—for she knew the girl would be heiress, since Belisarius had no other offspring—and having no confidence at all in the judgment of Antonina, and fearing that, after the close of her own life, Antonina would not prove faithful to her house, although she had found her so kindly in matters of the greatest urgency, and would tear up what had been agreed, did an unholy deed.

[40612] 5.21. For she joined the girl in cohabitation with the youth by no lawful form. And they say that she even compelled the girl, unwilling, to lie with him in secret, and that the maiden, thus deflowered, was joined in wedlock to the boy, so that the emperor might not hinder what was being done.

[40908] 5.22. Yet when the deed was accomplished, Anastasius and the girl were held by some burning love for each other, and a span of not less than eight months was worn away for them in this manner of life.

[41110] 5.23. But when Antonina, after the empress' death, came to Byzantium, she willfully forgot what the other had lately done for her, and reckoning not at all that, if the girl were joined in cohabitation with anyone else, she would be reputed to have been first prostituted, she dishonored the offspring of Theodora as a son-in-law, and, forcing the girl most unwilling, tore her away from the man she loved.

[41518] 5.24. And by this deed she carried off among all men a great reputation for ingratitude; and when her husband came, with no trouble she persuaded him to share with her in this abomination. So thereupon the man's character was plainly proven.

[41761] 5.25. And yet, though he had sworn oaths to Photius and to certain of his friends before, and had in no way kept his oaths, he obtained pardon from all men.

[41919] 5.26. For they supposed the cause of the man's faithlessness to be not the rule of his wife, but his fear of the empress.

[42042] 5.27. But when, after the death of Theodora, as I have said, no account was made either of Photius or of any other of his intimates, but his wife appeared as his mistress and Calligonus the pander as his master, then at last all despaired of him, and mocked him, spreading the tale abroad, and reviled him as one convicted of folly. Such, then, is the plain telling of the wrongs done by Belisarius.

[42443] 5.28. As for the wrongs done in Libya by Sergius, the son of Bacchus, I have set them out sufficiently in the appropriate books—Sergius, who was most responsible for the ruin of the Romans' affairs there, having disregarded the oaths he had sworn to the Levathae upon the Gospels, and having made away with the eighty envoys without any pretext. This much only must I now add to my account: that these men did not come to Sergius with any treacherous intent, nor did Sergius have any pretext of suspicion concerning them, but, having invited the men to a banquet under solemn oath, he made away with them with no decency.

[43066] 5.29. From this it befell Solomon and the Roman army and all the Libyans to be ruined.

[43154] 5.30. For on his account, especially after Solomon had died, as I have said, neither any commander nor any soldier was willing to go into the danger of war.

[43312] 5.31. And most of all John, the son of Sisiniolus, was out of the fight through his hatred of him, until Areobindus came to Libya.

[43444] 5.32. For Sergius was soft and unwarlike, and in character and age altogether young, given beyond measure to envy and arrogance toward all men, dissolute in his way of life and puffing out his cheeks.

[43646] 5.33. But because he happened to be the betrothed of the granddaughter of Antonina, the wife of Belisarius, the empress was by no means willing to exact any punishment from him or to relieve him of his command, although she saw Libya being most continuously ruined, since she and the emperor had also let Solomon, the brother of Sergius, go unpunished for the murder of Pegasius. What this is I shall now make clear.

[44064] 5.34. When Pegasius had ransomed Solomon from the Levathae and the barbarians had returned home, Solomon, with Pegasius who had ransomed him and a few soldiers, was setting out for Carthage; and on this journey Pegasius, catching Solomon doing some wrong, said he ought to bear in mind that God had lately rescued him from the enemy.

[44399] 5.35. But Solomon, angered that he had reproached him as a captive of the spear, at once killed Pegasius, and paid the man this as a ransom-price.

[44547] 5.36. And when Solomon came to Byzantium, the emperor cleared him of the murder, on the ground that he had slain a traitor of the Roman empire.

[44692] 5.37. And he gave him a writing furnishing him security on these matters. And Solomon, thus escaping retribution, went gladly to the East to see his homeland and the family of his house.

[44880] 5.38. But the retribution from God, overtaking him on this journey, removed him from among men. So matters concerning Solomon and Pegasius went thus.

[45031] 6.1. Of what sort of human beings Justinian and Theodora were, and in what manner they tore apart the affairs of the Romans, I now proceed to tell.

[45180] 6.2. While Leo held the imperial power in Byzantium, three young farmers, Illyrians by birth—Zimarchus and Dityvistus and Justin from Bederiana—struggling unremittingly at home with the troubles that come from poverty, and wishing to be quit of these, set out to take up soldiering.

[45464] 6.3. And making their way on foot to Byzantium, they went carrying sheepskin cloaks on their shoulders, in which, when they arrived, they had nothing else but the twice-baked loaves they had put in from home; and being enrolled in the soldiers' registers, the emperor chose them for the guard of the Palace. For they were all most fair in body.

[45810] 6.4. In time afterward, when Anastasius had taken over the kingship, war arose against the nation of the Isaurians, who had taken up arms against him.

[45962] 6.5. And he sent against them an army worthy of mention, which John, surnamed the Hunchback, led. This John, for some fault, shut Justin up in a prison, and on the following day was about to make away with him, had not a vision in a dream, coming in the meantime, prevented it.

[46241] 6.6. For he said that in the dream the general met a certain figure, huge in body and in other respects greater than to be likened to a man.

[46383] 6.7. And this figure enjoined upon him to release the man whom he happened to have shut up that day; but he, on rising from sleep, made no account of the dream-vision.

[46552] 6.8. But when another night came on, it seemed to him in the dream that he heard again the words he had heard before, yet even so he did not resolve to carry out

[46715] 6.9. what was enjoined. And a third time the dream-vision, standing over him, threatened him with utter ruin if he should not do what was bidden, and added that, in time to come, when enraged, he would have great need of this very man and of his kindred.

[46971] 6.10. So at that time it befell Justin thus to survive; but as time went on this Justin advanced to great power.

[47085] 6.11. For the emperor Anastasius appointed him commander of the guards in the Palace. And when the emperor had departed from among men, Justin, by the power of his office, took over the kingship—already an old man at the grave's edge, and unlettered in all letters, and, as the saying is, illiterate, which had not happened before among the Romans.

[47435] 6.12. And whereas it was customary for the emperor to set his own letters to documents, in everything that was issued at his command, he himself neither issued anything nor was able to share understanding of what was being done.

[47665] 6.13. But the man who was allotted to sit beside him, holding the office of what is called quaestor, Proclus by name,

[47784] 6.14. himself did everything by his own independent judgment. But in order that those whose task this is might have testimony of the emperor's hand, the following device was contrived.

[47970] 6.15. Cutting into a small piece of worked wood the shape of four letters which can be read in the Latin tongue, and dipping a pen in the ink with which emperors are accustomed to write,

[48158] 6.16. they would put it into this emperor's hand. And placing the wood I mentioned upon the document, and taking hold of the emperor's hand, they would lead it round with the pen over the outline of the four letters, winding it into all the cuts of the wood, and so they would depart, bearing such letters of the emperor.

[48481] 6.17. So matters concerning Justin stood thus for the Romans. He lived with a woman named Lupicina. She, being both a slave and a barbarian, had become the concubine of the man who had previously bought her. And she, with Justin, at the close of his life, held the kingship.

[48757] 6.18. Now Justin was able to do his subjects neither any harm nor any good. For he was possessed of much simplicity, being altogether tongue-tied and most boorish.

[48922] 6.19. His nephew Justinian, while still young, directed the whole government and became the cause of disasters to the Romans such as, and so many as, no one in all the ages had ever before received by report.

[49132] 6.20. For he proceeded most easily to the unjust murder of men and the plunder of others' money, and it was nothing to him that many tens of thousands of men were made to vanish from among the living, though they had furnished him no cause.

[49374] 6.21. And he thought it right to preserve none of the established things, but wished forever to make everything new; and, to put it all in a word, this man was the greatest

[49548] 6.22. destroyer of things well established. The plague, of which I spoke in my earlier books, though it fell upon the whole earth, was escaped by no fewer men than those who chanced to be destroyed by it—either men who were not seized by the disease at all, or who survived after it befell them to be seized.

[49858] 6.23. But to escape this man, for any human being who was a Roman, befell no one at all; rather, like some other affliction sent from heaven falling upon the whole race, he left no one wholly untouched.

[50062] 6.24. For some he killed without any reason, while others he set loose to struggle with poverty, making them more wretched than the dead, since they prayed to put an end to their present lot by the most pitiable death. From some, however, he took away their lives along with their money.

[50351] 6.25. And since it was nothing to him merely to overthrow the Roman empire, he was able to win the mastery of Libya and Italy for no other reason than that, together with those already under him, he might destroy

[50565] 6.26. the people there. At any rate, not yet ten days established in power, he killed Amantius, commander of the eunuchs in the Palace, along with certain others, for no cause, bringing nothing against the man except that he had spoken some rash word against John, the high priest of the city.

[50860] 6.27. And from this he became the most terrible of all men. At once, too, he sent for Vitalian the usurper, to whom he had previously given his pledges for his safety, having shared with him in the mysteries of the Christians.

[51088] 6.28. And a little later, on the suspicion that the man had given him offense, he made away with him in the Palace, together with his intimates, on no charge, not deigning at all to keep pledges so very dreadful.

[51302] 7.1. The populace having long since been divided into two factions, as I have said in my earlier books, he attached to himself one of them, the Blues—which had in fact been the object of his zeal before—and so was able to throw everything into confusion and turmoil. And by this he brought the Roman state down upon its knees.

[51630] 7.2. Not all the Blues, however, resolved to follow this man's policy, but only those who happened to be partisans.

[51747] 7.3. And even these, as the evil now advanced, came to seem the most temperate of all men.

[51839] 7.4. For they offended less than their license allowed. Nor indeed did the partisans of the Greens remain quiet, but they too were always committing crimes to whatever extent was possible for them, though they were continually punished, each on his own.

[52094] 7.5. And this led them ever more by far to grow bolder. For when men are wronged, they are wont to turn to recklessness.

[52216] 7.6. At that time, then, while he fanned the Blues and openly incited them, the whole Roman empire was shaken to its foundations, as though an earthquake or a flood had fallen upon it, or as though every city had been taken by the enemy.

[52455] 7.7. For everything everywhere was thrown into confusion, and nothing thereafter remained on its own footing, but the laws and the order of the state, with confusion supervening, turned to the very opposite.

[52664] 7.8. And first the partisans changed the fashion of their hair to a novel mode. For they cut it in no way like the other Romans.

[52794] 7.9. For they did not touch the moustache or the beard at all, but wished to let these grow long, like the Persians, forever.

[52921] 7.10. But the hair on their heads they cut away in front as far as the temples, and let the back hang down at very great length, with no reason, like the Massagetae. For this reason they even called this style the Hunnic.

[53144] 7.11. Next, in the matter of dress, they all thought it proper to be richly bordered, clothing themselves in attire more pretentious than each man's rank deserved.

[53309] 7.12. For they could acquire such things from improper sources. And the part of the tunic about the hands was drawn in close at the wrist, but from there to either shoulder spread out to an unspeakable breadth.

[53521] 7.13. And as often as their arm was waved while they shouted in the theaters and hippodromes, or, as was their custom, cheered, this part rose up high of itself, giving the senseless the impression that their bodies were so fair and stout that they had to be covered by such garments—not perceiving that, by the thinness and emptiness of their dress, the wasted state of their bodies was all the more exposed.

[53932] 7.14. Their cloaks, too, and their trousers, and most of their footwear, were assigned to the name and fashion of the Huns.

[54057] 7.15. They carried weapons by night at first, nearly all of them openly, but by day they hid two-edged daggers along the thigh under their cloaks; and gathering into bands, when it grew dark, they would rob the more respectable men, both in the open marketplace and in the narrow streets, taking from those who fell in their way their cloaks and belts and golden brooches and anything else they had in hand.

[54466] 7.16. Some, along with the robbery, they thought fit even to kill, so that the victims might report to no one what had befallen them.

[54601] 7.17. At these things all men, and especially those of the Blues who were not partisans, were most distressed, since they too did not remain unscathed.

[54754] 7.18. And from this most men thereafter used belts and brooches and cloaks of bronze, and much below their rank, so as not to be destroyed for their love of fine things; and before the sun had set they would withdraw into their houses and hide.

[55000] 7.19. As the evil dragged on and no attention was paid to the offenders by the authority set over the populace, the audacity of the men rose ever to a great height.

[55166] 7.20. For wrongdoing, when it is granted impunity, is by nature carried to infinity, since even crimes that are punished are wont not to be wholly cut off.

[55323] 7.21. For by nature most men turn readily to wrongdoing.

[55381] 7.22. So matters with the Blues went thus. As for the opposing partisans, some of them inclined to the others' faction, out of desire to share in the crimes and not pay the penalty; others, keeping to flight, hid themselves in other regions; and many, caught on the spot, were destroyed by their adversaries or punished by the authority.

[55720] 7.23. And many other young men flowed together into this society, who had never before been zealous about such things, but were drawn there by the license of power and outrage.

[55898] 7.24. For there is no abomination named by men that was not committed in this time and yet remained outside punishment.

[56019] 7.25. First, then, they destroyed their opponents; but as they went on, they killed even those who had given them no offense.

[56146] 7.26. And many, bribing them with money, would point out their own enemies, whom these men at once made away with, fastening on them the name of Greens,

[56300] 7.27. though they were utterly unknown to them. And these things were no longer done in the dark or in secret, but at all hours of the day and in every quarter of the city, with men of the highest distinction sometimes looking on at what was done before their eyes.

[56567] 7.28. For they had no need to conceal their crimes, since no fear of punishment hung over them, but there was even attached to it a claim of distinction, as they made a display of strength and manliness, that with a single blow they killed some unarmed man who fell in their way; and no one had any hope of living on, amid the precariousness of life.

[56919] 7.29. For all suspected, in their terror, that death was hanging over them, and no place seemed secure to anyone, nor any occasion safe, since men were destroyed for no reason even in the most venerable sanctuaries and at festivals, and no trust was any longer left toward friends or kinsmen. For many died even by the plotting of those closest to them.

[57274] 7.30. Yet no inquiry was made into what had been done. But the disasters fell unexpected upon all, and no one defended the fallen.

[57406] 7.31. And no force of any law or contract was left in the security of order, but everything, turned to violence, was thrown into confusion, and the state was most like a tyranny—not, however, an established one, but one changing every day and forever beginning afresh.

[57676] 7.32. And the minds of the magistrates seemed like men thunderstruck, their spirit enslaved by fear of a single man; and the judges, in giving their decisions on disputed matters, cast their votes not as seemed to them just and lawful, but according as each of the litigants stood, hostile or friendly, with the partisans. For a judge who disregarded their dictation incurred death as his penalty.

[58075] 7.33. And many creditors were forced, with much violence, to give back their bonds to their debtors without having received any of the debt; many, against their will, set their slaves free.

[58266] 7.34. And they say that some women too were compelled to many things they did not wish by their own slaves.

[58375] 7.35. And by now even the sons of men of distinction, mingling with these young men, compelled their fathers to do many things against their will, and to hand over their money to them.

[58561] 7.36. And many unwilling boys were compelled to come to an unholy bed with the partisans, their fathers not unaware.

[58679] 7.37. And to women living with husbands the same thing befell. And it is said that one woman, decked in much finery, was sailing with her husband to a suburb on the opposite shore, and when the partisans fell in with them on this passage, and snatched her with threats from her husband and put her into their own boat, she got into the boat with the young men, charging her husband secretly to take courage and to fear nothing base on her account;

[59128] 7.38. for nothing would befall her body to be outraged. And while her husband still looked upon her with great grief, she let her body down into the sea and at once vanished from among the living.

[59326] 7.39. Such, then, were the deeds dared by these partisans at that time in Byzantium. Yet these grieved the victims less than the wrongs done to the state by Justinian, since for those who have suffered the worst at the hands of criminals, the greatest part of the grief that has entered through disorder is taken away by the constant expectation of retribution from the laws and the authority.

[59721] 7.40. For when men are of good hope toward the future, they bear their present lot more easily and with less labor; but when they are oppressed by the very authority set over the state, they are all the more pained, as is natural, by their misfortunes, and turn forever to despair at the hopelessness of retribution.

[60039] 7.41. He did wrong not only because he was in no way willing to pretend to defend the oppressed, but because he by no means disdained to set himself up openly as protector of the partisans.

[60230] 7.42. For he gave great sums of money to these young men, and kept many of them about him, and thought fit to call some of them to offices and other dignities.

[60391] 8.1. These things, then, were being done in Byzantium and in every city. For, like some other disease, the evil, beginning here, fell upon every part of the Roman empire.

[60563] 8.2. But the emperor cared not at all for what was being done, since the man had no perception of it, although he was always an eyewitness of what was done in the hippodromes.

[60740] 8.3. For he was prodigiously stupid, most like a sluggish ass, ready to follow whoever drew the bridle, with his ears constantly twitching.

[60881] 8.4. Justinian did these things and threw everything else into turmoil. As soon as he laid hold of his uncle's rule, he was eager to squander the public moneys with no order, since he had become master of them.

[61093] 8.5. For he gave very much to the Huns who from time to time fell in with him, on the state's account; and from this it befell that the land of the Romans

[61249] 8.6. lay open to frequent inroads. For these barbarians, once they had tasted the wealth of the Romans, could no longer keep away from the road that led there.

[61410] 8.7. And he thought fit to throw away much money also on certain buildings against the sea, forcing back the perpetual surge of the waves.

[61550] 8.8. For from the shore he advanced with heaps of stones, contending against the inflowings from the deep, and, as though by the power of his wealth, vying with the might of the sea.

[61734] 8.9. And he gathered to himself the private property of individual Romans from all the earth, bringing against some a charge of a crime that had not been committed, and in the case of others pretending that they had made him a gift of their intention.

[61987] 8.10. And many, convicted of murders and other such crimes, then escaped paying the penalty for what they had done wrong by surrendering all their money to him.

[62149] 8.11. Others, disputing improperly with their neighbors over some pieces of land—when they could in no way win judgment against their opponents, since the law stood against them—would make a present of these disputed properties to him and be quit of the case, themselves gaining, by a costless favor, the advantage of being known to this man, and being able, in a most lawless manner, to obtain a condemnation of their adversaries.

[62582] 8.12. I think it not out of place to describe the appearance of this man. In body, then, he was neither tall nor unduly short, but of middle height, not lean, however, but somewhat fleshy; in face he was round and not ill-favored, for he kept his color even after fasting two days.

[62865] 8.13. But to indicate his whole appearance in sum, he most closely resembled Domitian, the son of Vespasian, whose wickedness the Romans had to such a degree to endure that, even after cutting him to pieces, they did not feel they had vented their anger against him in full, but a decree of the senate was passed that the name of this emperor should not appear in writings, and that no image of him whatever should be preserved.

[63295] 8.14. At any rate this name, everywhere on the writings of Rome and wherever else it happened to be inscribed, may be seen erased alone among the others, and no image of him appears anywhere in the Roman empire, except a single bronze one, for the following reason.

[63562] 8.15. Domitian had a wife, free-born and otherwise respectable, who herself never did harm to any human being, nor did any of her husband's deeds please her.

[63721] 8.16. Therefore, since she was greatly loved, the senate then sent for her and bade her ask whatever she wished.

[63835] 8.17. And she made this one request: to receive Domitian's body and bury it, and to set up one bronze image to him, wherever she might wish.

[63977] 8.18. And the senate granted this. But the woman, wishing to leave to later time

[64059] 8.19. memorials of the inhumanity of those who had cut her husband to pieces, devised this. Gathering up Domitian's flesh, and fitting it exactly together and joining it to itself, she sewed up the whole body, and showing it to the sculptors, bade them reproduce in a bronze image this disaster.

[64356] 8.20. So the craftsmen at once made the image. And the woman, taking it, set it up on the right of the ascent leading to the Capitol, as one goes there from the marketplace, displaying the appearance of Domitian and his fate down to this time.

[64601] 8.21. One might guess that the whole body of Justinian, and his very face and all the features of his countenance, were plainly to be seen in this image.

[64756] 8.22. Such, then, was his appearance; his character I could not describe with exactness. For this man was both a doer of evil and easily led into it, the kind whom they call a malicious fool—neither speaking the truth himself to those he met with, but with a treacherous mind ever saying and doing everything, and lying open with no trouble to those who wished to deceive him.

[65134] 8.23. And a certain unusual mixture had grown up in him, compounded of folly and wickedness. And perhaps this was what one of the philosophers of the Peripatetic school declared in former times, that even the most opposite qualities happen to exist in human nature, as in the mixing of colors.

[65429] 8.24. I write, however, of what it has not been possible for me to attain in full. This emperor, then, was dissembling, treacherous, false, dark in his anger, double, a man clever, most accomplished at feigning his opinion, and shedding tears not from any pleasure or grief, but contriving them on occasion according to the need of the moment, lying forever—yet not at random, but making both writings and the most dreadful oaths upon his agreements, and that toward his own subjects.

[65915] 8.25. But he would at once withdraw from what he had agreed and sworn, like the worst of slaves, who are led by fear of the tortures hanging over them to confess under oath.

[66090] 8.26. An unstable friend, an implacable enemy, a burning lover of murders and of money, quarrelsome and most given to innovation, easily led to evil, but coming to good by no counsel, quick to conceive base things and to carry them out, but thinking the very hearing of good things distasteful.

[66386] 8.27. How could anyone be able to attain in words the character of Justinian? These and many evils still greater he plainly possessed beyond what is human; rather, Nature seemed to have taken away all wickedness from the rest of men and laid it up in the soul of this man.

[66660] 8.28. And besides all the rest, he was exceedingly ready to believe slanders, and quick to inflict punishments. For he never judged after investigation, but on hearing the slanderer he resolved at once to bring out his verdict.

[66889] 8.29. And he wrote, with no hesitation, letters ordering the captures of districts and the burning of cities and the enslavement of whole nations for no cause.

[67050] 8.30. So that, if one wished to weigh up from the beginning all the things that have befallen the Romans and to set them against these, it seems to me he would find that more murders of men came about through this one man than have chanced to occur in all the rest of time.

[67325] 8.31. As for other men's money, he was most untiring in seizing it without scruple; for he did not even think it worth while to put forward any pretext as a screen of justice for trespassing on what did not belong to him. But when it had become his own, he was most ready, out of a senseless love of display, to scorn it and to throw it away to the barbarians for no reason.

[67701] 8.32. And, to put it all in a word, he himself had no money, nor did he allow anyone else of all men to have any, as though he were overcome not by avarice but by envy of those who possessed it.

[67897] 8.33. Easily, then, banishing wealth from the land of the Romans, he became the artificer of poverty for all.

[68284] 9.2. There was a certain Acacius in Byzantium, keeper of the beasts in the wild-beast hunts of the Green faction, whom they call a bear-keeper.

[68429] 9.3. This man died of a disease while Anastasius held the imperial power, leaving three girls—Comito and Theodora and Anastasia—of whom the eldest happened to be not yet seven years old.

[68617] 9.4. The woman, his widow, went over to another man, who, together with her, was to take charge thenceforth both of the household and of this office.

[68768] 9.5. But the dance-master of the Greens, Asterius by name, persuaded by money from another, removed these from this honor and, with no trouble, set in their place the man who had given him the money. For dance-masters were allowed to manage such things at their discretion, as they wished.

[69059] 9.6. But when the woman saw the whole populace gathered in the wild-beast hunt, she set garlands on the heads and in both hands of the girls and seated them as suppliants.

[69232] 9.7. But the Greens resolved by no means to receive the supplication; the Blues, however, established them in this same honor, since their beast-keeper too had lately died.

[69406] 9.8. When these children came of age, the mother at once set them upon the stage there, since they were comely in appearance—not all at the same time, however, but as each seemed to her to be ripe for this work.

[69619] 9.9. [69942] 9.10. For a while, then, Theodora, being still unripe, was in no way able to lie with a man, nor to have intercourse as a woman; but she carried on a kind of masculine commerce of the basest sort with the wretches—and these slaves—who, following their masters into the theater, wrought this ruin as an incidental of the opportunity they had; and she spent much time also in a brothel at this unnatural traffic of the body.

[70366] 9.11. But as soon as she came of age and was now ripe, she let herself down among the women on the stage, and at once became a courtesan, of the kind the men of old called common.

[70547] 9.12. For she was neither a flute-player nor a harpist, nor had she been trained for the dance, but she gave over her bloom to all who came her way, working with her whole body for hire.

[70735] 9.13. Then she consorted with the mimes in all their work on the stage and shared in their pursuits, serving them in some buffoonish jests. For she was exceedingly witty and a mocker, and at once became notable for her work.

[70961] 9.14. For the woman had no share of shame, nor did anyone ever see her abashed, but she went into shameless services with no hesitation; and she was the kind of woman who, when struck and slapped on the cheeks, would make merry and laugh aloud, and who, stripping off both before and behind, would show naked to those she met what it is proper for men to keep unseen and hidden.

[71341] 9.15. With her lovers she trifled, playing the coquette, and by ever-new contrivances of intercourse she was able to win over forever the souls of the dissolute; for she did not think it proper to wait to be solicited by any of those she met, but, on the contrary, herself jesting and making lewd play with her hips, she would solicit all who fell in her way, especially boys who were still beardless.

[71744] 9.16. For no one was ever so given over to every kind of pleasure. For often she would come to a public banquet with ten young men, or more than these, in the full vigor of bodily strength and making lechery their business, and would lie with all her fellow-diners the whole night through; and when they had all given out at this work, she would go to their servants, thirty in number, as it might be, and couple with each of them, and yet not even so did she reach satiety of this lewdness.

[72237] 9.17. And once, coming to the house of one of the men of distinction, in the midst of the drinking, while all the fellow-revelers, they say, were watching her, she went up onto the projecting part of the couch, and at the foot of it, drawing up her garments in no decent fashion, she did not disdain to display her wantonness there.

[72571] 9.18. And though she worked from three openings, she would reproach Nature, complaining that the openings of her breasts had not also been bored wider than they are, so that she might be able to contrive yet another mode of intercourse there.

[72815] 9.19. And she conceived often, but, contriving by almost every device, she was at once able to procure abortion.

[72929] 9.20. And often even in the theater, under the eyes of the whole populace, she stripped and became naked in the midst, having only a girdle about her private parts and groin—not, indeed, because she was ashamed to show even these to the people, but because no one is permitted to come there utterly naked without having a girdle about the groin. In this guise, then,

[73297] 9.21. she would lie back upon her back, stretched out on the floor. And certain hirelings, whose task this was, would scatter grains of barley over her private parts, which the geese, kept ready for this purpose, would pick up one by one with their bills from there and eat.

[73573] 9.22. And she, far from rising in a blush, even seemed to take pride in this performance. For she was not only shameless, but most of all a maker of shamelessness in others.

[73748] 9.23. And often, too, stripping with the mimes, she would stand in the midst on the stage, bending and thrusting back her hips both to those who had had experience of her and to those who had not yet lain with her, parading the exercises of the palaestra in which she was practiced.

[74032] 9.24. And so wantonly did she outrage her own body that she seemed to have her shame not in the natural place, as with other women, but in her face.

[74182] 9.25. Those who consorted with her were plainly known at once from that fact, that they did not make their unions according to the law of nature; and those of the more respectable who met her in the marketplace turned aside and hurriedly withdrew, lest, by touching any of the woman's garments, they should be thought to share in this pollution.

[74529] 9.26. For to those who saw her, especially at the day's beginning, she was an ill-omened sight. Toward her fellow-actresses she was wont to be ever most savagely venomous; for she was held by much malice.

[74735] 9.27. Later she followed Hecebolus, a Tyrian who had taken over the rule of Pentapolis, to serve him in the basest things; but having given the man some offense, she was driven thence as quickly as possible. As a result she fell into want of necessities, which thereafter she procured by working her customary transgression upon her body.

[75075] 9.28. First, then, she came to Alexandria. Then, going round the whole East, she returned to Byzantium, plying her trade in each city—a trade which, I think, God could never be propitious to a man for naming—as though the demon would not endure that any place should be ignorant of Theodora's wantonness.

[75381] 9.29. In such a manner, then, it befell this woman to be born and reared, and to become notorious among many of the common people and among all men.

[75531] 9.30. But when she came again to Byzantium, Justinian fell in love with her with an extraordinary passion, and at first consorted with her as a mistress, although he raised her to the rank of patrician.

[75735] 9.31. Thereupon Theodora was at once able to surround herself with extraordinary power and considerable wealth. For it seemed to the man the sweetest of all things—as is wont to happen with those who love extravagantly—to grant his mistress every favor and all his money.

[76008] 9.32. And the state became fuel for this love. With her, then, he ruined the populace much more than before, not here only, but throughout the whole Roman empire.

[76172] 9.33. For both, being of old of the Blue faction, put the affairs of the state in great measure into the hands of these partisans.

[76304] 9.34. But long afterward the greater part of the evil abated, in the following manner.

[76392] 9.35. It happened that Justinian fell ill for many days, and in this illness he came into such danger that it was even said he had died. The partisans, meanwhile, were committing the crimes that have been described, and killed a certain Hypatius, a man of no obscurity, in broad day in the sanctuary of Sophia.

[76704] 9.36. When the deed had been done, the disturbance of the affair reached the emperor; and each of those about him, since he had got hold of Justinian's absence, was eager to magnify the monstrousness of what had been done, reciting from the beginning everything that had chanced to happen.

[76995] 9.37. Then the emperor charged the prefect of the city to exact retribution for all that had been done. This man's name was Theodotus, whom they called by the surname Pumpkin.

[77172] 9.38. And he, investigating everything, was able to seize and execute by law many of the criminals; but many also, escaping notice,

[77305] 9.39. were saved. For in the meantime it was fated that they should perish along with the affairs of the Romans. But the emperor, unexpectedly saved by a sudden recovery, at once set about killing Theodotus too, as a poisoner and sorcerer.

[77546] 9.40. And since he had no pretext to use for destroying the man, he most bitterly tortured certain of his friends and forced them to utter words against him that were anything but sound.

[77734] 9.41. And while all stood aloof and in silence lamented the plot against Theodotus, Proclus alone, who held the office of what is called quaestor, declared the man clear of the charge and in no way deserving of death.

[77953] 9.42. Therefore Theodotus was conveyed to Jerusalem by the emperor's decision. But learning that certain men had come there who would destroy him, he was in hiding in the sanctuary the whole time, and living thus he died.

[78176] 9.43. So matters concerning Theodotus went thus. The partisans, from then on, became the most temperate of all men.

[78293] 9.44. For they no longer endured to commit such crimes, although it was open to them to indulge their lawlessness of life more fearlessly.

[78433] 9.45. And here is the proof: when a few of them later displayed the like audacity, no retribution came upon them.

[78548] 9.46. For those who always had the power of punishing granted to these men who had done such dreadful deeds the license of escaping notice, by this very indulgence leading them on to trample upon the laws.

[78755] 9.47. So long, then, as the empress Lupicina was still alive, Justinian had no means of making Theodora his wedded wife. For in this one matter alone she opposed him, though she opposed him in nothing else.

[78963] 9.48. For the woman happened to be very far from wickedness, but she was altogether boorish and barbarian by birth, as I have said.

[79096] 9.49. Nor was she in any way able to lay hold of virtue, but remained utterly without experience of the affairs of state—she who did not even enter the Palace under her own name, as being a ridiculous one, but was called Euphemia. But in the course of time it befell that the empress died.

[79387] 9.50. And Justin, having become silly and altogether decrepit, was an object of laughter to his subjects, and all, treating him with much contempt because he understood nothing of what was being done, looked down upon him; but Justinian they courted with much fear. For, ever stirring and confounding things, he kept everything in continual uproar.

[79737] 9.51. Then indeed Justinian set about making the betrothal to Theodora. But since it was impossible for a man who had attained senatorial rank to be united with a courtesan—forbidden of old by the most ancient laws—he compelled the emperor to dissolve the laws by another law, and thenceforth lived with Theodora as his wedded wife, and made the betrothal to courtesans open to all other men; and, playing the tyrant, he at once usurped the honor of the sovereign power, cloaking the violence of the act with a counterfeit pretext.

[80270] 9.52. For they proclaimed him emperor, together with his uncle, all the Romans of any standing being led to this vote by an excess of fear.

[80411] 9.53. So Justinian and Theodora took over the kingship three days before the feast of Easter, at a time when it is not even permitted to greet any of one's friends or to wish him peace.

[80598] 9.54. And not many days later Justin died of a disease, having survived his accession by nine years, and Justinian, with Theodora, held the kingship alone.

[80755] 10.1. Thus, then, Theodora, having been born and reared and educated in the manner described, came to the rank of empress, hindered by nothing.

[80900] 10.2. For no thought of disgrace came to the man who married her, although it was open to him, choosing from the whole Roman empire, to make his wedded wife the woman who, of all women, was best born, and who had partaken of a secluded upbringing, and was not unpracticed in modesty, and was united with chastity, and besides her beauty was extraordinary, and was both a virgin and, as the saying goes, firm-breasted.

[81319] 10.3. But he did not disdain to make the common pollution of all mankind his own private possession, feeling no shame at any of the things already disclosed, and consorting with a woman invested with great pollutions besides—the many child-murders of willful abortions. And I think there is nothing else at all left for me to mention concerning this man's character.

[81687] 10.4. For all the passions of his soul this marriage would be sufficient witness to indicate fully, becoming interpreter and witness and recorder of his character.

[81852] 10.5. For whoever, disregarding shame for what he has done, does not scruple to appear loathsome to those he meets, for him no path of lawlessness is untrodden, but, ever holding shamelessness before his brow, he advances most easily and with no trouble to the foulest of deeds.

[82132] 10.6. Yet not one man of the senate, seeing the state fasten this disgrace upon itself, resolved to take it ill or to refuse the act, though all were about to do her reverence as a god.

[82319] 10.7. Nor did any priest show himself indignant, and that when they were going to address her as their mistress.

[82433] 10.8. And the populace that had formerly been her spectators thought it right, with no shame, to be her slaves and to be so called, with upturned hands.

[82587] 10.9. Nor was any soldier angered if he was going to undergo the dangers of the camp on behalf of Theodora's interests; nor did any other of all men oppose her, but all, I suppose, bowing to the fact that this had been so granted, gave way to the completion of this pollution—as though Fortune had made a display of her power, who, governing all human affairs, cares not at all either that what is done should be reasonable, or that it should seem to men to have come about according to reason.

[83083] 10.10. At any rate she suddenly raises some man to a great height by an irrational power, against whom many obstacles seem to be woven, but nothing in any single act stands in his way; rather, he is borne by every device wherever it has been ordained for him, all readily giving way and withdrawing before Fortune as she advances. But let these things be and be spoken of in whatever way is pleasing to God.

[83662] 10.12. To tell most of what she did in her stage life all eternity would not suffice; but having selected a few things in my earlier account, I shall be able to make plain the woman's whole character to those who come after.

[84014] 10.14. [84786] 10.17. And he seemed like one secretly vexed and grumbling, yet unable openly to oppose his wife; and often, changing their roles, they would take the opposite course to each other under the appearance of holding power.

[85007] 10.18. For he would think fit to punish the Blues as wrongdoers, while she, angry in word, would take it ill that she had, against her will, been worsted by her husband.

[86112] 10.22. And she would at once work irreparable deeds against them, while he, pretending to be unaware of what was being done, would gladly take their whole property by a shameless seizure.

[86301] 10.23. And by these devices, while agreeing among themselves but in the open feigning faction, they were able both to divide their subjects and to establish their tyranny most firmly.

[86486] 11.1. When, then, Justinian took over the kingship, he was at once able to throw everything into confusion. For he introduced into the state things that had previously been forbidden by law, and abolished all that existed and was customary, as though he had received the imperial form for this very purpose, that he might change everything into another form.

[86846] 11.2. For some offices he abolished, and set up others that did not exist over affairs; and the laws and the registers of the soldiers he treated in the same way, not yielding to justice nor led to it by expediency, but only that everything might be new and named after himself. And if he was unable to change anything at the moment, yet he set his own name upon it.

[87214] 11.3. No satiety of plundering money or of murdering men ever seized him; rather, having plundered very many houses of prosperous men, he sought out others, giving away at once to some of the barbarians, or to senseless buildings, the money that was the spoil of the former.

[87490] 11.4. And having destroyed perhaps countless men for no reason, he at once set about plotting against many others.

[87606] 11.5. So, while the Romans were at peace with all mankind, not knowing what to do in his lust for murders, he set all the barbarians against one another, and summoning the leaders of the Huns for no cause, he gave them great sums of money in his absurd love of display, making these, as he said, pledges of friendship—which he was said to have done also in the time of Justin's reign.

[87992] 11.6. And they, having received money, would send some of their fellow-chiefs with their own followers, bidding them overrun the emperor's land in their raids, so that they too might be able to sell peace to him who wished to buy it for no reason.

[88241] 11.7. And these at once enslaved the Roman empire, and were no less in the pay of the emperor; and others, after these, at once set about plundering the wretched Romans, and after the plunder received as the prize of their raid the emperor's bounty.

[88492] 11.8. And thus, in a word, all of them, letting slip no opportunity, in rotation drove off and carried away everything.

[88613] 11.9. For these barbarians have many divisions of chiefs, and the war went round and round, taking its cause from senseless love of display, but finding no end at all, but circling back upon itself

[88812] 11.10. through all time. Therefore no spot, whether mountain or cave or anything else of the Roman land, remained unravaged in this time, and it befell many regions to be captured more than five times.

[89015] 11.11. And these things, and all that befell at the hands of the Medes and Saracens and Sclaveni and Antae and the other barbarians, I have related in my earlier books; but, as I said at the beginning of this account, it was necessary for me here to state the cause of what befell.

[89298] 11.12. And to Chosroes he gave a great quantity of centenaria for peace, but, taking it upon himself with no reason, he became most responsible for the truce being broken, being zealous and eager to win over to himself Alamundarus and the Huns who were in alliance with the Persians—a thing which, in my books concerning them, I think I did not state openly.

[89658] 11.13. And while he stirred up and fanned for the Romans the evils of factions and wars, with this one purpose—that the earth might be filled with human blood by many devices and that more money might be plundered—he contrived much other slaughter of his subjects in the following way.

[89945] 11.14. There are many rejected beliefs of Christians in the whole Roman empire, which they are accustomed to call heresies—of the Montanists and Sabbatians and all the others by which the opinions of men are wont to be led astray.

[90177] 11.15. All these he ordered to change their old belief, threatening the disobedient with many things, and especially that they should no longer be able to pass on their property to their children or kinsmen.

[90386] 11.16. Now the sanctuaries of these so-called heretics, and especially of those who practiced the belief of Arius, had a wealth beyond report.

[90530] 11.17. For neither the whole senate nor any other very great part of the Roman empire could be compared, in respect of property, to these sanctuaries.

[90682] 11.18. For they had treasures of gold and silver, and ornaments composed of precious stones, beyond telling and beyond number, and houses and villages in great abundance, and much land everywhere on the earth, and every other kind of wealth that exists and is named among all men, since none of those who had ever reigned before had troubled them.

[91031] 11.19. And many men, even of the orthodox belief, ever had from this source, under the pretext of these pursuits, the resources of their livelihood.

[91181] 11.20. So the emperor Justinian, first confiscating the property of these sanctuaries, suddenly took away all their money. From this it befell most men to be cut off thereafter from their livelihood.

[91382] 11.21. And many men at once, going about everywhere, compelled those they fell in with to change from the ancestral belief.

[91507] 11.22. And since this seemed unholy to country folk, they all resolved to resist those who gave this command.

[91618] 11.23. Many, then, were destroyed by the soldiers, and many also made away with themselves, supposing in their foolishness that they were thus most piously serving God; and the greater part of them fled, abandoning the land of their fathers. But the Montanists, who dwelt in Phrygia, shutting themselves up in their own sanctuaries, at once set fire to these churches and perished together with them for no reason; and from this the whole Roman empire was filled with murder and flight.

[92106] 11.24. And when a law of this kind was at once passed also concerning the Samaritans, an indiscriminate disturbance seized Palestine.

[92241] 11.25. Now those who dwelt in my own Caesarea and in the other cities, thinking it a slight thing to suffer hardship for a foolish doctrine, exchanged their existing name for the name of Christians, and by this pretense were able to shake off the danger from the law.

[92510] 11.26. And those of them who had any share of reason and decency by no means disdained to be faithful to this belief; but most, as though indignant that, not willingly but compelled by the law, they had changed their ancestral doctrine, at once inclined to the Manichaeans and to the so-called Polytheists.

[92818] 11.27. And the farmers, all gathering in a body, resolved to take up arms against the emperor, setting up as their king one of the brigands, Julian by name, the son of Sabarus.

[92996] 11.28. And for some time, coming to blows with the soldiers, they held out; but afterward, defeated in battle, they were destroyed together with their leader.

[93156] 11.29. And it is said that a hundred thousand men perished in this struggle, and the region that was the best of all the earth was thereby made empty of farmers.

[93319] 11.30. And to the owners of the lands, who were Christians, this matter came to a great evil. For it became necessary for them, getting nothing thence, to pay forever to the emperor the annual tax, a heavy one, since no mercy was shown in the enforcement of this matter.

[93591] 11.31. From there he led his persecution against the so-called Hellenes, torturing their bodies and plundering their money.

[93716] 11.32. But even those of them who resolved nominally to take the name of Christians, in word warding off their present troubles, these too, not long after, were for the most part caught at libations and sacrifices and other unholy works.

[93955] 11.33. For what was done concerning the Christians will be told in my later books.

[94039] 11.34. And after this he forbade pederasty by law—not investigating what came after the law, but those who had long since been seized by this disease.

[94191] 11.35. And the proceeding against them was conducted with no decency, since the retribution against them was carried out even without an accuser, and the word of one man or boy—and that a slave, as it might be, and compelled unwillingly to testify against his master—

[94460] 11.36. was held to be an exact proof. Those thus convicted they paraded with their genitals cut off. The evil, however, was not at first directed against all, but only against those who seemed either to be Greens, or to be invested with great wealth, or who happened to have given some other offense to the tyrants.

[94777] 11.37. And further, they were harsh toward the astronomers. Therefore the authority appointed over thieves tortured them for no other cause, and, scourging them many times across the back, paraded them through the whole city mounted on camels—old men, and otherwise respectable—having nothing else to charge against them except that they wished to be wise concerning the stars

[95155] 11.38. in such a place. So a great throng of men continually fled, not only to the barbarians, but also to the Romans settled far off, and one could see in every region and city most of the inhabitants strangers.

[95369] 11.39. For, in order to escape notice, each readily exchanged the land of his fathers for a foreign one,

[95475] 11.40. as though their country had been captured by enemies. So the wealth of those reputed prosperous, in Byzantium and in every city, next after those of the senate, Justinian and Theodora plundered and held in the ways described.

[95709] 11.41. But in what manner they were able to take away the whole of the money of those in the senate too, I shall now make clear.

[95839] 12.1. There was a certain man in Byzantium, Zeno by name, grandson of that Anthemius who had formerly held the kingship in the West. This man they deliberately appointed governor over Egypt and sent out.

[96044] 12.2. But he, filling his ship with the most precious of his goods, made his preparations for the voyage; for he had an uncountable weight of silver, and gold plate adorned with pearls and emeralds and other such precious stones. But certain persons, persuading some of those reputed his most faithful servants, carried off the money from there as quickly as possible, and putting fire into the hold of the ship, bade them report to Zeno that the flame had broken out in the ship of itself and that the money had perished.

[96568] 12.3. And in the course of time it befell Zeno suddenly to die, and they at once became masters of his property as his heirs.

[96695] 12.4. For they produced a certain will, which was noised abroad as not having been composed by him.

[96796] 12.5. And of Tatian and Demosthenes and Hilara they made themselves heirs in the same manner—men who, in other respects and in rank, were first in the senate of the Romans. And of some they got the property by fabricating not wills but letters.

[97042] 12.6. For in this way they became heirs of Dionysius, who dwelt in Lebanon, and of John, the son of Basil, who had been the most distinguished of all the people of Edessa, but had been given by force by Belisarius to the Persians as a hostage, as I have said in my earlier books.

[97323] 12.7. For this John, Chosroes would no longer release, charging the Romans with having disregarded all the agreements on which he had been given by Belisarius; he thought fit, however, to ransom him, as one who had become a captive of the spear.

[97570] 12.8. And the man's grandmother (for she still chanced to be living), offering as ransom not less than two thousand pounds of silver, was likely to buy back her grandson.

[97742] 12.9. But when this ransom came to Daras, the emperor learned of it and would not allow the transaction to be made, lest the wealth of the Romans, he said,

[97899] 12.10. should be conveyed to the barbarians. And not long after it befell John to fall ill and vanish from among men; and the man who governed the city, fabricating a certain letter, said that John, as his friend, had written to him not long before that it was his wish that his property should go to the emperor.

[98214] 12.11. The names of all the others, of whom they became heirs of their own accord, I could not recount.

[98319] 12.12. But until the so-called Nika revolt occurred, they thought fit to gather in the properties of the prosperous one by one; but when this came about, as I have said in my earlier books, then indeed, confiscating almost all at once, so to speak, the properties of all the members of the senate, they disposed as they wished of all the furniture and of whatever lands were finest, and, selecting those subject to a bitter and most heavy tax, they gave them back, under the pretense of humanity, to their former owners.

[98841] 12.13. Therefore, choked by the tax-collectors and worn down by the ever-flowing interest of their debts, they passed their lives unwillingly in a lingering death.

[99006] 12.14. For these reasons, to me and to most of us these never seemed to be human beings, but certain avenging demons, and, as the poets say, a pair of destroyers of mortals, who, taking common counsel how they might be able most easily and quickly to destroy all the races and works of men, put on human form and, having become human demons, in this manner shook the whole world.

[99387] 12.15. One might find proof of this in many things, and especially in the power of what was done. For the deeds of demons are distinguished from those of men by a great difference.

[99569] 12.16. Many men, to be sure, have come into being in all time, terrible in the extreme through chance or nature, who have wrecked cities or lands or some other such thing by their own doing; but the destruction of all men and the disasters of the whole inhabited world no one was able to accomplish except these men, whose purpose Fortune also served, working together with their will at the destruction of men.

[99982] 12.17. For by earthquakes and plagues and inundations of river waters very much was destroyed in this time, as I shall presently relate. So it was by no human, but by some other power, that they wrought these dreadful things.

[100209] 12.18. And they say that his mother too said to certain of her intimates that he was not the son of Sabbatius her husband, nor of any human being.

[100357] 12.19. For when she was about to conceive him, a demon visited her, unseen, but giving her a perception that he was present with her, as a man consorting with a woman, and then vanished as in a dream.

[100559] 12.20. And some of those who were with him at a late hour of the nights, and conversed with him in the Palace—those whose souls were pure—thought they beheld a strange demonic apparition in his place.

[100761] 12.21. For one of them said that he suddenly rose from the royal throne and walked about there—for he was never accustomed to sit long—and that Justinian's head at that moment vanished, while the rest of his body seemed to make these long traverses; and that he himself, his eyes being anything but sound about the sight, stood for a long while distressed and at a loss.

[101133] 12.22. But later, when the head returned to the body, he thought he made up, beyond his expectation, what had until then been wanting.

[101269] 12.23. And another said that he stood beside him as he sat, and that of a sudden he saw his face become like shapeless flesh; for it bore neither eyebrows nor eyes in their proper place, nor any other distinguishing mark at all; but in time he saw the form of the face return to him. These things I write not as having seen them myself, but as having heard them from those who insisted that they saw them at the time.

[101688] 12.24. And they say also that a certain monk, most dear to God, persuaded by those who shared the desert with him, was sent to Byzantium to defend those settled nearest them, who were being oppressed and wronged beyond endurance; and that on arriving there he at once obtained admission to the emperor;

[101992] 12.25. but as he was about to come in to him, he set one of his feet upon the threshold there, and then suddenly drew back and went backward.

[102135] 12.26. The eunuch who was his usher, and those present there, earnestly entreated the man to go forward; but he, answering nothing, and like one struck with a stroke, departed thence to the lodging where he was staying; and when his attendants asked him why he did this, they say he declared outright that he saw the chief of the demons sitting on the throne in the Palace, with whom he would not deign to consort or to ask anything of him.

[102577] 12.27. And how was this man not likely to be some destroying demon, who never came to satiety of drink or food or sleep, but, tasting somehow of what was set before him, would go about the palace at unseasonable hours of the night, though he was demonically zealous for the pleasures of love?

[102871] 12.28. And some of Theodora's lovers say that, while she was on the stage, a demon, falling on them by night, drove them out of the chamber where they were spending the night with her. And there was a certain dancing-girl, Macedonia by name, of the Blues at Antioch, invested with much power.

[103165] 12.29. For, writing letters to Justinian while he was still administering the kingship under Justin, she would with no trouble destroy whomever she wished of the men of distinction in the East, and would have their money entered in the public registers.

[103420] 12.30. This Macedonia, they say, once when Theodora came from Egypt and Libya, was greeting her, and when she saw her exceedingly distressed and vexed at the way she had been outraged by Hecebolus and had lost her money on this journey, she greatly consoled and encouraged the woman, since Fortune was of such a kind as to become for her again the provider of great wealth.

[103795] 12.31. Then, they say, Theodora said that a dream too had visited her that night, bidding her take no thought for the sake of wealth.

[103930] 12.32. For when she should come to Byzantium, she would come to the bed of the chief of the demons, and would live with him as his wedded wife by every device, and from this she would become mistress of all money.

[104145] 13.1. But these things were thus held by most as matters of belief. As for Justinian, he was indeed such in the rest of his character as has been shown; yet he made himself accessible and gentle to those who met with him, and no one of all men was shut out from coming into his presence; rather, even with those who stood or spoke before him in no proper fashion he was never angry.

[104529] 13.2. Yet for all that he did not blush at any of those who were to be destroyed by him. Nor indeed did he ever show himself betraying anger or irritation toward those who had offended him, but with a gentle face, with brows let down, with a subdued voice, he would order tens of thousands of men who had done no wrong to be destroyed, cities to be razed, and all their money to be entered in the public registers.

[104945] 13.3. One might guess from this character that the man had the temper of a sheep. But if anyone, beseeching him, tried to win pardon for the victims with suppliant prayers, then, grown fierce and snarling, he seemed to be full of wrath, so that to none of those reputed to be his intimates was there any hope thereafter of begging off a man.

[105288] 13.4. He seemed to hold a firm belief concerning Christ, but even this to the destruction of his subjects. For he allowed the priests more fearlessly to use violence against their neighbors, and rejoiced with them when they plundered the property of those bordering on them, thinking in this way to be pious toward the divine.

[105616] 13.5. And in giving such judgments he thought he was doing holy work, if anyone, having seized under the name of sacred things something that did not belong to him, went off victorious. For he thought that justice lay in the priests prevailing over their adversaries.

[105885] 13.6. And he himself, acquiring from improper sources the properties of the living or the dead, and at once dedicating these to one of the churches, prided himself in the screen of piety, so that the possession of these things might not again revert

[106136] 13.7. to those who had been despoiled. Indeed, he wrought an indiscriminate number of murders for these reasons. For, being eager to bring all into one belief concerning Christ, he destroyed the rest of mankind for no reason, and that while doing it under the pretext of piety; for he did not consider it murder of men, if those who perished did not happen to be of his own belief.

[106519] 13.8. So zealously set upon the destruction of men was he forever, that he and his consort never ceased contriving causes that led to it.

[106658] 13.9. For both these persons had, for the most part, desires that were sisters; but where it befell them to differ in character, each was wicked, yet by displaying the most opposite traits they destroyed their subjects.

[106879] 13.10. For he was lighter in mind than dust, subject to those who at any time wished to lead him wherever they pleased, provided the matter did not tend to humanity or to forgoing gain, and most constantly admitting flattering words.

[107114] 13.11. For those who flattered him persuaded him with no trouble that he was lifted up on high and walked the air.

[107230] 13.12. And once, sitting beside him, Tribonian said that he was utterly in dread lest he should, through his piety, be taken up unawares into heaven. Such praises—or rather mockeries—he treated as established in his mind.

[107453] 13.13. But if he chanced to admire someone's virtue, a little later he would revile him as wicked. And having abused one of his subjects, he would again become his praiser in word, having changed for no reason.

[107665] 13.14. For the workings of his mind went contrary to what he himself said and wished to seem to be.

[107766] 13.15. How his character stood toward friendship and enmity I have already indicated, drawing my proof from what the man for the most part did.

[107911] 13.16. For as an enemy he was sure and unchanging, but toward his friends exceedingly unstable. So that, at any rate, he destroyed very many of those he had been zealous about, while he never became the friend of anyone he had once hated.

[108151] 13.17. And those whom he seemed to hold most as acquaintances and intimates, these, to gratify his consort or anyone else whatever, he would shortly betray to destruction, though he knew well that they would die solely for their goodwill toward him.

[108402] 13.18. For he was faithless in all things, except plainly in his inhumanity and avarice. From these no one was ever able to turn him away.

[108542] 13.19. But even in matters where his consort could not persuade him, by casting before him hopes of great money to come from the deed, she would drag her husband, unwilling though he was, to the action she desired.

[108758] 13.20. For the sake of unseemly gain he by no means disdained both to enact laws and again to abolish them.

[108867] 13.21. And he gave judgment not according to the laws which he himself had written, but according as the promise of money, appearing greater and more magnificent, led him.

[109040] 13.22. For he thought it brought him no shame to take away the properties of his subjects by petty thieving, whenever he could not take everything at once on some pretext, either by bringing an unexpected charge or under the pretense of a will that had not been made.

[109309] 13.23. And while he ruled the Romans, there remained no faith or sure belief toward God, no firm law, no settled transaction, no contract at all.

[109456] 13.24. And when his intimates were sent by him on some business, if it befell them to destroy many of those who fell in their way and to plunder a quantity of money, these were at once reputed and named by the sovereign as men of good report, as having carried out their instructions exactly; but if they came back to him having shown any mercy toward men, he was thereafter ill-disposed and hostile toward them.

[109870] 13.25. And despairing of them, as men of an old-fashioned nature, he would no longer call them to his service. So that many were eager to show him that they were wicked, even though their conduct was not in fact of that sort.

[110097] 13.26. Often, having promised something to people and made his promise more secure by oath or by writing, he would at once willfully fall into forgetfulness, thinking that this deed brought him a kind of glory.

[110309] 13.27. And these things Justinian did not only toward his subjects, but also toward many of the enemy, as I have said before.

[110436] 13.28. He was, for the most part, sleepless, and never came to satiety of food or drink, but, tasting almost with the tip of his finger, he would withdraw.

[110593] 13.29. For such things seemed to him a kind of incidental task forced upon him by nature, since he would often remain without food for two whole days and nights, especially when the time before the so-called feast of Easter was upon him.
